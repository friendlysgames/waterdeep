# Tyrant Archetypes

Tyrants are path-challenge bosses. The party must discover the correct, non-obvious way to fight them. Every archetype in this document is defined by three elements drawn from the Tyrant puzzle list:

- **Defining Trait** — The primary wall. What the party cannot do until they solve the puzzle.
- **Strength** — The urgency mechanism. What punishes the party for not solving the puzzle fast enough.
- **Weakness** — The payoff. What becomes trivially easy once the Defining Trait is resolved.

Archetypes are organized into three categories based on their Defining Trait:

- **Offensive** — The wall is something the boss *does to you*. The puzzle is about surviving or negating pressure.
- **Defensive** — The wall is something the boss *does to itself*. The puzzle is about finding a way in.
- **All-Rounder** — The wall combines offensive and defensive pressure simultaneously.

**Archetype names describe combat identity, not creature type or origin.** A Warlock may be a dragon that breathes from range and crumbles in melee. A Fortress may be a golem, a siege construct, or an armored warlord. A Hunter may be a spirit, a beast, or a trained assassin. The name is a shorthand for the puzzle the boss imposes — not a template for what kind of creature it is, what abilities it uses, or where its power comes from.

---

## Puzzle Type Reference

| Puzzle Type | Failure State |
|---|---|
| **Exposure** | I can't figure out how to attack it. |
| **Access** | I can't figure out how to hit it. |
| **Penetration** | I can't figure out how to damage it. |
| **Endurance** | I can't figure out how to avoid its attacks. |
| **Attrition** | I can't figure out how to stop taking massive damage. |

---

## Offensive Archetypes

Offensive archetypes impose an **Endurance or Attrition** Defining Trait. The primary wall is the boss's offensive output — the party must solve a pressure problem before they can win the damage race.

---

### The Hunter

| Element | Puzzle Type | Expression |
|---|---|---|
| Defining Trait | Exposure | The boss cannot be meaningfully engaged — it is too mobile, too elusive, or controls when and where contact happens. |
| Strength | Endurance | While the party struggles to pin it down, it is relentlessly pursuing them. It dictates the terms of every engagement. |
| Weakness | Access | Once the party forces it to fight on their terms, it is surprisingly easy to hit. Its evasion was its only real defense. |

**Design notes.** The Hunter's Exposure is active, not passive — it is not simply hiding. It is choosing the moment of contact, breaking off when threatened, and re-engaging from a position of advantage. The Strength creates urgency by ensuring the party cannot simply wait it out: the Hunter always finds them first. The Weakness is the payoff for disrupting this rhythm — once cornered or forced into a sustained engagement, it has no answer for sustained pressure.

The solution must be discoverable through play. Players should be able to observe that the Hunter always retreats when surrounded, always attacks isolated targets, or always approaches from a specific direction. The tell should be visible in the first two rounds.

**The key design risk** is making the Exposure feel unfair — like the boss can simply opt out of combat indefinitely. The Hunter must always be reachable in principle; the puzzle is about *how*, not *whether*.

**Examples from fiction:**

*Smaug (The Hobbit, J.R.R. Tolkien — literature).* Smaug controls every engagement entirely. He chooses when to emerge, attacks from the air where he cannot easily be reached, and withdraws to his mountain when threatened. Direct assault on his terms is suicidal. The solution — exposing his one unarmored scale, forcing him into a position where a single precise shot matters — is the Hunter's Weakness made literal: once the correct approach is found, the hit lands.

*The Xenomorph (Alien, 1979 — film).* The Xenomorph dictates every encounter. It vanishes into vents, strikes from concealment, and refuses sustained engagement. The party cannot fight it on equal terms — the puzzle is about controlling space, setting traps, and forcing it into a confrontation it cannot escape. Once cornered, it is dangerous but no longer evasive.

*The Leshen (The Witcher — video games).* The Leshen is inseparable from its forest — it controls terrain, relocates through trees, and attacks through roots and ravens rather than direct engagement. The puzzle is about severing its connection to the environment: destroying its totems, burning its marking trees, forcing it to fight as an individual rather than as an extension of the wilderness. Once that connection is broken, it is merely a creature, and creatures can be killed.

### The Juggernaut

| Element | Puzzle Type | Expression |
|---|---|---|
| Defining Trait | Attrition | The boss hits infrequently but catastrophically, and something keeps reversing the party's progress — regeneration, sustain, or a loop that negates net damage. |
| Strength | Penetration | While the attrition loop runs, the party's attacks seem ineffective. The wrong approach deals damage that disappears before it matters. |
| Weakness | Endurance | Once the sustain loop is broken, the Juggernaut's infrequent attacks become avoidable. Its threat was never its accuracy — it is easy to dodge when it is no longer recovering. |

**Design notes.** The Juggernaut hits hard but rarely. Its attack bonus is deliberately below curve — it is not trying to land every swing, only the ones that matter. The Attrition loop is what makes it feel hopeless: the party deals damage, but the Juggernaut recovers faster than they can net progress. The puzzle is identifying and breaking the sustain source.

Once the loop is broken, the Weakness reveals itself naturally. The same telegraphed, infrequent attacks that felt catastrophic when the Juggernaut was unkillable now feel manageable — the math has simply flipped. No additional mechanical change is required. The monster has not changed; the party's situation has.

**The key design risk** is the sustain source being too obscure. The regeneration, healing trigger, or loop mechanism must be visible and attributable within the first two rounds. Players should be able to form a hypothesis about what is keeping it alive before they confirm it.

**Examples from fiction:**

*The T-800/T-1000 (Terminator franchise — film).* The Terminator's defining quality is not how fast it attacks — it is that it keeps coming. Conventional damage slows it, but the threat persists. The puzzle is always about finding the thing that actually stops the loop: the hydraulic press, the molten steel, the magnetic field. Until that solution is found, the damage race is unwinnable.

*Alucard (Hellsing, Kouta Hirano — manga/anime).* Alucard's soul-absorption makes conventional combat completely futile — he regenerates from seemingly anything. The attrition loop is absolute. His weakness is the specific condition that ends the loop: the rules governing his power, the thing that actually sticks. Until the party identifies and addresses that condition, every point of damage is temporary.

*Calamity Ganon (The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild — video games).* Without the Divine Beasts weakening him, Calamity Ganon's malice regeneration makes conventional damage progress feel meaningless. The loop is environmental and structural: the puzzle is not how to hurt him but how to dismantle the system sustaining him. Once that system is addressed, the damage race resolves decisively.

### The Executioner

| Element | Puzzle Type | Expression |
|---|---|---|
| Defining Trait | Endurance | The boss attacks constantly with high accuracy, generating overwhelming volume. Individual hits are modest, but the cumulative pressure is unsustainable. |
| Strength | Access | While the party is absorbing the volume, they also struggle to fight back effectively. The Executioner makes it hard to act while it is acting constantly. |
| Weakness | Attrition | Once the party reduces how often the Executioner connects — through positioning, conditions, or disrupting its attack routine — the individual hits become trivial. The threat collapses entirely when the volume stops. |

**Design notes.** The Executioner is the mechanical mirror of the Juggernaut. Where the Juggernaut hits rarely and hard, the Executioner hits constantly and softly. Its attack bonus is at or above curve; its damage per hit is below. The puzzle is not about absorbing the hits — that math is unwinnable. It is about finding the approach that reduces hit frequency: imposing disadvantage, forcing difficult terrain, triggering a behavioral change, or disrupting the attack pattern itself.

The Weakness is structurally elegant: the party does not need to find a way to deal more damage. They need to find a way to get hit less. When they succeed, the encounter does not become merely manageable — it collapses. An Executioner that cannot land hits has effectively no threat.

**The key design risk** is the Endurance pressure feeling undifferentiated — like a boss that simply deals damage rather than one with a readable pattern the party can disrupt. The Executioner's attack routine should have visible structure so that players can identify what to interfere with.

**Examples from fiction:**

*Gyutaro (Demon Slayer, Koyoharu Gotouge — manga/anime).* Gyutaro's sickles generate constant pressure across multiple axes — he attacks continuously, poisons on hit, and makes sustained engagement deeply costly. The puzzle is not absorbing the hits but finding the moment to act within the pattern, and ultimately addressing the structural problem (simultaneous decapitation) that makes the encounter winnable.

*Agent Smith (The Matrix, 1999 — film).* Smith attacks relentlessly and with perfect accuracy — every engagement is a losing grind for anyone who tries to match his volume. The solution is never to out-fight him on those terms. Neo's answer is to change what kind of fight it is entirely: to become the thing Smith's attack volume cannot process. Reducing contact to zero is the Executioner's Weakness in practice.

*Carcer Dun (Night Watch, Terry Pratchett — literature).* Carcer is a street fighter whose defining quality is that he never stops. He exploits hesitation, attacks without warning, and sustains pressure that most opponents cannot absorb indefinitely. The solution is not matching his aggression — it is finding the moment and method that interrupts the pattern completely.

## Defensive Archetypes

Defensive archetypes impose an **Exposure, Access, or Penetration** Defining Trait. The primary wall is the boss's defensive configuration — the party must find a way in before damage becomes relevant.

---

### The Fortress

| Element | Puzzle Type | Expression |
|---|---|---|
| Defining Trait | Penetration | The boss has high AC, high HP, and significant resistances or immunities. Conventional damage makes no meaningful progress. |
| Strength | Access | While the party fails to crack the defenses, the Fortress also imposes a secondary threat — status effects, area denial, or a compounding debuff — that makes sustained failure costly. |
| Weakness | Endurance | Once the party penetrates the defenses, the Fortress's attacks are slow and telegraphed. They become easy to avoid. Its offensive threat was always secondary to its defensive one. |

**Design notes.** The Fortress is not primarily a damage dealer. Its slow, heavily telegraphed attacks are almost a red herring — they look like the danger, but they are the most avoidable thing about the encounter. The real pressure is the Strength: the secondary threat that ticks upward while the party exhausts resources against defenses they cannot crack.

The secondary threat must be clearly attributable to the Fortress itself from round one. Status effects, aura damage, or escalating debuffs that could be mistaken for environmental hazards undermine the puzzle's legibility. The party needs to understand that the clock is the Fortress — not something else in the room.

The Weakness is structurally satisfying: the thing that looked like a slow, unstoppable engine of destruction turns out to have completely readable attack patterns once you stop bouncing off its armor. Players feel smart for surviving long enough to reach that payoff.

**Examples from fiction:**

*Talos (Jason and the Argonauts, 1963 — film).* Talos is a bronze giant impervious to every conventional attack the Argonauts attempt. The secondary threat is simply his scale and persistence — he cannot be outrun indefinitely, and every moment spent failing to damage him is a moment closer to catastrophic contact. The solution is the plug in his ankle: a specific structural vulnerability that conventional assault would never find. Once opened, he collapses completely.

*Doomsday (DC Comics).* Doomsday's defining quality is adaptation — he cannot be killed the same way twice, which means conventional damage not only fails but actively teaches him to resist it. The secondary threat is escalating: the longer the fight goes wrong, the more resistant he becomes. The puzzle is finding the approach that addresses his adaptation loop before it becomes total.

*Boros (One Punch Man, ONE — manga/anime).* Before Saitama, Boros is effectively an unsolvable Fortress — his regeneration and accumulated power make conventional damage meaningless for anyone else. The secondary threat is not his attack speed but the despair of fighting something that simply does not go down. His individual attacks are massive but readable; his defense is the real problem.

### The Phantom

| Element | Puzzle Type | Expression |
|---|---|---|
| Defining Trait | Access | The boss can be located but attacks consistently fail to connect — high AC, reactive defenses, or a parry and deflection mechanic that intercepts attacks before they land. |
| Strength | Exposure | The Phantom controls the rhythm of engagement. It retreats when threatened, denying sustained contact, and re-engages on its own terms. |
| Weakness | Attrition | The Phantom's retreat phases give the party recovery windows. A party that uses those windows effectively — healing, repositioning, preparing countermeasures — will find that the Phantom's damage output cannot match their recovery rate once they solve the Access puzzle. |

**Design notes.** The distinction between the Phantom's Defining Trait and its Strength is a distinction of sequence, not kind. Exposure means the party cannot reach it. Access means the party can reach it but cannot connect. The Phantom presents Access as the wall — the party can see it, can target it, but hits simply do not land — while using Exposure as the urgency mechanism to prevent the party from standing still and methodically solving the problem.

The retreat is not a full reset. The Phantom should remain present and exploitable during its withdrawal phases — the party gets a window to recover and prepare, but the Phantom is still in the room. A retreat that completely resets the encounter removes the urgency and turns the fight into a waiting game.

The Weakness is the Phantom's own rhythm used against it. Every retreat is also a gift: time to heal, time to think, time to prepare the countermeasure. A party that recognizes this and banks those windows will find the encounter tips decisively in their favor once the Access wall falls.

**Examples from fiction:**

*Jack Sparrow (Pirates of the Caribbean — film).* Jack is almost impossible to hit cleanly — not through superhuman speed or power, but through unpredictability, misdirection, and a complete refusal to fight conventionally. He controls when and whether contact happens, retreating into chaos when threatened and re-engaging when the odds shift. When finally cornered, he is not especially hard to hit. The evasion was the entire defense.

*Kenshin Himura in Battōsai mode (Rurouni Kenshin, Nobuhiro Watsuki — manga/anime).* At his peak, Kenshin's speed and technique make it nearly impossible for opponents to land meaningful hits — attacks are read, sidestepped, or deflected before they connect. He controls the rhythm entirely: engaging and withdrawing on his own terms, never where an opponent expects. The recovery windows he grants are not generosity — they are inevitability, because he has already decided the fight is won.

*Zorro (created by Johnston McCulley — literature/film).* Zorro controls engagement through terrain and misdirection, retreating into shadow or crowd when threatened and re-emerging to strike from an unexpected angle. His cape, his acrobatics, and his refusal to stand and trade make him maddeningly difficult to pin down. Once cornered without his mobility tools, he fights conventionally — and the conventional fighter is considerably easier to hit than the legend.

### The Shield Master

| Element | Puzzle Type | Expression |
|---|---|---|
| Defining Trait | Access | The boss has a near-impenetrable guard — parries, blocks, or a defensive stance that intercepts attacks before they land. |
| Strength | Penetration | Attacks that do get through are turned against the attacker — counters, reflected damage, or punishing ripostes that make the wrong approach hurt the party more than the boss. |
| Weakness | Exposure | The Shield Master does not move. It holds its ground absolutely. Once its guard is broken, it has no answer for aggressive repositioning, flanking, or forced movement. Getting to it becomes trivial when it refuses to retreat. |

**Design notes.** The Shield Master is defined by what it will not do: it will not run. Its entire combat identity is positional dominance through defensive mastery. This makes its Weakness structurally inevitable — a combatant built entirely around standing its ground has no contingency for the moment that ground is compromised.

The Strength is what makes this archetype dangerous rather than merely frustrating. Attacks that fail to breach the guard do not simply miss — they are answered. The party that keeps swinging the wrong way is actively damaging itself. This is the urgency: not a clock ticking down, but a resource drain driven by the players' own choices.

The correct approach varies by monster implementation — it might be a specific damage type, a condition that interrupts the guard, attacking from a direction the guard cannot cover, or coordinated simultaneous strikes that overwhelm the intercept window. The tell must be present and readable from round one.

**Examples from fiction:**

*Obi-Wan Kenobi (Star Wars — film).* Obi-Wan's Form III lightsaber style (Soresu) is almost purely defensive — he intercepts attacks, exhausts opponents, and counters from a position of absolute guard. He does not press forward. He holds his ground and lets attackers break themselves against his defense. His weakness, as the archetype predicts, is an opponent who changes what they are asking his guard to answer.

*King Leonidas and the 300 (300, 2006 — film).* The Spartan phalanx formation is a Shield Master expressed at scale. The shield wall intercepts everything, the spears punish every attempt to break through, and the formation holds its ground by design — retreat is not a tactical option. The solution, as the Persians eventually find, is to find the angle the formation cannot cover: the goat path, the flanking route, the approach the guard was never built to answer.

*Whitebeard (One Piece, Eiichiro Oda — manga/anime).* Whitebeard's combination of Haki and physical dominance creates a guard that conventional attackers cannot breach. Attempts to attack him directly are deflected or punished. He does not retreat. He does not reposition. The puzzle is about finding the gap in that absolute defensive presence — and the Weakness is the same stubbornness that makes him formidable.

## All-Rounder Archetypes

All-Rounder archetypes combine an offensive and a defensive puzzle type across their Defining Trait and Strength. The party faces two interlocking walls simultaneously. Neither is fully solvable until both are addressed. These are the most demanding archetypes to design and to run.

**Design principle for All-Rounders:** the two puzzle elements must interact, not merely coexist. The Defining Trait should create conditions that make the Strength harder to address, and solving the Defining Trait should open access to the Weakness. If the two elements simply stack without informing each other, the encounter feels like two separate problems rather than one compound one.

---

### The Trickster

| Element | Puzzle Type | Expression |
|---|---|---|
| Defining Trait | Exposure | The boss cannot be meaningfully engaged because it exploits misdirection, deception, or illusion. Players cannot reliably locate or target the real threat. |
| Strength | Access | When players do manage to attack, they are likely attacking the wrong thing — decoys, false positions, or feints that intercept the attack without consequence. |
| Weakness | Attrition | The Trickster's tricks are finite. Its misdirection tools are resources — decoys that are destroyed, feints that have a tell once seen, deceptions that expire once understood. A party that reads the pattern will find the Trickster burning through its bag of tricks, and once those are exhausted, its damage output is modest. |

**Design notes.** The Trickster's puzzle must be solvable through observation, not through tool access. The correct approach should be discoverable from what happens during the fight: decoys that behave differently from the real target, feints with a consistent tell, false positions that cannot trigger certain effects. If the solution requires a specific ability that not every party has access to, the puzzle becomes a gear check rather than a cognitive challenge.

The Strength and Defining Trait are deeply linked here — Access follows directly from Exposure. The party cannot hit it because they cannot find it; when they do find something to target, it turns out not to be the real target. Both failures teach the same lesson from different angles.

The Weakness reveals itself through engagement rather than deduction. The party may not fully understand the trick until they have burned through several of its pieces.

**Examples from fiction:**

*Loki (Norse mythology / Marvel adaptations — mythology/film).* Loki across all his iterations is a pure Trickster. He controls what the party thinks they are fighting, who they think they are fighting, and where they think the real target is. His actual combat capability is often modest — the bag of tricks is the threat, not the combatant underneath it. Once the tricks run out or are seen through, the encounter resolves quickly.

*Mysterio (Spider-Man: Far From Home, 2019 — film).* Mysterio's entire combat identity is constructed illusion — the party is never fighting what they think they are fighting. The Exposure and Access problems are identical in his case: you cannot attack what you cannot correctly perceive. The solution is finding the mechanism behind the illusion (the drones, the projectors, the tells) rather than trying to fight the illusion directly.

*Arcade (Marvel Comics).* Arcade does not fight — he builds the fight around you. His Murderworld constructs eliminate the distinction between Exposure and Access entirely: the party cannot find the real threat because the real threat is the environment itself, and the environment is him. The solution is never to fight Murderworld on its own terms but to find and reach Arcade directly — at which point his actual combat capability is negligible. The bag of tricks is everything; the man inside it is nothing.

### The Warlock

| Element | Puzzle Type | Expression |
|---|---|---|
| Defining Trait | Attrition | The boss deals sustained ranged damage that bypasses or overwhelms conventional mitigation — through any vector that punishes the party for remaining at range or in the open. |
| Strength | Exposure | While absorbing the damage, the Warlock maintains distance and positioning that prevents easy engagement. Closing in requires solving a geography problem under pressure. |
| Weakness | Access | Once the party closes the distance and engages the Warlock directly, it is surprisingly easy to hit. Its defenses are built around preventing engagement, not surviving it. |

**Design notes.** The Warlock's two puzzle elements work in sequence: Exposure keeps the party at range, and Attrition punishes them for being at range. The correct response — closing the distance — is the answer to both problems simultaneously. A party that commits to aggressive engagement rather than trying to out-range or out-heal the damage loop will find that the Warlock collapses quickly once contact is made.

This means the Weakness must be genuine and immediate. A Warlock that is difficult to hit even after the party closes distance fails to reward the correct solution. The transition from "untouchable at range" to "surprisingly fragile up close" should be the encounter's dramatic turning point.

The Warlock's attrition vector is deliberately unspecified — it may be magical, chemical, mechanical, environmental, or psychological. What matters is that it punishes range and rewards closing in.

**The key design risk** is that the Exposure barrier is too high — if closing the distance requires resources or tools that drain the party significantly, reaching the Weakness may be a pyrrhic victory.

**Examples from fiction:**

*Yahaba (Demon Slayer, Koyoharu Gotouge — anime).* Yahaba never engages directly. His Koketsu Arrow demons redirect movement and compound in pressure, controlling the entire space between him and his opponent. The Attrition loop is spatial: every moment spent failing to reach him is another moment the arrows accumulate and disorient. The Exposure problem is genuine — getting to Yahaba at all requires solving the arrow system first. Once Tanjiro reaches him, the fight resolves on entirely different terms.

*Darkseid (DC Comics).* Darkseid's Omega Beams are relentless, nearly unavoidable, and track targets across any distance — the attrition loop is as close to inevitable as ranged pressure gets. He maintains distance through sheer dominance: engaging him at range is a losing proposition, and yet his cosmic stature makes closing that distance one of the great challenges in DC fiction. The few times opponents reach him directly, the nature of the confrontation shifts entirely — the Omega Beams become less relevant when the fight is inches away.

*Seath the Scaleless (Dark Souls — video games).* Seath bombards from range with crystal breath that deals sustained damage and inflicts curse buildup — a compounding attrition loop that makes prolonged exposure at distance fatal. He maintains the engagement on his terms through his positioning in a confined arena. The solution is closing distance to his tail or his body directly, at which point his attacks become far easier to avoid and his actual durability, while substantial, is workable. His Primordial Crystal is the specific sustain source the party must address to end the loop permanently.

### The Duelist

| Element | Puzzle Type | Expression |
|---|---|---|
| Defining Trait | Endurance | The boss reads and counters the party's attacks with precise, punishing responses. It does not simply hit hard — it hits *correctly*, responding to what the party does with disproportionate precision. |
| Strength | Access | While absorbing the counters, the party also finds it difficult to land their own attacks effectively. The Duelist's defensive technique and combat instinct make it hard to act without being answered. |
| Weakness | Penetration | Once the party finds the approach that breaks through the Duelist's read — the unexpected angle, the overwhelming force it cannot answer, the simultaneous strike it cannot fully counter — damage lands and sticks decisively. The Duelist has no sustained defense once its reactive system is overwhelmed. |

**Design notes.** The Duelist is defined by reactive precision rather than raw power. It does not hit the hardest in the room — it hits the most correctly. Every attack the party makes is an input that the Duelist processes and responds to. The puzzle is about finding the input it cannot process: a simultaneous attack from multiple angles, an approach that overwhelms its counter speed, an unconventional technique outside its experience.

The Access Strength flows naturally from this: a combatant that is constantly reading and responding to attacks is also extremely difficult to hit without telegraphing. The same quality that makes it dangerous to attack also makes it hard to connect.

The Weakness is structurally satisfying. The Duelist's precision is its only defense — it has no raw HP cushion, no damage absorption. Once the party finds the approach that bypasses the read, the encounter tips decisively and immediately.

**Examples from fiction:**

*Achilles (Greek mythology — mythology).* Achilles reads every opponent and responds with overwhelming precision — his combat style is reactive perfection. Every conventional attacker is processed and countered. The solution, as the myth makes explicit, is the approach his system cannot account for: the unconventional angle (Paris's arrow), the single point his precision cannot protect. Once found, it ends immediately.

*Dracule Mihawk (One Piece, Eiichiro Oda — manga/anime).* Mihawk is the canonical Duelist. His combat identity is absolute reactive precision — he deflects, counters, and reads everything put in front of him with minimal effort. His weakness, as the archetype predicts, is the opponent whose style simply does not fit his mental model: overwhelming speed he cannot track, or an approach so fundamentally strange that his read fails entirely.

*Malenia, Blade of Miquella (Elden Ring — video games).* Malenia punishes every attack with a precise, disproportionate counter — her Waterfowl Dance alone has ended more attempts to fight her conventionally than almost any other boss in the medium. What makes her a Duelist rather than a Fortress is that her defense is reactive, not passive: she reads the input and answers it. Her lifesteal on every hit she lands is the Strength made mechanical — the party cannot simply absorb her counters and grind. The solution is learning the specific windows her read cannot cover and committing to them completely.
