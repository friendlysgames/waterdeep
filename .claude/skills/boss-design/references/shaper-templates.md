# Shaper Templates

---

## The Environment Master

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the battlefield is the combatant. The boss is the reason the battlefield is fighting the party — but the party's immediate problem is always the space, not the creature standing in it. The puzzle is reading each new configuration fast enough to act before the next one arrives.**

---

### 1. High Concept

An Environment Master's high concept must communicate authored chaos — a boss whose threat is expressed through what it does to the space rather than what it does to the party directly. The Environment Master is not simply a creature that fights in a dangerous location. It is a creature that *makes* the location dangerous, actively and continuously, as its primary form of combat.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what does the battlefield look like from inside this encounter, and what is the logic governing how it changes?* The transformations should feel like expressions of a single underlying force — not random hazards but authored configurations, each with a preferred response, generated by a boss that is using the environment as its weapon. The logic is the identity.

**Strong examples:** "The Volcanic Sovereign in Whose Domain All Ground Burns," "The Tide-Caller Whose Arena Has No Stable Floor," "The Reality-Warper Who Builds the Room Around the Party," "The Possessed Temple That Is Itself the Threat."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Environmental Hazard Boss," "Moving Terrain Enemy," "Floor Is Lava Boss." These describe mechanics, not identity. The high concept should communicate *what kind of force the Environment Master embodies* — the fiction behind the terrain changes is what makes reading them feel like comprehension of something coherent rather than adaptation to noise.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Environment Master template asks a specific question before anything else: **what configurations does the terrain cycle through, what is the preferred response to each, and how does the party learn the pattern fast enough to use it?**

All three parts of this question must be answered before any stat block entry is written.

#### The Environment Master's Tools

**Terrain Generation Tools** — the specific mechanisms through which the environment transforms.
*(Examples: a bonus action that shifts one terrain zone from safe to hazardous; a trait that causes a new terrain configuration to automatically activate at the start of each of the Environment Master's turns; a reaction that reconfigures the terrain in response to the party's positioning, moving hazards toward occupied zones; a lair action triggered at fixed initiative counts that transforms the battlefield's structure. Every terrain generation tool must produce a change that has a clear preferred response — a direction to move, a surface to avoid, a zone to reach. Terrain that punishes all positions equally is not a puzzle, it is attrition.)*

**Personal Combat Tools** — what the Environment Master contributes as a direct combatant.
*(Examples: a primary strike that is modest in damage but always originates from an advantageous terrain position — the Environment Master is always standing on safe ground; a repositioning ability that moves the Environment Master in response to terrain changes it generates, keeping it in its preferred configuration; a defensive trait that makes the Environment Master harder to attack from hazardous terrain zones, rewarding the party for being in the right position before engaging. The Environment Master's personal combat should reflect that it is native to the terrain it generates — it does not struggle with the space the party is struggling with.)*

**Pattern Communication Tools** — what makes the terrain's logic readable within two rounds of engagement.
*(Examples: a consistent visual or narrative cue that announces each terrain change before it fully activates — the ground glows before the lava arrives, the water begins to churn before the flood, the walls vibrate before they close; a behavioral tell in the Environment Master's positioning — it always moves to a specific location before generating a specific configuration; a consistent sequence of configurations that repeats across the encounter, such that the party can predict what comes next after seeing it twice. The pattern must be communicable through the DM's narration. If a player cannot describe the rule they discovered, the pattern is not legible enough.)*

#### The Terrain Configurations

Each terrain configuration is a state the battlefield occupies for a defined window — typically one full round — before transitioning to the next. The Environment Master cycles through configurations in a pattern the party can decode.

Each configuration should have:

**A clear hazard profile.** What parts of the battlefield are dangerous in this configuration, and how dangerous are they. Lava spreading from the east wall. A flood rising to knee height, imposing difficult terrain everywhere except elevated platforms. Walls of force redirecting movement into a narrowing corridor. The hazard profile should be describable in one or two sentences and immediately interpretable from the DM's narration.

**A preferred response.** Where the party should be standing during this configuration to minimize exposure and maximize engagement opportunity. This is the configuration's "answer" — the spatial solution the party is trying to find fast enough to act on before the configuration transitions. The preferred response should be different for each configuration, creating genuinely different spatial demands across the cycle.

**A transition signal.** What the DM narrates at the end of one configuration to announce that the next is arriving. This signal is the Pace puzzle's primary mechanic — it is the information the party uses to anticipate rather than react. A reliable, narrated transition signal is what separates an Environment Master encounter from a random hazard generator.

**A connection to the pattern.** How this configuration relates to the others in the cycle. The Environment Master's terrain does not change randomly — it follows a logic. The connection to the pattern is what makes the transition signals learnable: the party sees the same signal precede the same configuration every time, and that consistency is what allows anticipation.

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Environment Master's action economy is built around **terrain generation as its primary offensive output and personal combat as a secondary threat** that punishes the party for focusing entirely on positioning rather than the boss itself.

#### Actions

**The Environment Master should have:**

- A **terrain shift action** that generates or advances the current terrain configuration — expanding a hazard zone, introducing a new obstacle, or triggering the next configuration in the cycle one step ahead of its normal timing. This action is the Environment Master's default and its primary damage output: the terrain does more collective damage than the boss's personal attacks. The DM should narrate this action specifically and in terms that give the party information: "The floor on the eastern side begins to glow — the lava is coming" is better than "The Environment Master uses Terrain Shift."

- A **primary strike** that is below curve in damage but always made from the safest terrain position available to the Environment Master. This attack communicates the boss's native relationship with the space: it is never standing in the wrong place, and the party fighting it from a hazardous position is at an additional disadvantage. The primary strike should impose a condition that interacts with the terrain — forced movement toward a hazard zone, a condition that makes navigating difficult terrain harder, or a debuff that reduces the party member's movement speed.

- A **Multiattack** that uses the terrain shift action and the primary strike once each. The Environment Master should never sacrifice a terrain shift for additional personal attacks — the terrain is the weapon, and the primary strike is what reminds the party not to ignore the boss entirely. When the terrain has reached its most complex configuration and the party is successfully managing it, the Environment Master shifts to using the primary strike twice, maintaining pressure as the positioning puzzle stabilizes.

> **Environment Master-specific note:** The terrain shift action should always generate a configuration with a readable preferred response, narrated at the moment of generation. An Environment Master that shifts terrain without communicating where the party should go is not imposing a puzzle — it is imposing damage. Every terrain shift is simultaneously a threat and a question. The party's job is to answer the question before the next one arrives.

#### Bonus Actions

The Environment Master's Bonus Actions are its **pattern acceleration tools** — what advances the terrain cycle faster than its normal pace and what gives the environment a sense of continuous, pressuring motion rather than discrete, manageable steps.

- A **cycle advance bonus action** that moves the current terrain configuration one step forward — transitioning to the next configuration in the pattern one round ahead of schedule. This ability fires when the party has successfully positioned for the current configuration and represents the Environment Master responding to their adaptation. The early transition means the party must immediately re-solve the new configuration rather than spending a full round operating from their achieved position. This ability should have a limited number of uses — the party should be able to rely on the normal cycle timing once the uses are exhausted.

- A **hazard intensification bonus action** that increases the damage or effect severity of the current configuration for one round — the lava deals more damage, the walls close further, the flood rises higher. This ability fires when the party is successfully operating from the preferred position for the current configuration, representing the Environment Master punishing correct positioning. The intensification does not change where the party should be standing — it just makes being there more urgent.

> **On the cycle advance bonus action:** This is the encounter's primary expression of the Pace Strength. A party that has decoded the pattern and is correctly anticipating each configuration will find the cycle advance firing on exactly the rounds they feel most confident — the Environment Master advancing the clock just as the party thinks they have a full round to breathe. This is not punitive; it is accurate. The Pace Strength is that the terrain changes arrive faster than the party can fully respond to, and the cycle advance is what makes that true mechanically.

#### Reactions

The Environment Master has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the Pace Strength is most directly expressed — moments where the party's positioning decisions generate immediate terrain responses that demand additional adaptation.

**Required:**

- **A terrain response reaction** *(trigger: a creature ends its turn in the optimal position for the current terrain configuration — the preferred response position).* The Environment Master immediately activates a minor localized hazard adjacent to that creature's space — a brief flame burst at the edge of the position, a surge of water threatening the perimeter — dealing modest damage on a failed Dexterity saving throw and imposing disadvantage on the creature's next attack roll. The creature is not displaced. The preferred position is still meaningfully better than any other position: party members in the wrong configuration take the full hazard damage and have no attack angles on the boss. Party members in the preferred position take minor conditional damage and can still act effectively. Correct positioning is the answer — it is just not free.

- **A configuration lock reaction** *(trigger: a creature attempts to move from one terrain zone to another during a configuration transition — the moment between one state and the next).* The Environment Master immediately completes the configuration transition, locking the new state into place before the creature's movement resolves. The party cannot exploit transition windows to move freely — the terrain changes are instantaneous when the Environment Master chooses to make them so. This reaction is what prevents the party from treating configuration transitions as movement opportunities rather than positioning challenges.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A pursuit configuration** *(trigger: a creature moves to a position more than 30 feet from the Environment Master).* The Environment Master immediately shifts the hazard zone to cover the area between it and the creature, creating difficult or dangerous terrain in the path of approach. The party cannot disengage and heal freely — the Environment Master can reach across the battlefield through terrain rather than through personal movement.
- **A pattern disruption** *(trigger: a creature correctly anticipates a terrain change and is already in the preferred response position before the transition signal fires).* The Environment Master immediately skips the anticipated configuration and jumps to the one after it in the cycle. Correct anticipation is punished by unpredictability — but only once. This reaction should have a single use, representing the Environment Master's one opportunity to break the party's pattern-recognition by breaking the pattern. After it fires, the cycle resumes normally.
- **A simultaneous shift** *(trigger: the Environment Master is reduced below half HP for the first time).* The Environment Master immediately activates two terrain configurations simultaneously — both the current configuration and the next one in the cycle, creating compound hazard zones that each have their own preferred response. For one round, the party must navigate two competing spatial demands at once. This reaction signals the phase transition and represents the Environment Master's desperation expressed as maximum terrain complexity.

> **On the terrain response reaction:** The gap between correct and incorrect positioning must be felt. A party member in the preferred position should experience a noticeably better round than one who is not — that gap is the reward for decoding the pattern. The reaction narrows the gap without closing it. Narrate the distinction explicitly: the party member in the preferred position takes a glancing hit from the perimeter hazard; the party member in the wrong position is engulfed in it. The difference in severity is the feedback that correct positioning is working.

### 4. The Terrain Cycle — Building the Pattern Problem

The terrain cycle is the encounter's puzzle engine. Before the Environment Master has a stat block, it needs a cycle. The cycle must be designed completely — all configurations, all preferred responses, all transition signals, the full pattern — before the encounter is run.

**The pattern must have:**

**A minimum of three distinct configurations.** Two configurations create a binary — safe and unsafe — that the party solves on round one and never revisits. Three configurations create a tripartite cycle with genuine spatial complexity: the party must track where they are in the cycle, what the current preferred position is, and what position they should be moving toward for the next configuration.

**A consistent visual language for each configuration.** Each configuration should have a distinct narration that the party can learn to associate with a specific preferred response. The same words should precede the same configuration every time. Consistency is what makes the pattern learnable: the party is not remembering arbitrary descriptions, they are building a map between narrated signals and spatial responses.

**A single governing rule.** Every configuration in the cycle should be explainable by the same underlying logic. "The hazard always travels clockwise." "The safe zone is always the elevated platform." "The floor is always safe in the quadrant opposite the boss's position." The rule does not need to be obvious — but it must be consistent, and a party that articulates it should find it predicts every future configuration correctly.

**A reward for anticipation that is visible and immediate.** When a party member is in the preferred position before a configuration activates — because they decoded the pattern and moved proactively — they should experience a noticeably better round than the party members who scrambled after the transition. Reduced damage from hazards, better attack angles on the boss, freedom from difficult terrain. The reward for being ahead of the pattern should be felt in the same round it is achieved.

---

### 5. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Environment Master's CR calibration follows the standard process with one Environment Master-specific adjustment: **the terrain's damage contribution is part of the effective DPR calculation, and CR benchmarks must account for both the boss's personal output and the terrain's aggregate damage across the encounter.**

The Environment Master typically deals below-curve personal damage — its primary output is the terrain. When calibrating against CR benchmarks, add the terrain's expected damage per round to the boss's personal DPR to get the true total.

**Steps:**

1. **Set AC at or below baseline, HP at baseline.** The Environment Master is hard to fight effectively because the party is always managing terrain, not because it is armored. Low to moderate AC expresses this — the boss is reachable and hittable, it is just always in a better position than the party. HP at baseline ensures the encounter ends at the right pace once the party cracks the pattern and can devote attention to the boss.

2. **Set personal strike damage below curve, terrain damage at or above curve.** The primary strike's per-hit damage should be noticeably below standard for its CR. The terrain's aggregate damage across a round — from hazard zones, forced movement into hazards, and the terrain response reaction — should bring the total to at or above the CR baseline. Calculate the terrain's expected contribution based on how many party members are likely to be in hazardous terrain each round during the early encounter, before the pattern is decoded.

3. **Set attack bonus at curve.** The Environment Master hits reliably when it attacks — the terrain is what challenges the party's positioning, not the boss's accuracy. A low attack bonus combined with a low damage die turns the boss's personal actions into irrelevant flavor rather than genuine secondary pressure.

4. **Assign ability scores based on the terrain generation mechanism.** The relevant ability score depends entirely on the Environment Master's nature: Intelligence for an arcanist shaping reality, Wisdom for a nature entity commanding elemental forces, Charisma for a bound spirit expressing divine will through its domain. Constitution should be moderate — the Environment Master is durable enough to sustain the terrain generation across the encounter's expected length but not so durable that the endgame phase drags.

5. **Design the terrain cycle's configuration count against the encounter's expected length.** A useful baseline: the full cycle should repeat two to three times across the encounter's expected duration. With a five-round encounter and a three-configuration cycle, the party sees each configuration two or three times — enough to learn it, enough to apply it, not so much that it becomes routine. Fewer repetitions and the pattern cannot be decoded. More and the encounter becomes monotonous.

6. **Calibrate the cycle advance bonus action's uses against the encounter's expected pacing.** Two to three uses is appropriate for most encounters. The cycle advance fires on rounds where the party has successfully positioned — which should be rounds two through four, once the party is getting comfortable with the pattern. Running out of cycle advance uses signals the encounter's final phase: the pattern is stable, the party is ahead of it, and the Environment Master is fighting with personal combat only.

---

### The Environment Master's Tell

Every Environment Master encounter must have one — for the governing rule of the terrain cycle. Write it in plain language before finalizing any stat block:

> *"The terrain in this encounter follows [specific governing rule — the hazard always travels in a consistent direction, the safe zone is always in a predictable relationship to the boss's position, the configurations always follow the same sequence]. The transition signal for each configuration is [specific narrated description — the ground begins to glow, the water starts to churn, the walls vibrate]. Players who [identify the governing rule and begin positioning proactively] will find themselves in the preferred position before each configuration activates — dealing full damage to the boss while the party members still reacting scramble from the hazards."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the terrain cycle has no governing rule — the configurations are disconnected, the pattern is unlearnable, and the encounter is a hazard generator rather than an Environment Master. Go back to the cycle design and connect every configuration to a single underlying logic. The rule does not have to be simple. It has to be consistent. Players should be able to articulate it by round three.

## The Accelerator

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the party understands what is happening. They cannot act on that understanding fast enough. The puzzle is not comprehension — it is compression. How do you reduce the number of decisions you need to make, or buy the space to make them, inside a system that is moving faster than your response time allows?**

---

### 1. High Concept

An Accelerator's high concept must communicate compounding tempo — a boss whose primary threat is not any individual attack, hazard, or change but the rate at which changes arrive. The Accelerator is not a creature that hits hardest or is hardest to hit. It is a creature that generates problems faster than the party can process and respond to them, and that gets faster.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what is the source of the acceleration, and what does it feel like from inside it?* The compounding should feel embodied — not just "lots of things happening" but a specific, characterful expression of escalating speed. A demon whose corrupting influence spreads to new hosts faster the longer it is not contained. A clockwork engine whose attack cycles shorten with every revolution. A hurricane spirit whose winds pull more debris into the air each round. The source of the tempo is the identity.

**Strong examples:** "The Whirlpool That Tightens With Every Tide," "The Engine Whose Cycle Never Stops Shortening," "She Who Acts Before Your Answer Is Finished," "The Cascade That Was Already Ahead of You."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Fast Boss," "Many Attacks," "Too Much Happening." These describe mechanical overwhelm, not identity. The high concept should communicate *why* the encounter accelerates — the compounding logic behind the tempo is what makes finding the pause meaningful rather than accidental.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Accelerator template asks two questions simultaneously: **what is the escalation source, and what creates the pause that breaks it?**

Both must be designed before any stat block entry is written. The escalation source is what the encounter is doing to the party. The pause is what the party can do to the encounter. Without the pause, the template describes an attrition race with no solution. Without the escalation source, it describes a fast encounter with no compounding logic.

#### The Accelerator's Tools

**Escalation Source Tools** — the specific mechanism through which the tempo compounds.
*(Examples: a trait that causes the Accelerator to take an additional bonus action at the start of each round after the first, up to a maximum of three; a hazard zone that expands by 10 feet in every direction at the start of each of the Accelerator's turns, shrinking the available safe space until the escalation is interrupted; a recurring terrain effect — falling debris, spreading fire, rising water — that covers an additional zone each round and cannot be extinguished by conventional means; a stacking condition applied to the entire battlefield that worsens by one tier each round, such as adding an additional effect on top of the existing one rather than replacing it — round one the floor crackles with electricity dealing damage on entry, round two it also sparks at the start of each creature's turn, round three it also arcs to an adjacent creature when triggered. The escalation source must be purely environmental — expressed through the battlefield and the Accelerator's own action economy, not through independent creatures taking their own turns. The party should feel the acceleration, not just observe it. Each round must be noticeably harder than the last in a way that is attributable to the compounding.)*

**Beneficial Change Tools** — the opportunities the Accelerator introduces that arrive and expire too quickly to exploit reliably.
*(Examples: a brief corridor of safe terrain that opens between two hazard zones and closes within one initiative count; a power-up that appears at a random location each round and provides a significant bonus to the creature that reaches it first but expires at the start of the next round; an environmental tool — a mechanism, a resource, a momentary vulnerability in the Accelerator — that is available for one round and unavailable for the next two. The beneficial changes are the Opportunity Strength's expression: the encounter is not simply punishing, it is offering tools the party cannot use fast enough. This creates a specific cognitive load — the party is tracking threats and opportunities simultaneously, and choosing what to ignore.)*

**Pause-Creation Tools** — the specific actions or conditions through which the party can force a momentary interruption in the escalation.
*(Examples: a specific damage threshold that, when reached in a single turn, disrupts the Accelerator's escalation mechanism for one round — the expanding hazard zone contracts, the stacking condition resets one tier; a specific condition — Stunned, Silenced, or Incapacitated — applied to the Accelerator that suppresses the escalation source for its duration; a specific interactable terrain feature on the battlefield — a valve, a seal, a focal point of the spreading effect — that when activated or destroyed halts the escalation for one round; a coordinated action requiring two or more party members to simultaneously occupy specific positions or interact with specific features, breaking the compounding loop. The pause must be achievable through the party's existing action economy under the encounter's tempo — not something that requires the entire party to stop fighting for multiple rounds.)*

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Accelerator's action economy is built around **escalation maintenance and the introduction of beneficial changes that expire before they can be fully exploited**, with personal combat as a secondary pressure preventing the party from dedicating their full attention to managing the tempo.

#### Actions

**The Accelerator should have:**

- An **escalation action** that advances the compounding mechanism by one step — the timer shortens, the additional bonus action activates, the battlefield condition worsens, or a new simultaneous change is introduced on top of existing ones. This is the Accelerator's defining turn structure: every round it fires, the encounter gets harder. The narration should always make the escalation visible — "the air grows thicker with debris, the intervals between shockwaves narrowing," "the engine's cycle completes again, the rhythm now twice what it was."

- A **beneficial change introduction action** that reshapes the battlefield in a way that creates simultaneous opportunity for both sides — a new hazard zone opens that the Accelerator can attack through freely while the party cannot, but a corridor or tool also becomes available that the party can seize if they act before it closes. The party gets a real opening; the boss gets new terrain advantage. Both are expiring. The party must decide whether to pursue their opportunity, contest the boss's, or ignore both — and that decision costs attention.

- A **primary strike** at or above curve in damage, used against whichever party member appears closest to finding the pause mechanism or has most recently exploited a beneficial change. Finding the pause and exploiting beneficial changes are both correct behaviors, and the Accelerator punishes both.

- A **Multiattack** that uses the escalation action and primary strike once each, or the beneficial change introduction and primary strike once each, alternating based on which is more tactically relevant. Escalation when the tempo is in danger of plateauing; beneficial change introduction when the party is ignoring opportunities and settling into a reactive rhythm. The alternation communicates that the Accelerator is managing the encounter's pressure, not simply escalating blindly.

> **Accelerator-specific note:** The Accelerator should never use its escalation action and beneficial change introduction in the same round unless a specific trigger forces it. Using both simultaneously collapses the encounter into undifferentiated chaos. The alternation rhythm gives the party moments where the tempo is slightly less overwhelming — and those moments are where the pause can be found.

#### Bonus Actions

The Accelerator's Bonus Actions are its **within-round tempo tools** — what makes the compounding feel continuous rather than turn-based, and what places beneficial changes in positions that test the party's prioritization.

- A **tempo expression bonus action** that activates one additional change within the current round — a hazard shifts, a beneficial change expires one initiative count early, an additional threat appears in a location the party thought was safe. Fires on rounds where the party is successfully managing the current state — the Accelerator responding to competent play by shrinking the margin.

- A **misdirection bonus action** that places a beneficial change in a location that requires moving through an active hazard to reach. The beneficial change is real and valuable, but accessing it has a cost. The party must decide whether the tool is worth the hazard transit — and quickly, because the offer is about to expire. Fires on rounds where the party is ignoring all beneficial changes, pressuring them to engage with the encounter's opportunity structure rather than treating it as pure attrition.

> **On these two bonus actions:** They should never fire on the same round. Tempo expression is for rounds where the party is managing well — it shrinks the margin. Misdirection is for rounds where the party is ignoring opportunities — it creates a more compelling offer. Running both simultaneously crosses from Pace pressure into noise.

#### Reactions

The Accelerator has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**.

**Required:**

- **A compounding reaction** *(trigger: a creature successfully uses a beneficial change — reaches a resource, passes through a safe corridor, uses an environmental tool).* The Accelerator immediately advances the escalation by one additional step. Exploiting beneficial changes is correct — the encounter is offering those tools to help manage the tempo. But it is not free. Every successful beneficial change use increases the pressure. The party is gaining resources while the encounter gets harder: they should always be ahead on resources and behind on tempo, until the pause resolves both.

- **A pause resistance reaction** *(trigger: a creature attempts the action or ability that constitutes the pause mechanism).* The Accelerator immediately adds one additional active change to the current round and imposes disadvantage on the pause attempt. The pause is real and achievable, but the first attempt will fail. The party must commit across multiple turns — accepting disadvantage and additional changes — to force the interruption. This reaction communicates that the pause is the correct answer while ensuring it requires sustained commitment: *yes, and keep going.*

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **An opportunity collapse** *(trigger: a creature ends its turn adjacent to an unexploited beneficial change without having used it).* The beneficial change immediately expires and is replaced by a hazard at the same location. The party cannot position near beneficial changes and use them at their leisure — the encounter notices hesitation and converts opportunity into threat.
- **A cascade trigger** *(trigger: two or more changes resolve in the same initiative count).* The Accelerator immediately introduces one additional simultaneous active change. Simultaneous resolution accelerates faster than sequential resolution. This rewards parties who find ways to stagger their engagements with active changes, making controlled resolution a survival strategy.
- **A false pause** *(trigger: the pause mechanism is successfully activated for the first time).* The escalation appears to stop for one round — then resumes at twice its previous rate at the start of the following round. The first successful pause is a trap: the party breathes and repositions, only to find the encounter coming back faster. The second successful pause is real. This option works best for Accelerators with a deceptive, mocking identity.

---

### 4. The Pace and the Pause — Building the Tempo Problem

The Accelerator encounter is built around a single design commitment: the escalation must be interruptible, and the interruption must be achievable under the encounter's own tempo. An Accelerator that cannot be paused is a TPK machine. An Accelerator that can be paused in round one with a cantrip has no Pace Strength at all.

**The tempo problem must have:**

**An escalation that the party can feel compounding.** Each round must be observably harder than the last — not just statistically harder but experientially harder. The party should be making different decisions in round three than in round one because the situation has genuinely changed, not because their resources have depleted.

**A pause mechanism that requires the party to read the encounter first.** The pause is not a universal solution — it requires something specific, and that specific thing must be discoverable by watching the encounter's behavior. The pause mechanism should be narrated consistently across the encounter so the party can identify it: every time the escalation advances, the same mechanism is the source.

**Beneficial changes that genuinely help when used.** Each beneficial change should represent a genuinely significant advantage if seized — meaningful HP restoration, a damage bonus of roughly 50% of the primary strike's value for one round, or a momentary suppression of one active hazard. Below these thresholds, beneficial changes are decorative. The party that uses them well should feel the difference.

**A resolution that is recognizably stable.** When the pause is achieved, the encounter must change shape in a way the party can feel. The changes that were arriving faster than decisions could be made now arrive at a pace that allows deliberate response. The encounter does not become easy — it becomes fair. Against an Accelerator, fair feels like a profound relief.

---

### 5. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Accelerator's CR calibration follows the standard process with one Accelerator-specific adjustment: **effective difficulty varies significantly between pre-pause and post-pause states, and CR benchmarks must account for both.**

Pre-pause, the encounter is substantially harder than the Accelerator's personal stat block suggests — the compounding changes and denied opportunities represent a difficulty multiplier that exceeds raw numbers. Post-pause, it is substantially easier — the terrain resolves into a readable space the party can navigate with their accumulated knowledge. Calibrate against the mid-encounter state.

**Steps:**

1. **Set AC at baseline, HP at or slightly above baseline.** The Accelerator is not hard to hit — it is hard to deal with in aggregate. Moderate AC expresses this: the boss is reachable, the problem is finding time to reach it. Slightly above baseline HP reflects that the post-pause phase should still require meaningful effort.

2. **Set the primary strike at or above curve.** If the personal strike is weak, the party can ignore the boss entirely and focus on managing the tempo — collapsing the encounter into a pure environmental challenge. The primary strike must make personal engagement a real pressure during the pre-pause phase.

3. **Set the escalation source's severity against the encounter's expected length.** The escalation should reach its most intense state in rounds four or five. Peaking in round two overwhelms the party before they can find the pause. Peaking in round eight means the first six rounds are extended setup.

4. **Design beneficial changes to provide real value when seized.** The opportunity worth pursuing should restore meaningful HP, provide a significant damage bonus for one round, or suppress one active hazard. Below these thresholds, beneficial changes are flavor. The party should feel the difference between rounds where they seized one and rounds where they did not.

5. **Calibrate the pause mechanism's resistance against the party's action economy.** The pause should require two to three focused attempts across multiple rounds — each absorbing the pause resistance reaction's disadvantage and additional changes. That investment should pay off reliably on the third attempt, or the mechanism feels arbitrary.

6. **Design the beneficial change expiration rate against the party's movement speed.** A change expiring at the start of the following round is reachable in one move action. A change expiring at the end of the current initiative count is reachable only for party members who have not yet acted. Vary expiration rates across different beneficial changes to create the layered prioritization problem that defines the Opportunity Strength.

---

### The Accelerator's Tell

Every Accelerator encounter must have two — one for the escalation source, one for the pause:

> *"This Accelerator's escalation advances when [specific observable trigger]. The party can identify it by [specific observable tell — the same narrated description precedes each escalation step, the source is always in the same location, the escalation always produces the same additional change type]. The escalation will reach full intensity by round [Z]."*

> *"The pause mechanism is [specific action or condition]. It requires [specific number] successful uses across [specific number of turns] to interrupt the escalation. Each attempt draws the pause resistance reaction. Players who [commit to the pause mechanism across multiple turns while absorbing the resistance] will find the encounter stabilizing — and the knowledge they accumulated while being overwhelmed will be directly applicable to the post-pause fight."*

If you cannot complete both sentences, either the escalation has no observable source or the pause has no concrete mechanism. Both failures produce the same result: the party cannot meaningfully engage with the encounter's puzzle, and the Accelerator becomes a damage race with a clock rather than a solvable problem. The escalation must be visible. The pause must be achievable. The party who finds both should feel, in the post-pause phase, that everything they learned while being overwhelmed is now exactly the knowledge they need.

## The Transformer

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the boss is not fighting the party in the arena — it is building the arena as it fights. The terrain is not a hazard the party navigates around; it is the boss's primary weapon, constructed in real time and aimed at whoever is in the wrong position. The puzzle is reading the construction logic before the battlefield becomes something the party cannot stand in.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A Transformer's high concept must communicate active, authored terrain generation — a boss whose attacks are not primarily directed at the party's bodies but at the space the party occupies. The Transformer is not a creature that fights while a dangerous environment exists. It is a creature that *makes* the environment dangerous as its fundamental combat expression.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what is the Transformer building, and why is the shape of what it builds a strategy?* The terrain generation should feel purposeful and directed — the Transformer is not producing random hazards, it is constructing a configuration that funnels the party toward a specific bad position, cuts off a specific escape route, or collapses the safe space toward a specific point of the boss's choosing. The shape of the construction is the attack.

**Strong examples:** "The Cinder-Weaver Who Builds the Floor She Fights On," "The Geomancer Whose Strategy Is the Room Itself," "The Architect of Closing Walls," "The Sculptor of Inevitably Bad Ground."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Generates Hazards," "Controls Terrain," "Spreading Fire Boss." These describe the output, not the identity. The high concept should communicate *why* the Transformer builds what it builds — the tactical logic behind the terrain generation is what makes reading it feel like understanding an opponent rather than surviving an environment.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Transformer template asks a specific question before anything else: **what logic governs where hazards are placed, and what does following that logic tell the party about where to stand next?**

The answer to this question is the encounter's puzzle. If the answer is "the hazards appear randomly," the encounter is not a Transformer — it is an environmental attrition challenge. The logic must be specific, consistent, and discoverable through observation.

#### The Transformer's Tools

**Terrain Generation Tools** — the specific mechanisms through which the boss builds its hazard configuration.
*(Examples: a bonus action that places a hazard zone in the space the Transformer just vacated — the boss always leaves danger where it stood; an action that extends an existing hazard zone by 10 feet in a consistent direction — the fire always spreads toward the party's current concentration; a trait that generates a new hazard zone at the target of each attack that misses — failed strikes become terrain, not wasted actions; a reaction that fills the space between the Transformer and a creature that just moved away from it with hazardous terrain. Every tool should express the same underlying construction logic: the Transformer is building toward something specific.)*

**Construction Logic Tools** — what gives the hazard generation a coherent tactical shape rather than random placement.
*(Examples: a behavioral pattern in where the Transformer positions itself — it always moves to the edge of its most recently generated hazard, meaning the hazard's shape predicts the boss's next position; a consistent relationship between attack direction and hazard direction — it always generates terrain to the left of its attack vector; a funneling logic that places hazards at the perimeter first and works inward, predictably collapsing the safe space from the outside in. The construction logic must be consistent enough that a party member who watches for two rounds can articulate a hypothesis about where the next hazard will appear.)*

**Personal Combat Tools** — what the Transformer contributes as a direct combatant, expressed primarily through the terrain it has already constructed.
*(Examples: a primary strike that is stronger when made from within a hazard zone the Transformer generated — it fights better on its own ground; a repositioning ability that moves the Transformer along the edge of its existing hazard zones, always staying adjacent to its own construction; a defensive trait that treats hazard zones the Transformer generated as cover rather than obstacle, making the boss harder to target from outside its own terrain. The Transformer's personal combat should reflect its native relationship with the terrain it creates: it is always in the right place, because the right place is where it put itself.)*

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Transformer's action economy is built around **terrain generation as its primary offensive expression and personal positioning as its secondary**, with the combat's danger arising from the shape of the construction rather than the boss's direct attack output.

#### Actions

**The Transformer should have:**

- A **terrain generation action** that places one or more hazard zones on the battlefield according to the construction logic. This is the Transformer's primary offensive action — not because the hazard necessarily deals damage but because it advances the strategic configuration the boss is building toward. A hazard zone is any feature the Transformer generates that changes what the party can do in a space — fire that damages those who enter it, walls that block movement and line of sight, fog that imposes disadvantage on attacks, ice that makes the ground difficult terrain, pillars that provide the Transformer with cover. The narration should always communicate both what was placed and where in the larger pattern it sits: "the stone wall rises across the northern corridor, cutting off the exit you were watching" tells the party more than "a wall appears."

- A **primary strike** that is below curve in direct damage but carries a positioning effect — forced movement into a hazard zone, a push toward a position the construction logic has made dangerous, or a pull toward a zone the Transformer has just generated. The primary strike should feel like a piece of the terrain strategy rather than a separate attack: the boss hits the party toward the thing it built, not simply at them.

- A **Multiattack** that uses the terrain generation action and the primary strike once each. The order matters: terrain generation fires first, then the primary strike positions the party relative to the new terrain. The party sees the hazard appear and then feels themselves moved toward it — the construction and the weaponization happen in the same turn, in the right sequence. When the Transformer's construction is largely complete and no new generation is needed, it shifts to using the primary strike twice, now exploiting the terrain it has already built rather than adding to it.

> **Transformer-specific note:** The primary strike should always move the target toward the Transformer's most recently generated hazard. This is what makes the terrain generation and the personal combat feel like a unified strategy rather than two separate mechanics happening simultaneously. The party should feel the attack and the terrain working together — the boss builds the trap, then closes it.

#### Bonus Actions

The Transformer's Bonus Actions are its **construction acceleration tools** — what allows the terrain to develop faster than turn-by-turn action allows, and what creates the mid-round moments where the battlefield changes shape between a player's action and their movement.

- A **rapid generation bonus action** that places a smaller or more targeted hazard zone than the main terrain generation action — a single square rather than a zone, a specific chokepoint rather than a broad area, a precise position that cuts off one specific route. This ability fires after the primary strike, completing the turn's construction: the main action built the zone, the bonus action closed the gap. The rapid generation always targets the position the party was most likely to use as an escape route from the main hazard, communicating that the Transformer has anticipated their response.

- A **construction reinforcement bonus action** that extends or deepens an existing hazard zone rather than creating a new one — the existing fire grows hotter, the existing wall grows taller and blocks ranged attacks as well as movement, the existing fog thickens to impose the Blinded condition on those within it, the existing obstacle expands to block an adjacent square. This ability fires on rounds when the Transformer has already generated its primary terrain and is now investing in the durability of what it has built. Reinforcement tells the party that this part of the construction is permanent — it will not expire on its own, and it will not be easy to clear.

> **On the rapid generation bonus action:** This ability is the Transformer's most important tool for making the construction feel active rather than additive. Without it, the terrain grows by one zone per round — predictable and easy to stay ahead of. With it, the terrain grows by one zone and one targeted closure per round, which means the party is always managing both the main expansion and the specific chokepoint the Transformer has identified as their most likely response. The bonus action is the Transformer thinking two moves ahead.

#### Reactions

The Transformer has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the construction logic is most directly weaponized against the party's movement decisions — the moments where a creature's choice to move triggers more terrain in the way.

**Required:**

- **A construction response reaction** *(trigger: a creature moves out of a hazard zone the Transformer generated).* The Transformer immediately extends that hazard zone by 10 feet in the direction the creature moved, or places a new hazard zone of any type in the creature's destination square. Moving out of the terrain does not escape it — the construction follows. This reaction is the Pattern Strength made mechanical: the party cannot simply step out of each hazard as it appears, because stepping out generates the next one. The only escape is anticipating where the construction will not go, which requires reading the logic.

- **A chokepoint reaction** *(trigger: a creature moves through the only unobstructed path between two sections of the battlefield).* The Transformer immediately places a hazard zone of any type in that path — a wall that blocks the route, a fire that makes passage costly, a fog cloud that removes line of sight through it. The party cannot freely use the seams in the existing construction — actively navigating the open corridors draws the Transformer's attention and begins closing them. This teaches the party that finding the seam is not enough; they must use it decisively, not repeatedly.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A pattern assertion** *(trigger: a creature moves to a position that would be safe after the Transformer's next terrain generation fires according to its construction logic — they have correctly anticipated the pattern).* The Transformer immediately generates its terrain in that creature's current position instead of where the construction logic would normally place it, and places the originally-intended hazard where the logic dictates. The party member who read the pattern has drawn the boss's attention, and the boss has adjusted. This option works best for Transformers with a reactive, intelligent high concept. It should have limited uses — the Transformer can deviate from its own logic only so many times before its construction becomes incoherent.
- **A punishing crossing** *(trigger: a creature moves through or enters a hazard zone the Transformer generated).* The Transformer immediately places a second, smaller hazard zone of a different type at the creature's entry point into the first — layering the construction. Entering a hazard willingly draws deeper investment from the Transformer: the creature who crossed the fire now finds a wall rising behind them. This option rewards parties who avoid hazard zones entirely and pressures those who move through them as a shortcut.
- **A repositioning reaction** *(trigger: a creature uses the Dash action or moves more than their normal speed in a single turn).* The Transformer immediately places a hazard zone of any type in the space the creature was occupying at the start of their movement. Rapid movement across the construction is noticed and responded to — the Transformer marks where the creature was and makes that position part of the construction. A party that moves quickly through the battlefield will find the construction filling in their wake.

> **On the construction response reaction:** This is the Transformer's most important reaction for communicating the Pattern Strength. Every time it fires, the narration must make the logic explicit: "as you step back from the flames, stone rises to fill the space you moved into — the construction is always one step behind you, which means it will be one step ahead if you keep moving in the same direction." The party member who triggered this reaction has been given the most direct possible hint about the construction's pattern: it reacts to movement. A party that internalizes this will stop moving reactively and start moving predictively.

---

### 4. The Construction Logic — Building the Pattern Problem

The construction logic is the Transformer encounter's puzzle engine. Before any stat block entry is written, the DM must be able to state the construction logic in a single sentence and verify that every terrain generation action, bonus action, and reaction in the encounter is consistent with it.

**The construction logic must have:**

**A single governing rule.** Every hazard the Transformer generates must be placeable by the same rule. "Hazards always appear in the space the Transformer just left." "Hazards always extend toward the highest concentration of party members." "Hazards always appear at the target of any missed attack." The rule should be simple enough to state in one sentence and specific enough to generate concrete placement decisions in every round. A rule that requires judgment calls to apply is not consistent enough.

**A direction.** The construction should be building toward something — a specific position the party is being pushed into, a specific corridor that is being closed, a specific area being cleared for the Transformer's exclusive use. The direction gives the pattern a shape: the party can see not just where the last hazard was placed but where the construction is going. Anticipating the direction is what allows the party to act before the terrain arrives rather than after.

**A visible seam.** Every consistent construction logic has a position or direction it cannot reach — a quadrant the rule never generates in, a position that the funneling logic always creates safe passage toward, a direction the extension always moves away from. The seam is where the party can stand without being followed. Finding it is the encounter's Opportunity Weakness made concrete: the party that reads the pattern identifies the one place the construction has not and will not go.

**An accelerating density.** Early in the encounter, hazard zones are sparse and the party has room to maneuver. Late in the encounter, hazard zones are dense and movement options are constrained. The construction should feel like it is closing in — not because individual hazards expand arbitrarily, but because new ones fill the remaining space each round. This density arc is the encounter's pacing mechanism: the early rounds are about understanding the pattern, the middle rounds are about applying it under increasing pressure, the late rounds are about executing in a nearly-constructed battlefield.

---

### 5. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Transformer's CR calibration follows the standard process with one Transformer-specific adjustment: **the terrain's damage contribution and movement restriction are both part of the effective challenge, and CR benchmarks must account for the battlefield's constructed state rather than its opening state.**

A Transformer in round one occupies a sparse battlefield with minimal hazard coverage. A Transformer in round four occupies a dense battlefield with most routes restricted. Calibrating against round one produces an encounter that feels weak early and overwhelming late. Calibrating against round four produces an encounter that opens at full difficulty. Calibrate against round three — partial construction, meaningful pressure, but still navigable.

**Steps:**

1. **Set AC at baseline, HP at or slightly below baseline.** The Transformer is not hard to hit — it is hard to reach on viable terms. Moderate AC expresses this: the boss is available and targetable, the problem is getting to it through the construction. Below-baseline HP reflects that the Opportunity Weakness — once the pattern is decoded — should resolve the fight efficiently.

2. **Set the primary strike below curve in direct damage, with a strong positioning effect.** The Transformer's attack damage is secondary to its terrain damage. A below-curve primary strike that reliably moves the target into a hazard zone will deal more effective damage across the encounter than an at-curve primary strike with no positioning. Calculate expected DPR by adding the primary strike's direct damage to the expected hazard damage from the forced movement.

3. **Set the attack bonus at curve.** The Transformer needs to reliably connect its positioning effects. A below-curve attack bonus means the forced movement rarely triggers, disconnecting the construction from the personal combat. The attack bonus is what makes the terrain weapon coherent.

4. **Design hazard zones around their strategic function, not their damage output.** A hazard zone is any terrain feature that changes what the party can do — damage, difficult terrain, line-of-sight blocking, forced disadvantage, or isolation. Damage-dealing hazards should deal modest amounts; their primary value is that creatures avoid them, which constrains movement and positions. Non-damage hazards — walls, fog, difficult terrain — have no DPR contribution but carry significant effective challenge through movement restriction and positioning. When calculating total DPR against CR benchmarks, add the primary strike's direct damage to the expected damage from damage-dealing hazard zones only, and account separately for the action economy cost imposed by non-damage hazard zones.

5. **Design the construction logic's seam to be discoverable in rounds two through three.** The seam — the position the pattern cannot reach — should not be immediately obvious from the encounter's opening state. The party needs to see two or three placements to identify the pattern before they can locate the seam. If the seam is obvious from round one, the encounter resolves immediately. If it requires six rounds to find, the battlefield will be fully constructed before they discover it.

6. **Calibrate the construction's density arc against the encounter's expected length.** At the start of round one, hazard coverage should be zero. By round three, roughly half the battlefield should have at least one hazard zone. By round five, three-quarters of the battlefield should be covered, with the seam as the remaining safe corridor. An encounter that reaches full construction in round two has no pattern-discovery phase. An encounter that reaches full construction in round eight has no meaningful construction pressure.

---

### The Transformer's Tell

Every Transformer encounter must have one — for the construction logic and the seam it creates. Write it in plain language before finalizing any stat block:

> *"This Transformer's construction follows [specific governing rule — where hazards are placed relative to the boss's position, attack targets, or party movement]. The direction of construction is [specific spatial direction — toward the party's concentration, toward a specific corner, away from a specific wall]. The seam — the position the construction cannot reach — is [specific location or direction that the rule never generates in]. Players who [observe two or three placements and articulate the rule] will find they can [position in the seam and attack from a known-safe position], turning the Transformer's own construction into the guide for where to stand."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the construction has no governing rule and no seam — the hazards are placed at the DM's discretion rather than a consistent logic, and the encounter cannot be solved through observation. The Transformer is the one Shaper archetype where the DM's own preparation is the puzzle's foundation: if the rule is not designed in advance, it cannot be communicated consistently enough to be decoded. Write the rule first. Build the encounter around it.

## The Form Shifter

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **what worked last round does not work this round, and the party has one turn to read the new configuration before they pay for attacking the wrong way. The puzzle is not what to do — it is what to do *right now*.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A Form Shifter's high concept must communicate instability as identity — the sense of a creature that is not one thing but many, cycling through configurations that each represent a complete and coherent version of itself. The Form Shifter is not a boss that changes tactics. It is a boss whose *nature* rotates, and the party must track that rotation to remain viable.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what is the logic behind the forms?* The forms should feel like expressions of a single underlying identity, not a random assortment of modes. An elemental being cycling through fire, ice, and lightning is expressing the full spectrum of its elemental nature. A shapeshifting predator cycling through forms adapted to different prey is expressing a hunting logic. A dying god cycling through aspects of its divine portfolio is expressing theological breadth. The rotation should feel coherent, even if it is not immediately predictable.

**Strong examples:** "The Prismatic Serpent of the Ruined Spire," "She Who Wears All Faces," "The Unbound Elemental in Its Hunger," "The Last Metamorph of the Shaper Cults."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Shape-Changing Boss," "Immune-Rotating Enemy," "Phase Boss." These describe statistics, not identity. The high concept should communicate *what the forms mean* — the fiction behind the rotation is what makes identifying each form feel like learning something, not just reading a buff tracker.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Form Shifter template asks a specific question before anything else: **what are the forms, what does each form do, and what is the signal that tells the party which form is active?**

Organize your tools into three categories before writing a single stat block entry:

**Form Tools** — the two or three configurations the Form Shifter rotates through.
Limit active configurations to two or three. More than three configurations create a tracking problem that exceeds most tables' working memory, especially in a live combat encounter. Each form should have:
- A **defensive profile** — what damage types, conditions, or approaches it resists, is immune to, or is vulnerable to in this configuration.
- An **offensive profile** — what kind of attack or pressure it generates while in this form. The offense should be thematically coherent with the defense: a form that is immune to fire should deal fire damage; a form that is immune to psychic effects should impose psychic conditions.
- A **residue** — what it leaves behind on the battlefield when it shifts out of this form. This is the Terrain Strength made concrete. Ice patches that persist when the ice form rotates out. Scorched zones from the fire form. A lingering aura from the lightning form. The residue accumulates, and the battlefield becomes increasingly complex with each full cycle.

**Signal Tools** — what tells the party which form is currently active, before they have wasted a turn on the wrong approach.
The signal must be clear, narrated, and present at the moment of transition — not a mechanic the party discovers by failing. Consider:
- A physical transformation the DM describes: the creature's scales shift color, its breath changes, its silhouette alters.
- A change in the creature's behavior: it begins circling rather than advancing, or it becomes still rather than mobile.
- An environmental cue tied to the residue: the temperature drops noticeably as the ice form becomes active, the air smells of ozone before the lightning form.
- A verbal or auditory cue: the creature's voice changes register, it speaks a word in an unknown language, its breathing pattern shifts.

The signal serves as the clock the party is racing. When they see or hear the signal, they have one full round in the new form before it may shift again. Players who learn to read the signal can begin repositioning and selecting tools *before* the new form's offense lands.

**Transition Tools** — what happens mechanically at the moment of each shift.
The transition itself should have a cost for the party, but also a brief opportunity:
- The Form Shifter cannot make attacks on the turn it shifts — it is between configurations and briefly exposed. This is the Opportunity Weakness made visible: every shift is a moment the party can exploit, and a party that tracks the cycle will be ready for it.
- The transition may deal incidental damage or impose a condition as a reflexive release of the old form's energy — fire form releasing a burst of heat as it dissipates, ice form releasing a brief cold snap.
- The transition refreshes one of the Form Shifter's reaction slots, representing the new form's defenses coming online.

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Form Shifter's action economy is built around **form-coherent offense that punishes attacking the wrong way, and brief transition windows that reward tracking the cycle.**

#### Actions

**The Form Shifter should have, within each form:**

- A **primary strike** that is thematically coherent with the current form and deals at-curve damage of the form's associated damage type. The primary strike imposes a condition or secondary effect that extends the form's threat beyond the attack itself — an ice form's primary strike leaves the target with reduced speed; a fire form's primary strike applies ongoing fire damage; a lightning form's primary strike imposes disadvantage on the target's next saving throw. The strike says: *this is what I am right now, and you are inside its logic.*

- A **form expression** that is a secondary offensive ability unique to the current form — an area effect, a sustained emanation, or a condition applied to the whole field. The form expression is what makes each configuration feel genuinely different from a combat standpoint. An ice form's expression might create difficult terrain in a radius. A fire form's expression might deal damage to all creatures that start their turn within 10 feet. A lightning form's expression might grant the Form Shifter a free reaction against ranged attacks.

- A **Multiattack** that lets the Form Shifter use its primary strike twice, or its primary strike and its form expression once each. The Multiattack's composition should stay consistent within a form and change completely between forms — this is one of the clearest ways to signal a form transition to attentive players.

> **Form Shifter-specific note:** Write all forms' Actions in the same stat block under clearly labeled subsections — "Fire Form," "Ice Form," "Lightning Form" — rather than as separate stat blocks. The Form Shifter is one creature whose available actions change; giving it separate stat blocks implies it is a different creature in each form, which is both mechanically confusing and thematically incorrect. The player-facing stat block should clearly show what changes between forms and what stays constant.

#### Bonus Actions

The Form Shifter's Bonus Actions are its **cycle management tools** — what controls when it shifts and what it does with the transition window.

- A **shift bonus action** that triggers the form transition. This ability fires at the start of the Form Shifter's turn, before its actions, and announces the new form to the party. The shift should not be random — it should follow a consistent pattern the party can learn. Either a fixed sequence (fire → ice → lightning → fire), a triggered shift when the Form Shifter takes a specific damage type, or a shift that occurs when the Form Shifter is reduced below a HP threshold. The pattern must be consistent across the encounter so the party can learn it.

- A **residue deployment bonus action** that places or extends the current form's environmental residue. This ability should fire on rounds when the Form Shifter is not shifting — the party has solved the identification puzzle this round, and the Form Shifter responds by making the battlefield more complex. The residue should be placed to interfere with the approach the party is currently using against the active form.

> **On the shift bonus action:** This is the encounter's central rhythm and the source of the Opportunity Weakness. The shift fires at the top of the Form Shifter's turn — before it acts — which means the party has the information from the signal tool before the Form Shifter's attacks land. A party that has learned to read the signal will already know the form before the shift fires; a party that has not will be one round behind until they do. The shift's predictability is not a weakness in the design — it is the reward mechanism. Predictable shifts are how the party gets ahead of the encounter.

#### Reactions

The Form Shifter has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are form-coherent defenses that punish attacking the wrong configuration and communicate the defensive profile of the current form.

**Required:**

- **A form defense reaction** *(trigger: a creature attacks the Form Shifter with a damage type or approach the current form resists or is immune to).* The Form Shifter takes reduced or no damage from the attack and immediately deals damage of its current form's type to the attacker. This is the Access wall — attacking the wrong form is not merely ineffective, it is harmful. The reaction must fire with a clear narrative description each time: attacking the ice form with cold damage and watching it simply absorb the attack, then feeling the chill radiate back. Players who see this reaction fire know immediately that they are attacking wrong — and they know which direction the right approach is, because the form's vulnerability is the opposite of what the defense just rejected. One mistake teaches twice.

- **A residue response reaction** *(trigger: a creature enters or ends its turn in an area of the Form Shifter's environmental residue).* The Form Shifter immediately triggers the residue's effect — a creature stepping into the ice patch must make a Dexterity saving throw or fall Prone, a creature ending its turn in the scorched zone takes fire damage, a creature within the lightning aura has its next attack made with disadvantage. This reaction is the Terrain Strength made active: the accumulated residue is not just difficult terrain, it is something the Form Shifter can leverage in real time.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A transition burst** *(trigger: the Form Shifter uses its shift bonus action).* As it transitions out of its current form, the Form Shifter releases a burst of the old form's energy — all creatures within 10 feet take damage of the departing form's type. This reaction makes transitions dangerous moments for the party, creating a cost for clustering near the Form Shifter at any time. A party that learns to read the shift and spread out before it fires has solved an additional layer of the puzzle.
- **A form lock** *(trigger: the Form Shifter takes damage from its current form's vulnerability type).* The Form Shifter cannot shift on its next turn — it is pinned in its current configuration by the effective attack. This reaction rewards the party for attacking correctly: a successful hit with the right approach not only deals full damage, it holds the Form Shifter in the vulnerable configuration for one additional round. Players who discover this reaction exists will prioritize finding the vulnerability every turn, because the payoff is two turns in the window instead of one.
- **A predatory shift** *(trigger: a creature uses an action to attempt to identify the Form Shifter's current vulnerability through a skill check or similar ability).* The Form Shifter immediately shifts to a new form. This reaction punishes passive information-gathering and rewards active engagement: players who stop to analyze will find their analysis invalidated. This option works best for Form Shifters whose identity is predatory and responsive — creatures that hunt by exploiting the moment of hesitation.

> **On the form defense reaction:** This is the Form Shifter's most important reaction for communicating the puzzle. It must fire with an immediate narrative cause-and-effect that tells the party two things at once: this did not work, and here is the direction you should be going instead. The reaction's damage type should always be the current form's type — fire form absorbs cold attacks and returns fire, ice form absorbs fire and returns cold — so the party learns the defensive and offensive profile simultaneously from a single wrong attack.

---

### 4. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Form Shifter's CR calibration follows the standard process with one Form Shifter-specific adjustment: **the Form Shifter's effective DPR and effective HP both fluctuate with the party's adaptation speed, and CR benchmarks should be set against the average across the full cycle rather than any single form.**

A Form Shifter the party is attacking correctly in its current form is roughly at its stat block values. A Form Shifter the party is attacking incorrectly is significantly more durable than its HP suggests and deals more effective damage than its DPR implies, because attacks are both absorbed and returned. CR calibration must account for an average that includes both states.

**Steps:**

1. **Set HP at baseline and AC slightly above baseline.** The Form Shifter's durability comes from its form defense reaction and from the party's wasted attacks on wrong approaches, not from raw HP. HP at baseline ensures the encounter ends decisively once the party is consistently attacking correctly. AC slightly above baseline represents the Form Shifter's general evasiveness — it is a moving, changing target, and that motion makes it harder to pin down.

2. **Set each form's offensive output at curve.** The Form Shifter should deal at-curve damage in every form. What changes between configurations is the type and texture of the damage, not the magnitude. Keeping all forms at curve prevents the encounter from having a "safe form" the party can wait out.

3. **Set each form's vulnerability as a damage type the party can reliably access.** The Opportunity Weakness only lands if the party has the tools to exploit it. Check the party's damage type distribution before finalizing forms. Every form's vulnerability should be something at least two party members can deliver.

4. **Assign ability scores once, not per form.** The Form Shifter has one set of ability scores that persists across all configurations. Only the damage type and thematic profile changes between forms — not the creature's fundamental physical and mental stats. Use the attack bonus and Save DC of the primary strike to set the ability scores as normal.

5. **Distribute damage across categories with Form Shifter priorities:**
   - **Actions:** At-curve DPR from the primary strike and form expression combined, consistent across all forms.
   - **Bonus Actions:** The shift bonus action deals no damage. The residue deployment bonus action may impose conditions but deals no direct damage. The Form Shifter's Bonus Action DPR is zero or minimal.
   - **Reactions:** The form defense reaction deals damage of the current form's type when it fires. Estimate the expected damage contribution based on how often the party will attack with the wrong approach — typically once or twice per round early in the encounter, dropping to zero once the party solves the cycle. The residue response reaction deals modest damage on a saving throw.

6. **Design the residue accumulation curve deliberately.** The Terrain Strength compounds over the encounter's duration. A useful rule of thumb: residue from a given form persists for a number of rounds equal to the number of active forms — so in a three-form rotation, each form's residue lasts three rounds before dissipating. This means the first full cycle leaves one active residue zone, the second cycle leaves two overlapping, and by the third cycle the battlefield has become genuinely complex.

---

7. **Design the Phase 3 mechanical payoff before the encounter begins.** When the Form Shifter locks into its final form, something concrete must change — not just the pace. Options: all prior residue becomes simultaneously active; the locked form's immunity collapses to resistance; the Form Shifter's AC drops as it loses the evasive fluidity of shifting; a condition that could not be applied mid-cycle can now be applied freely. The payoff must be a specific mechanical shift the party can feel immediately. Name it before the session. The party that decoded the cycle should arrive at Phase 3 with a boss that is more exploitable than it was at any earlier point — more time in the window plus a weaker target is the Weakness fully expressed.

---

### The Form Shifter's Tell

Every Form Shifter stat block must have one per form, plus one for the transition, plus one for the Phase 3 payoff. Write them in plain language before finalizing the stat block:

> *"This Form Shifter signals its [form name] configuration by [specific observable cue — physical, behavioral, environmental, or auditory]. Its vulnerability in this form is [damage type or approach]. Players who [read the signal and attack correctly within the window] will deal full damage — and if they trigger the form lock reaction, they hold the window for a second round."*

> *"This Form Shifter transitions by [specific observable behavior]. Players who [read the transition signal and spread out before it fires] will avoid the transition burst and be positioned for the next form before it acts."*

> *"When Phase 3 locks in, [specific mechanical change — AC drops by X, all residue activates simultaneously, immunity becomes resistance, condition Y becomes applicable]. Players who have decoded the cycle will recognize this as the Weakness opening — the same approach that worked mid-cycle now works on a weaker target with no time pressure."*

If you cannot complete all three sentences, either the cycle is not readable through play or the Phase 3 payoff has not been designed. Go back and define both before running the encounter. Go back to the signal tools and define the cue for each form and each transition with enough specificity that the DM can narrate it consistently from round one. The cycle must be learnable. Players who pay attention should feel ahead of it by round three.

## The Reverser

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the boss is turning the party's own capabilities against them, and the more competently the party plays, the worse the situation becomes — until they figure out what they are actually doing to themselves. The puzzle is not how to hit harder. It is how to stop hitting in ways that help.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A Reverser's high concept must communicate reactive inversion — a boss that does not simply defend or evade but specifically converts the party's actions into fuel for its own power, sustain, or offense. The Reverser is not strong because it has high stats. It is strong because the party keeps making it strong, and they do not realize it until they have been doing it for two rounds.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what does the Reverser convert, and into what?* The inversion should feel specific and legible — not "it counters everything" but a coherent fiction for why this particular thing turns back on the party. A demon of karmic law that reflects violence back at its source. A construct built to absorb and redirect kinetic energy. A martial artist whose style teaches using an opponent's force against them. A cursed mirror that makes every offensive act self-harm. The conversion logic is the identity.

**Strong examples:** "The Mirror That Remembers Every Blow," "The Karmic Engine of the Iron Monastery," "She Who Grows from What You Give Her," "The Construct That Was Built to Be Fought."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Reflects Damage," "Counters Attacks," "Absorbs Spells." These describe single mechanical outputs, not identity. The high concept should communicate *what the Reverser does with what it receives* — the downstream consequence of the inversion is what makes the puzzle feel like understanding a system rather than finding an exception.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Reverser template asks two questions simultaneously: **what does the Reverser convert, and what is it doing with the converted resource?**

Both must be fully designed before any stat block entry is written. The inversion input is what the party must stop doing. The inversion output is what the party must survive while they figure that out. Without a clear output, the inversion feels like a damage-nullification gimmick. Without a clear input, the puzzle has no solution.

#### The Reverser's Tools

**Inversion Tools** — the specific mechanism through which the Reverser converts the party's actions.
*(Examples: a trait that causes the Reverser to regain HP equal to half the damage dealt by attacks of a specific damage type; a reaction that converts a condition imposed by a party member into the same condition on the party member; a passive that causes the Reverser to gain a stacking attack bonus each time it takes damage from a specific source; a feature that causes any forced movement applied to the Reverser to be applied instead to the creature that applied it. The inversion must be specific — it covers a defined category, not "all attacks." The narrower the inversion, the cleaner the puzzle; the broader, the more overwhelming without being more interesting.)*

**Output Tools** — what the Reverser does with the resource the inversion provides.
*(Examples: regained HP that extends the encounter; a stacking damage bonus applied to its personal strikes; a compounding condition that the party accumulates while feeding the inversion; a buff to its defenses that makes it progressively harder to hit while the wrong approach is being used. The output is what creates urgency — not just "this approach doesn't work" but "this approach is actively making things worse in a specific, visible way.")*

**Blind Spot Tools** — what the Reverser cannot convert, and what makes that blind spot discoverable.
*(Examples: a specific damage type the inversion explicitly excludes; a category of action the conversion logic does not cover — the Reverser absorbs attack damage but not conditions, or absorbs direct damage but not ongoing effects; an approach that requires the party to act in a way that contradicts its instincts — dealing no damage for a round, targeting the environment rather than the boss, using an item rather than an ability. The blind spot must be observable: the party should be able to notice that the Reverser is not reacting to a specific thing, and from that non-reaction deduce that this is the path forward.)*

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Reverser's action economy is built around **personal lethality that scales with the party's continued wrong approach and a passive inversion that punishes conventional play**, with the boss needing to do relatively little on its own turn because the party is doing most of the work for it.

#### Actions

**The Reverser should have:**

- A **primary strike** that is at or above curve in damage and becomes stronger the more the inversion has been fed this round — expressed as a bonus to attack or damage tied to the number of times the inversion fired this round, or access to an additional effect that only becomes available after the inversion has activated. This attack communicates that the wrong approach is not just ineffective — it is actively improving the Reverser's offense. The party should feel the primary strike getting worse as the encounter progresses on wrong terms.

- A **secondary strike** that is below curve in damage but imposes a condition — the Reverser's fallback for rounds where the party has successfully withheld the inversion's fuel. The secondary strike is the Reverser fighting on its own terms without assistance from the party, and it should feel noticeably weaker. This contrast is important: the party should feel the difference between a round where they fed the inversion and a round where they did not.

- A **Multiattack** that uses the primary strike twice when the inversion has been fed, or the primary and secondary once each when it has not. The Reverser's Multiattack composition is itself a tell: a party that notices the boss using double primary strikes is a party that knows they triggered the inversion twice this round.

> **Reverser-specific note:** The Reverser should never generate its own inversion fuel on its own turn — the inversion is triggered by the party's actions, not the Reverser's. A Reverser that generates its own fuel is not a Reverser; it is a self-sustaining boss. Every point of advantage the Reverser has at the start of its turn was put there by the party, and that should be legible.

#### Bonus Actions

The Reverser's Bonus Actions are its **teaching tools** — what makes the inversion logic visible and communicates the blind spot to a party that has not yet found it.

- A **blind spot test bonus action** that becomes available each time the Reverser uses its mirror reaction — the mirror reaction stores the deflected ability, and this bonus action expends that stored ability by copying it back at the attacker on the Reverser's next turn. Once used, it is unavailable until the mirror reaction fires again and stores a new ability. The Reverser can only hold one stored ability at a time — if the mirror reaction fires again before this bonus action is used, the new ability replaces the old one.

- A **pressure bonus action** that the Reverser uses on rounds where the inversion has not fired — when the party has withheld the fuel or found the blind spot. This should be a modest additional effect: a repositioning, a minor condition, a small area of difficult terrain. Its purpose is to ensure the encounter maintains pressure even when the party is playing correctly, and to communicate that the Reverser has noticed the change in approach.

> **On the blind spot test bonus action:** This fires exactly once, on the round the party appears to have settled into a consistent approach. The narration must make it feel like demonstration rather than attack — "the Reverser mimics your form precisely and strikes back with your own element." Players who notice the mimicry have been told, in the clearest possible terms, what category the inversion covers.

#### Reactions

The Reverser has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the inversion is most directly communicated — the moments where the party's attacks trigger visible, immediate consequences that teach them what they are doing to themselves.

**Required:**

- **An inversion reaction** *(trigger: a creature attacks the Reverser using an action that falls within the inversion's coverage — a specific damage type, a category of ability, a type of forced movement).* The Reverser immediately applies the inversion's output — regaining HP, gaining a stacking bonus, reflecting a condition, or applying an effect to the attacker. This is the encounter's primary teaching moment: the party member whose attack triggered this reaction sees the immediate consequence of their action, and the narration must make the causal relationship explicit. The inversion reaction should fire visibly and often during the puzzle phase, because every trigger is a data point that helps the party identify the coverage.

- **A mirror reaction** *(trigger: a creature attacks the Reverser using an action that falls outside the inversion's coverage — the blind spot).* The Reverser automatically succeeds on any saving throw involved, or reduces the damage to zero, and stores the ability for use as its blind spot test bonus action on its next turn. When it uses that bonus action, it copies the ability back at the attacker — requiring a saving throw or attack roll appropriate to the Reverser's nature and primary ability score. A martial Reverser that absorbed a grapple might force a Strength saving throw; a spellcasting Reverser that deflected an arcane effect might force an Intelligence or Charisma saving throw; a creature of pure physical force might make an attack roll against the original attacker's AC using its primary ability modifier. The specific roll type should be established in the stat block before the encounter begins and remain consistent across all uses of the blind spot test. This reaction communicates two things simultaneously: the blind spot approach bypassed the inversion, so the boss had to answer it by other means; and the boss has now demonstrated exactly what that approach looks like, narrating its own vulnerability in the act of defending against it. A party that sees this reaction fire knows they just found the right tool — the Reverser did not absorb it, it deflected it, and the deflection exposed the gap.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A pursuit inversion** *(trigger: a creature uses the Disengage action after having triggered the inversion this round).* The Reverser applies one additional stack of the inversion's output to that creature — the deliberate choice to withdraw safely after feeding the inversion compounds the return. A creature that attacks and then disengages is trying to have it both ways; the pursuit inversion ensures that strategy carries a cost. This option works best when the high concept involves karmic accumulation or physical momentum.
- **An overload** *(trigger: the inversion reaction fires three or more times in a single round).* The accumulated energy exceeds what the Reverser can cleanly process — it discharges the excess as an area burst centered on itself, dealing damage of its inversion's associated type to all creatures within 10 feet. The party that commits too heavily to the wrong approach in a single round does not simply feed the Reverser more efficiently; they overwhelm it into a dangerous release. This option adds a ceiling to how much the party can feed the inversion per round, and works best for Reversers whose inversion logic has a physical or elemental expression.
- **A stillness** *(trigger: a creature ends its turn without having attacked the Reverser or used an ability that would trigger the inversion).* The Reverser gains advantage on its next attack against that creature. A party member who correctly withholds the inversion fuel is doing the right thing — but the Reverser notices the restraint and marks them. The correct approach is not to do nothing; it is to use the blind spot. This option discourages passive play and ensures that withholding the inversion alone is not the solution.

> **On the mirror reaction:** This is the Reverser's most important reaction for communicating the Opportunity Weakness. When it fires, two things happen simultaneously: the party member learns their blind spot approach landed without feeding the inversion, and the Reverser demonstrates exactly what they just did by copying it back. The deflection is the tell; the copy is the confirmation. A party that sees this reaction fire once should understand that the blind spot approach is the correct path — and that committing to it will not trigger the inversion.

---

### 4. The Inversion Logic — Building the Pattern Problem

The inversion logic is the Reverser encounter's puzzle. Before any stat block entry is written, the DM must be able to state the inversion coverage, the output, and the blind spot in plain language — and verify that every reaction, bonus action, and trait in the encounter is consistent with them.

**The inversion logic must have:**

**A specific coverage definition.** The inversion covers a defined category, not "all offensive actions." The category should be specific enough to be discoverable through observation — "physical attack rolls," "fire and lightning damage," "conditions imposed by abilities," "any action that requires an attack roll" — and broad enough that the party's default approach falls within it. If the party's default approach is outside the inversion, the puzzle phase never begins. If everything is inside the inversion, the blind spot cannot exist.

**A visible output.** The inversion's consequence must be narrated every time it fires, and the narration must make the connection between the party's action and the Reverser's benefit explicit. HP restoration should be visible as a wound closing. A stacking bonus should be visible as a physical change in the boss. A reflected condition should arrive on the party member before their attack resolves. The output is what teaches the party that something is wrong with their approach.

**A discoverable blind spot.** The blind spot — the category outside the inversion's coverage — must be present from round one, observable through non-reaction, and achievable without requiring specific abilities the party may not possess. A blind spot requiring a specific spell the party's wizard did not prepare is a locked door. A blind spot requiring the party to act in a way anyone can manage — using an object in the environment, applying a non-damage condition, attacking in a way that is universally available — is a puzzle.

**A deduction trail.** The party should be able to reconstruct the inversion's coverage from two or three rounds of evidence. Round one: the fighter's sword swing triggers the inversion. Round two: the rogue's sneak attack triggers it. Round three: the wizard's fire bolt triggers it. Deduction: the inversion covers direct damage attacks. The druid's entangle — a non-damage condition — has not triggered it. That is the trail that leads to the blind spot.

---

### 5. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Reverser's CR calibration follows the standard process with one Reverser-specific adjustment: **the encounter's effective difficulty scales with how quickly the party identifies the blind spot, and CR benchmarks must account for the two-phase structure — wrong approach and correct approach — rather than a single consistent state.**

During the wrong approach phase, the Reverser is significantly more durable than its stat block suggests, because every attack the party makes is partially or fully feeding the inversion's output. During the correct approach phase, it is roughly at stat block values — the blind spot attacks deal normal damage and the inversion does not fire. Calibrate for the wrong approach phase, knowing the correct approach phase will be significantly faster.

**Steps:**

1. **Set AC at baseline, HP at or slightly below baseline.** The Reverser is not hard to hit — it is hard to damage net-positively while the inversion is running. Moderate AC expresses this: the boss is reachable and hittable, the problem is that hitting it in the wrong way makes it stronger. Below-baseline HP ensures the correct approach phase resolves quickly once the party finds the blind spot.

2. **Set the primary strike at or above curve, scaling above curve when the inversion fires.** The primary strike must be a genuine threat while the inversion is running. The party should feel the boss getting more dangerous in direct proportion to their wrong approach. This is the encounter's clearest feedback signal.

3. **Set the secondary strike below curve.** The secondary strike is the Reverser without the party's help. It should feel noticeably weaker than the primary — weaker than a standard CR creature of the same tier. This contrast is the encounter's second clearest feedback signal: a party that notices the boss hitting harder on some rounds and correlates it with the inversion firing is already solving the puzzle.

4. **Design the inversion's output with a specific chassis, not just a percentage.** The inversion should negate roughly 30–50% of the party's expected damage per round during the wrong approach phase — but the chassis determines how that feels at the table. Three options, each with different implications:

   - *HP restoration on hit:* The Reverser regains HP equal to a fixed amount whenever the inversion reaction fires — for example, half the damage dealt by the triggering attack. This chassis makes the inversion visually legible (wounds closing) and mathematically trackable. Its weakness is that it can feel swingy when a high-damage hit heals the boss dramatically. Use when the inversion output should feel dramatic and attributable.
   - *Stacking attack bonus:* The Reverser gains a +1 or +2 bonus to its next primary strike each time the inversion fires, up to a maximum of +4 or +6. This chassis makes the inversion's output forward-facing — the consequence of wrong-approach attacks is a future hit, not a present heal. It rewards the party for changing approaches quickly before the stack grows too large. Use when the inversion should feel like a compounding threat rather than immediate sustain.
   - *Reflected condition:* The Reverser applies the same condition the attacker imposed on it back to the attacker — Frightened, Restrained, Prone — if the attack carried one. This chassis only fires on attacks that impose conditions, which naturally narrows the inversion's scope. It is the most thematically pure option but requires the party to use condition-imposing attacks regularly. Use when the high concept is karmic or mirror-based.

   Mixing chassis is possible but adds complexity — if the inversion restores HP *and* grants a stacking bonus, the DM must track two outputs simultaneously. Default to one chassis per encounter.

5. **Design the blind spot to be achievable by at least two party members without preparation.** The blind spot cannot be locked behind a specific resource or class ability. At least two members of any standard party should be able to use it without spending a limited resource on round one. The puzzle is discovery, not access.

6. **Calibrate the inversion's coverage breadth against the encounter's intended difficulty.** A narrow inversion — covering only one damage type — is easy to notice and bypass. A broad inversion — covering all attack rolls — is harder to notice and bypass. The breadth should match the encounter's significance: an introductory Reverser should have narrow coverage; a climactic one should have broad coverage with a non-obvious blind spot.

---

### The Reverser's Tell

Every Reverser encounter must have two — one for the inversion and one for the blind spot:

> *"This Reverser's inversion covers [specific category of action]. The party can identify it by [specific observable tell — the inversion reaction fires with the same narrated consequence every time the covered category is used]. The output of the inversion is [specific visible consequence — HP restored, bonus gained, condition reflected]."*

> *"The blind spot is [specific category outside the inversion's coverage]. It is discoverable by [specific observable tell — the Reverser uses its mirror reaction when this category is used, deflecting rather than absorbing, and copies the ability back at the attacker]. Players who [identify the mirror reaction and commit to the blind spot approach] will find the encounter resolving on normal terms — the boss's advantage disappears, the secondary strike's weakness is exposed, and the fight that felt unwinnable becomes straightforward."*

If you cannot complete both sentences, the inversion has no specific coverage and no discoverable blind spot — the encounter is a frustrating wall with no correct answer. Go back to the inversion logic and define coverage with enough specificity to generate consistent reactions, and define the blind spot with enough accessibility that any party can reach it within three rounds of observation. The Reverser's power is in what the party gives it. The DM's responsibility is to ensure there is always something the party can choose not to give.

## The State Shifter

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the party knows what to do. They keep doing it at the wrong moment. The puzzle is not identification — it is timing. Solving the cycle means not just reading the current state but knowing when it will end, so the party can commit to the right approach in the last window before the next state arrives rather than the first moment after the previous one left.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A State Shifter's high concept must communicate cyclical identity — a boss that is not one thing but a sequence of things, each complete and coherent in itself, arranged in a logic the party must decode before they can act on what they know. The State Shifter is not hard to read. Its current state is visible. What is hard is knowing how long it will last and what triggers the next one.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what is the logic behind the cycle, and what does moving through it look like from the outside?* The states should feel like expressions of a single underlying identity cycling through aspects of itself — a demon of war cycling through attack, defense, and devastation phases; a bound elemental rotating through its constituent elements; a shapeshifter whose forms are driven by the hunger or exhaustion of the current shape. The cycle should feel authored, not arbitrary.

**Strong examples:** "The War-Aspect That Has Always Cycled," "The Bound Elemental Whose Natures Take Turns," "She Who Is Never Twice the Same Threat," "The Hunger That Feeds Through Each Form In Sequence."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Phase Boss," "State-Changing Enemy," "Rotating Defenses." These describe mechanics, not identity. The high concept should communicate *why* the cycle exists — the fiction behind the rotation is what makes reading it feel like understanding the boss rather than reading a buff tracker.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The State Shifter template asks two questions simultaneously: **what are the states, and what triggers the transitions between them?**

Both must be fully designed before any stat block entry is written. The states are the encounter's content — what the boss does and what works against it in each configuration. The transitions are the encounter's puzzle — what the party must learn to predict.

#### The State Shifter's Tools

**State Tools** — the discrete configurations the boss cycles through.
*(Each state must have a distinct defensive profile, a distinct offensive pattern, and a distinct preferred counter. The distinctness is what makes the cycle a puzzle rather than decoration. If two states can be addressed by the same approach, they are effectively the same state and should be merged. Limit to two or three states — more than three creates a tracking problem that exceeds table working memory under combat pressure.)*

**Transition Tools** — the specific conditions that trigger movement from one state to the next.
*(Examples: a fixed-duration trigger, where each state lasts exactly two rounds before transitioning regardless of party action; a damage-threshold trigger, where the state shifts when the boss takes a specific amount of damage in a single round; a behavioral trigger, where the transition fires when the party uses a specific type of ability or achieves a specific positional condition; a sequential trigger, where each state has a unique transition condition that must be decoded separately. The transition trigger is the Pattern Strength's core: if the party cannot figure out what causes the shift, they are always one step behind.)*

**Window Tools** — what makes each state's vulnerability accessible for the right amount of time.
*(The window is the gap between when the party can identify the current state's weakness and when the transition fires. Windows should be generous enough that a party with correct timing can act meaningfully — at least two full player turns — and narrow enough that a party that wastes time on the wrong approach cannot easily recover. The window is what makes timing the skill rather than just identification.)*

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The State Shifter's action economy is built around **state-coherent offense that communicates the current configuration to attentive players**, with transition signals embedded in the boss's behavior that reward the party for tracking the cycle rather than just reacting to it.

#### Actions

**The State Shifter should have, within each state:**

- A **state attack** that is thematically coherent with the current state's offensive profile and visibly distinct from the other states' attacks — not just in damage type but in structure. A defensive state might use an attack that creates distance; an offensive state might use one that closes it; a devastation state might use an area attack that was not available in the others. The structural difference is what allows the party to identify the current state from the attack pattern alone, providing a second legibility channel alongside any explicit transition signal.

- A **transition tell** embedded in the state attack's behavior — a change in how the attack is used, a shift in targeting, a behavioral pattern the party can learn to associate with an impending transition. The tell is not the transition itself; it is the signal that the transition is coming. A party that recognizes the tell has one additional round of warning before the window closes.

- A **Multiattack** whose composition is consistent within a state and distinct between states. The Multiattack is the most legible state indicator available: the boss uses three attacks in one state and two in another, or uses a specific combination of attacks in each state that it does not use in the others. The Multiattack composition change at the moment of transition is the clearest possible announcement that the state has shifted.

> **State Shifter-specific note:** Every attack the State Shifter makes should be narrated with state-appropriate description — not just damage type but texture, speed, style, and apparent intent. The party is learning the cycle through the boss's behavior, and behavior is communicated through narration. A DM who narrates all states identically is removing the party's primary information source.

#### Bonus Actions

The State Shifter's Bonus Actions are its **state reinforcement and transition preparation tools** — what deepens the current state's threat profile and signals that a transition is imminent.

- A **state intensification bonus action** that applies an additional effect of the current state's type — a secondary condition, an extended area, a deepened debuff — that was not available in the base state attack. This ability communicates that the boss is fully committed to the current state: it is not just doing the thing the state does, it is doing it as completely as possible. A party that sees this bonus action fire knows they are in a stable window and have time to act on their knowledge.

- A **transition preparation bonus action** that signals an impending state change — the boss shifts its posture, changes the register of its voice, or makes a gesture that attentive players can learn to associate with the coming transition. This ability must be the last thing the State Shifter does on its turn: it cannot be used before moving, attacking, or taking any other action, and using it immediately ends the State Shifter's turn. When it fires, the transition happens instantly — the state changes at the end of the current turn, not at the start of the next one. The transition preparation is therefore not advance warning of a future change; it is the announcement of an immediate one. A party that sees it fire and recognizes it has a narrow window — the remainder of the current round, on other creatures' turns — to reposition and prepare their approach before they need to commit to the new state.

> **On the transition preparation bonus action:** Because using this ability ends the State Shifter's turn immediately, it is always a sacrifice — the boss gives up whatever actions or movement it had not yet taken in order to transition now. A party that notices this pattern will begin watching for it: a State Shifter that has not yet moved or attacked when it uses the transition preparation has sacrificed more than one that used all its other actions first. The amount sacrificed tells the party something about the transition's urgency. Either way, a party member who recognizes the signal on the round it fires has the rest of that round — every other creature's turn before the next round begins — to prepare for the new state.

#### Reactions

The State Shifter has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These express the current state's defensive profile and punish the party for attacking at the wrong moment — either in the wrong state or in the window immediately after a transition, before they have identified the new state's weakness.

**Required:**

- **A state defense reaction** *(trigger: a creature attacks the State Shifter with an approach that the current state resists or is immune to).* The State Shifter reduces the damage to zero or succeeds automatically on the saving throw, and immediately makes one state attack against the attacker at advantage. This is the Vulnerability Defining Trait made mechanical: attacking in the wrong state is not just ineffective, it is actively dangerous. The reaction must fire with a narrated description that communicates the current state's defensive nature — "the blow passes through the defensive aspect as if striking smoke" or "the fire washes off the fire-aspect's skin and it turns toward you, interested." The party must hear what just happened and why.

- **A transition strike reaction** *(trigger: a creature attacks the State Shifter on the same turn the State Shifter used its transition preparation bonus action).* The State Shifter makes one immediate state attack against that creature using the new state's attack profile. The transition preparation has fired — the new state is here — and a party member who attacks anyway rather than repositioning has committed into the worst possible moment. The transition strike punishes this specifically: you saw the tell, and you attacked anyway.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A retaliatory lock** *(trigger: a creature attacks the State Shifter with the current state's preferred counter — the correct approach lands cleanly).* The State Shifter immediately makes one state attack against that creature at advantage. Landing the right approach draws the State Shifter's focused attention — the boss recognizes the threat and responds to it. Correct play is not free. The party member who found the right tool is the one who earns the boss's personal attention for it.
- **A punishing transition** *(trigger: a creature attacks the State Shifter on the round the transition preparation bonus action fired — the advance warning round).* The State Shifter makes one immediate state attack against that creature. Attacking on the warning round is a specific mistake — the party member saw the tell and attacked anyway rather than repositioning. The punishing transition makes that mistake cost something, teaching the party that the warning round is for preparation, not last-chance attacks.
- **A false tell** *(trigger: a creature takes an action on the same turn the transition preparation bonus action fired — after the transition has resolved).* The State Shifter reverts to the previous state for one round only, then transitions again at the start of its next turn — this time without using the transition preparation bonus action. The party member who committed to the new state immediately has their action spend into the wrong configuration, but only for one round. The punishment is the wasted action, not a full reversion that invalidates their understanding. The party that sees this fire once understands the lesson: the transition round is a preparation round, not an action round. The lesson costs one action, not a character death. This option has a single use; after it fires, the transition preparation always fires honestly and the next transition is permanent.

> **On the state defense reaction:** This is the State Shifter's most important reaction for communicating both the Vulnerability Defining Trait and the Pattern Strength. Every time it fires, it must accomplish two things simultaneously: punish the wrong approach, and communicate what the current state's defensive nature is. A party member who triggers this reaction and receives the narrated description has been given the most direct possible information about the current state — they know what it resists, which by implication tells them what the preferred counter is not.

---

### 4. The Cycle — Building the Timing Problem

The cycle is the State Shifter encounter's puzzle engine. Before any stat block entry is written, the DM must be able to narrate the full cycle — all states, all transition conditions, all tells — and verify that every mechanical element in the encounter is consistent with it.

**The cycle must have:**

**States that are distinctly different in both what the party should do and what the boss does.** The identification half of the puzzle is trivial if the states feel similar. Each state should demand a noticeably different approach — different damage types, different positioning, different ability categories — and produce noticeably different attacks. A party member who has not been paying attention should be able to tell the state changed from the boss's behavior alone, without being told explicitly.

**Transition conditions that are discoverable through observation.** The first transition may be surprising — the party did not know to watch for it. The second transition should be anticipated — the party saw the tell last time. The third should be pre-empted — the party has decoded the pattern and is positioning for the next state before the transition fires. This three-step learning curve is the encounter's pacing structure.

**A minimum window of two full player turns.** Every state must remain stable long enough for the party to act meaningfully within it. A state that lasts one player turn is not a window — it is a flash the party cannot respond to. Two full player turns means everyone at the table has at least one opportunity to act in the correct state once they have identified it.

**A consistent transition signal.** Whatever the transition condition, it should produce a visible, narrated tell every time it fires — the same description, the same behavioral cue, the same environmental effect. Consistency is what makes the tell learnable. A transition that looks different each time it fires cannot be anticipated.

---

### 5. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The State Shifter's CR calibration follows the standard process with one State Shifter-specific adjustment: **the effective difficulty of the encounter varies significantly between parties that have decoded the cycle and parties that have not, and CR benchmarks must account for a learning curve rather than a consistent threat level.**

In the first two rounds, before the cycle is decoded, the State Shifter is significantly more durable than its stat block suggests — attacks in the wrong state deal zero effective damage and risk triggering the state defense reaction. By round four or five, a party that has decoded the cycle is attacking consistently in the right windows and the boss is roughly at stat block values. Calibrate for the decoded state, knowing the first two rounds will be harder.

**Steps:**

1. **Set AC at baseline, HP at or slightly above baseline.** The State Shifter is not hard to hit — it is hard to damage in the wrong state. Moderate AC expresses this: the boss is reachable, the problem is hitting it at the right moment. Slightly above baseline HP reflects that each state's window requires sustained commitment rather than a single well-timed strike.

2. **Set each state's attack at or above curve, with distinct profiles per state.** Each state should feel like a genuinely different threat. The offensive profile should change not just in damage type but in structure — the number of attacks, the conditions imposed, the targeting logic. This distinctness is what makes state identification reliable through behavioral observation.

3. **Set each state's resistance or immunity to cover the party's most common approach.** The state defense reaction fires on incorrect approaches. If the party's default approach is outside every state's defensive profile, the reaction never fires and the Pattern Strength is never communicated. Design each state's defense to cover at least one common party approach, ensuring the reaction fires in the early encounter and teaches the party what they are doing wrong.

4. **Design windows that reward preparation over reaction.** The minimum two-turn window is the floor. Longer windows — three or four turns — are appropriate for introductory State Shifter encounters where the party needs time to identify and act. Shorter windows — approaching the two-turn minimum — are appropriate for climactic encounters where the party's pattern-decoding skill is expected to be high.

5. **Design transition conditions that require two rounds of observation to identify.** A transition condition the party figures out on round one is a non-puzzle. A transition condition that requires six rounds of data collection under combat pressure may never be decoded. Two to three rounds of observation, with consistent tells, is the correct calibration for a party of four.

6. **Calibrate the transition preparation bonus action's placement against the window length.** The transition fires at the end of the turn the preparation bonus action is used. This means the party's preparation window is the remainder of that round — every creature's turn between the State Shifter's and the end of the round. In a high-initiative State Shifter, that window is the entire party's turns; in a low-initiative one, it may be only one or two party members. Design the State Shifter's initiative position deliberately: a high-initiative boss that transitions immediately gives the party maximum preparation time within the round, while a low-initiative boss that transitions immediately gives almost none. The window length within each state and the initiative position together determine how much time the party actually has to respond to each transition.

---

### The State Shifter's Tell

Every State Shifter encounter must have one per state and one for each transition. Write them in plain language before finalizing any stat block:

> *"State [name] is identifiable by [specific behavioral or attack pattern difference from other states]. Its preferred counter is [specific approach]. Its defensive profile resists or is immune to [specific approach category]. The window lasts [X] rounds under normal conditions."*

> *"The transition from [state A] to [state B] is triggered by [specific condition]. When it fires, the State Shifter uses its transition preparation bonus action as the last act of its turn — signaled by [specific narrated tell] — and the state changes immediately. Players who [recognize the tell and use the remaining turns in that round to reposition] will enter the new window already committed to the correct approach."*

If you cannot complete both sentences for every state and every transition, the cycle is not designed completely enough to run at the table. The State Shifter is the archetype most dependent on DM preparation — a cycle that is partially designed will be narrated inconsistently, and inconsistent narration makes the tell unlearnable. Write the full cycle before writing the stat block. The cycle is the encounter.

## The Dynamic

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **everything is changing at once, the changes are connected, and the party cannot outrun the pace until they understand the logic — but understanding the logic requires surviving the pace long enough to gather evidence. The puzzle and the pressure are the same problem.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A Dynamic's high concept must communicate systemic chaos — a boss whose threat is not any individual transformation but the simultaneous operation of multiple changing dimensions that together form a coherent, decodable system. The Dynamic is not simply complex. It is *organized* complexity — organized in a way the party cannot see until they have gathered enough data to perceive the structure beneath the surface.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what is the single underlying logic that governs all of the changes?* Every dimension of change should be an expression of the same rule, applied differently. A storm entity whose terrain changes, attack patterns, and defensive rotations all cycle in sync with an elemental sequence the party must identify. A godlike strategist whose positioning, force composition, and tactical doctrine all evolve according to the same operational principle. An ancient machine whose multiple simultaneous processes all run on the same underlying cycle. The logic is the identity — and the party's discovery of it is the encounter's resolution.

**Strong examples:** "The Living Storm Whose Every Axis Rotates Together," "The Godmind Whose Strategy Is a Single Sentence," "The Engine of Many Gears and One Shaft," "The Pattern That Wears a Boss's Face."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Many Things Happening Boss," "Confusing Enemy," "Too Much to Track." These describe the party's experience of failure, not the boss's identity. The high concept should communicate *the nature of the system* — a party that hears the high concept should be able to form a hypothesis about what the governing logic might be.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Dynamic template asks one question before anything else: **what is the single governing logic, and how does it manifest across at least two simultaneous dimensions?**

This question must be answered before any mechanical design begins. The governing logic is the encounter's answer. Every dimension, every change, every tell is a clue that points toward it. If the governing logic cannot be stated in one sentence, the Dynamic is not designed — it is generated, and generated complexity cannot be decoded.

#### The Dynamic's Tools

**Governing Logic Tools** — the single rule that connects all dimensions of change.
*(Examples: an elemental sequence that determines both the terrain's hazard type and the boss's current defensive immunity — fire terrain always coincides with fire immunity, and the sequence is always fire, lightning, cold; a positional rule that determines both the boss's movement pattern and the locations where beneficial changes appear — the boss always moves clockwise, and beneficial changes always appear in the space it just left; a behavioral principle that determines both the boss's attack targeting and its current vulnerability — it always attacks the most isolated party member, and it is always vulnerable to attacks made by the most clustered ones. The governing logic must be consistent across all dimensions. If the dimensions appear to follow different rules, the encounter is not a Dynamic — it is multiple simultaneous simpler encounters.)*

**Dimensional Change Tools** — the specific dimensions through which the governing logic manifests.
*(Each dimension is one axis along which the encounter changes — terrain, boss defensive profile, attack targeting, available resources, positioning constraints. The Dynamic requires at least two simultaneous dimensions and should not exceed three. More than three dimensions create a tracking problem that exceeds table working memory, even when the underlying logic is simple. Choose dimensions that are individually legible — each one should be readable in isolation — because the party must track each separately before they can perceive the connection.)*

**Logic Legibility Tools** — what makes the governing logic discoverable within three rounds.
*(Examples: a consistent narrated cue that precedes every change across all dimensions simultaneously — "the air shimmers" always announces the rotation, regardless of which dimension is about to change; a visible synchronization moment where all dimensions change at once, making the connection between them unavoidable; a single dimension whose changes are simpler and faster to read than the others, giving the party a foothold — once they understand dimension A's pattern, they can apply that understanding to find dimension B's pattern faster. The governing logic must be findable through observation. If it requires external knowledge or cannot be deduced from in-encounter evidence, it is not a puzzle — it is a secret.)*

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Dynamic's action economy is built around **maintaining multiple simultaneous dimensions of change and punishing the party for acting before the governing logic is decoded**, with personal combat as a secondary pressure that ensures the party cannot simply freeze and gather data indefinitely.

#### Actions

**The Dynamic should have:**

- A **dimensional advance action** that advances all active dimensions of change by one step simultaneously — placing or repositioning hazard zones, shifting the boss's active defensive immunity, and changing the targeting priority. This action has concrete battlefield effects: hazard zones are physically placed or moved on the battlefield map, not merely described. A terrain dimension might place a 20-foot radius of difficult terrain or a damaging zone in a new location; a defensive dimension might place a visible aura on the boss expressing the current immunity; a targeting dimension might mark one creature as the priority target with a visible condition. All placements happen in the same action, narrated as a single event. The party must separately decode which dimensions changed and how — but the changes must be physically present and trackable on the battlefield, not just remembered from narration.

- A **primary strike** at or above curve in damage, always targeting the party member whose behavior most clearly demonstrates they have not decoded the governing logic — the one attacking into the current defensive immunity, standing in the current hazard zone, or missing the current beneficial change. The primary strike is the Pace Strength made personal: the Dynamic notices the party member who is most behind and makes their situation worse.

- A **Multiattack** that uses the dimensional advance action and the primary strike once each. The dimensional advance always fires before the primary strike — the Dynamic places the new configuration first, then attacks into it. The order communicates its tactical logic: it is building the situation it wants to attack into, not reacting to what the party is doing.

> **Dynamic-specific note:** The dimensional advance action should feel like a single event even though it places multiple things simultaneously. Narrate it as one coherent change — "the storm rotates" or "the system advances" — but execute each placement explicitly: move the hazard zone token, update the immunity aura, mark the new priority target. The physical presence of each dimension's effect on the battlefield is what gives the party the evidence they need to decode the governing logic. A dimension that exists only in narration cannot be tracked, and a pattern that cannot be tracked cannot be decoded.

#### Bonus Actions

The Dynamic's Bonus Actions are its **pace maintenance tools** — what sustains compounding pressure between main actions and prevents the party from finding breathing room between dimensional advances.

- A **dimensional surge bonus action** that applies an additional half-step to one active dimension — the active hazard zone expands by 5 feet, the current defensive immunity deepens to include an adjacent damage type, the targeting priority narrows to a single creature rather than a category. This fires on rounds where the party appears to have found a stable position relative to the current configuration — the Dynamic responds to apparent stability by making the current configuration more extreme. The surge should always target whichever dimension the party is currently managing most successfully, making their best-handled problem temporarily worse.

- A **pace compression bonus action** that fires when the Dynamic's HP drops below a specific threshold — it takes an additional partial dimensional advance immediately, without waiting for its next turn. All active dimensions shift by half a step: hazard zones move, immunities rotate partially, targeting priorities shift. The party's read on the current configuration is briefly invalidated. This represents the Dynamic accelerating as it is damaged — the system speeding up under pressure rather than slowing down.

> **On legibility:** The governing logic should be communicated entirely through the DM's narration of what the boss is already doing — the dimensional advance, the primary strike, the reactions. When narrating the dimensional advance action, describe all changing dimensions in terms that make their correlation attributable: "the terrain frosts as the ward closes — both shift together" is narration of an action, not a special information-delivery mechanic. The party reads the logic from the boss's behavior. There is no separate communication channel.

#### Reactions

The Dynamic has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the Pace Strength is most directly expressed — the moments where acting before decoding generates immediate additional consequences.

**Required:**

- **A dimensional punishment reaction** *(trigger: a creature attacks the Dynamic using an approach that the current dimensional configuration makes ineffective — attacking into the active defensive immunity, moving through the active hazard zone, or targeting the non-priority position).* The Dynamic immediately advances one dimension by one additional step and makes one primary strike against that creature. Acting before decoding accelerates the encounter and draws personal attention. This reaction fires on every wrong approach and is therefore the encounter's most frequent teacher: every wrong action generates both additional change and personal consequence.

- **A synchronization reaction** *(trigger: a creature successfully identifies one dimension's pattern and acts on it correctly — making the correct attack type, occupying the correct position, or using the current beneficial change appropriately).* The Dynamic immediately advances a different dimension by one step, changing a configuration the party member did not just solve. Solving one dimension does not stop the others from changing. The party member who cracked dimension A discovers dimension B just shifted — the solution is partially right and immediately incomplete. This teaches the party that decoding one dimension is necessary but not sufficient: they need the governing logic, not individual pattern solutions.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A logic obscuration** *(trigger: a creature makes a successful skill check or uses an ability that would reveal information about the governing logic — Arcana, History, Insight, or a similar investigation).* The Dynamic immediately advances all dimensions simultaneously, resetting the current configuration to a new state that partially invalidates the information just gathered. The party cannot stop-and-investigate their way to the governing logic — active investigation draws active disruption. This option works best for Dynamics with a deceptive or adversarial intelligence.
- **A compounding consequence** *(trigger: a creature takes damage from one of the Dynamic's dimensional hazards — terrain damage, immunity-punishing feedback, or a targeting consequence).* The Dynamic immediately applies a secondary effect of the same dimension — the terrain damage also imposes difficult terrain, the immunity feedback also applies a condition, the targeting consequence also draws a secondary strike. The wrong approach does not merely hurt once; it hurts in compounding layers. Each round spent in the wrong configuration is worse than the last not just because the encounter advances but because the consequences within each round multiply.
- **A false simplification** *(trigger: the Dynamic is reduced below half HP for the first time).* All dimensions reset to their simplest configuration simultaneously — the terrain clears, the defensive profile drops to baseline, the targeting randomizes. For one round, the encounter looks easy. Then, at the start of the Dynamic's next turn, all dimensions advance by two steps simultaneously, jumping to a more extreme state than they occupied before the reset. The party that uses the reset round to attack freely without accounting for what is coming finds themselves in the worst configuration of the encounter. This option works best for Dynamics with a theatrical or predatory identity.

> **On the synchronization reaction:** This is the Dynamic's most important reaction for communicating the distinction between partial and complete decoding. Every time it fires, the narration must make the displacement legible: the party member solved dimension A correctly, and dimension B moved in response. Players who see this consistently will stop trying to solve dimensions individually and start looking for the underlying logic that connects them — which is exactly the shift the encounter requires.

---

### 4. The Governing Logic — Building the Pattern Problem

The governing logic is the Dynamic encounter's answer — not just the puzzle, but the solution already embedded in the encounter's design. The party's journey through the encounter is the process of reverse-engineering it. Before any stat block entry is written, the DM must be able to state the governing logic in one sentence, verify that every dimensional change is consistent with it, and confirm that the evidence pointing toward it is present from round one.

**The governing logic must have:**

**A single sentence form.** "The dimensions always rotate together, and the sequence is always A, then B, then C." "The terrain and the defensive immunity always match the same element, and both are determined by the boss's current position." "All dimensions shift whenever the boss takes damage from a specific source." If it cannot be stated in one sentence, it is not a logic — it is a collection of independent patterns, and the encounter is not a Dynamic.

**Evidence present from round one.** The party must be able to observe the governing logic operating from the first dimensional advance. The first advance should provide data points for at least two dimensions simultaneously, with enough correlation to permit a hypothesis. The hypothesis may be wrong — the first advance's data is insufficient to confirm — but it should be formable.

**A minimum of two simultaneous dimensions.** One changing dimension is a State Shifter or Form Shifter. Two or more simultaneous dimensions are what make this encounter a Dynamic. The dimensions must change in visible coordination — the party must be able to see that they changed together, even if they cannot yet see why.

**A resolution that feels like comprehension, not luck.** When the party decodes the governing logic, the encounter should change shape in a way they can feel. The changes that felt chaotic become predictable. The vulnerabilities that seemed to rotate randomly become a schedule. The resolution must feel earned. The Vulnerability Weakness opens not because the boss becomes weaker but because the party's knowledge transforms the encounter from a blizzard into a sequence.

---

### 5. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Dynamic's CR calibration follows the standard process with one Dynamic-specific adjustment: **effective difficulty varies more dramatically between undecoded and decoded states than any other Shaper archetype, because the party is operating across multiple simultaneous dimensions of failure before decoding and multiple simultaneous dimensions of advantage after.**

**Steps:**

1. **Set AC below baseline, HP above baseline.** The Dynamic is not hard to hit — it is hard to hit effectively while all dimensions are configured against the party. Low AC expresses this: the boss is reachable, the problem is that hitting it in the wrong configuration produces additional consequences. Above-baseline HP reflects that the decoded state should still require sustained effort.

2. **Set the primary strike at or above curve.** The Dynamic's personal combat must be a genuine threat while the encounter is undecoded. If the primary strike is weak, the party can absorb wrong-approach consequences indefinitely while gathering data — the Pace Strength fails to create urgency.

3. **Assign each active dimension a per-round cost that is modest individually but meaningful in aggregate.** A useful baseline: each dimension should impose a cost of roughly 25–35% of a single party member's expected HP per round when that dimension is configured against them. Two dimensions wrong simultaneously costs 50–70%. Three dimensions wrong simultaneously approaches unsustainable. This range ensures the undecoded state creates real pressure without being a TPK before round three.

4. **Set the dimensional advance's placement areas specifically before the encounter.** Each step in each dimension must place a concrete battlefield element — a hazard zone with defined boundaries, a marked immunity, a designated priority target. Define these placements in advance for at least the first three advances. Improvising placement during the encounter produces inconsistent patterns that cannot be decoded.

5. **Design the governing logic to be decodable within three rounds.** Round one: the party observes the first dimensional advance and notes what was placed. Round two: the second advance correlates with the first across both dimensions. Round three: a party member tests a hypothesis. The correlation between dimension A's placement and dimension B's placement must be visible in both rounds one and two — not just round two. If the correlation is not present from round one, the party cannot form a hypothesis until round three, which means they cannot test it until round four, which is too late.

6. **Design the dimensional punishment reaction's consequences to be survivable individually but costly in aggregate.** The reaction fires on every wrong approach — frequently in the early encounter. Each individual trigger should deal roughly 15–25% of a party member's expected HP. Two triggers in one round is painful but survivable. Three is a crisis. Four is a near-death event. Design the encounter space so that a party operating without decoded knowledge will typically generate two triggers per round, not four — the early encounter should feel dangerous and urgent, not immediately fatal.

---

### The Dynamic's Tell

Every Dynamic encounter must have one — for the governing logic:

> *"This Dynamic's governing logic is [single sentence — the rule connecting all active dimensions simultaneously]. The evidence pointing toward it is [specific data points available from the first two dimensional advances — what the party can observe about dimension A, dimension B, and their correlation]. Players who [form the correct hypothesis and test it by round three] will find all dimensions of the encounter aligning in their favor simultaneously — the boss is vulnerable, the terrain is navigable, and the resources are available. The encounter that was a system of compounding failures becomes a system of compounding advantages."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the governing logic is not single and discoverable. Go back to the dimensional change design and connect every dimension to a single rule. The rule does not need to be simple to execute. It needs to be simple to state. A party that decodes the Dynamic should be able to tell you, in one sentence, what they figured out. If they cannot, the encounter was a Dynamic in structure but not in experience.

## The Randomizer

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **everything in this encounter is an offer, and most offers are traps. The puzzle is not finding the tools — the tools are everywhere, arriving constantly, impossible to miss. The puzzle is figuring out which ones will help and which ones will make things worse, before the party has spent themselves on the wrong ones.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A Randomizer's high concept must communicate deliberate abundance — a boss that has seeded the encounter with apparent advantages, knowing that the party's instinct to use every available tool is itself the vulnerability. The Randomizer is not hiding power from the party. It is offering power, continuously, and the offers are not what they appear to be.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what is the logic behind which offers are real and which are traps?* The logic must be specific, characterful, and present from round one. A Randomizer without a coherent distinction between genuine and false opportunity is just a random hazard generator wearing a different costume. The logic is the identity — and the party's discovery of it is the resolution.

**Strong examples:** "The Gambler Who Always Holds a Winning Hand for You," "The Generous Betrayer," "She Who Gives You Exactly What She Wants You to Have," "The Trap That Looks Like a Gift in Every Configuration."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Drops Random Loot," "Has Many Effects," "Confusing Boss." These describe the party's experience of being overwhelmed, not the encounter's identity. The high concept should communicate *why* the offers exist — the deliberate intelligence behind the abundance is what makes decoding it feel like understanding an adversary rather than surviving a slot machine.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Randomizer template asks one question before anything else: **what is the rule that separates genuine opportunities from harmful ones, and how does that rule manifest visibly in the encounter?**

The rule must be present from round one. The rule must be consistent throughout the encounter. The rule must be discoverable through observation — through watching what happens when opportunities are used, not through external knowledge or a lucky guess. If the rule cannot be stated in one sentence after the encounter, it was not discoverable during it.

#### The Randomizer's Tools

**Opportunity Generation Tools** — the specific mechanism through which apparent resources appear on the battlefield.
*(Examples: at the start of each round, one new apparent resource appears at a random location within the encounter space — a weapon, a consumable, a power enhancement, a terrain feature that seems beneficial; the Randomizer generates opportunities as a bonus action, placing them in positions that are spatially convenient and therefore suspicious; opportunities appear when the Randomizer takes damage, making aggression look like it is producing rewards; opportunities appear at fixed locations throughout the encounter, cycling through a set of predictable types. The generation mechanism should feel abundant — there should always be more opportunities available than the party can comfortably evaluate. Scarcity would invite careful testing; abundance is the pressure.)*

**Discrimination Logic Tools** — the specific rule that separates genuine from false.
*(Examples: genuine opportunities are always placed near hazards, while false ones are always placed in open safe ground — the offer that seems easier to reach is the trap; genuine opportunities always appear after the Randomizer uses a specific ability, while false ones appear on any other trigger; genuine opportunities are always the same color, texture, or scale as each other, with a consistent tell that false ones lack; false opportunities always linger slightly longer than genuine ones — manufactured openings are designed to be noticed, while real ones are brief because they are incidental. The rule must produce a consistent, observable tell. One sentence, every time.)*

**Consequence Tools** — what happens when the party engages with a false opportunity.
*(Examples: a false consumable applies a condition — Poisoned, Frightened, Blinded — instead of its apparent benefit; a false weapon enhancement works for one attack and then backtracks, dealing its bonus damage to the wielder instead; a false terrain feature provides cover for one round and then collapses into difficult terrain; engaging a false opportunity gives the Randomizer a free attack against the creature that engaged it. The consequence must be specific and attributable — the party member who used the wrong resource must be able to trace the bad outcome directly to that choice, not to general bad luck.)*

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Randomizer's action economy is built around **opportunity generation and personal combat pressure** that punishes the party for either engaging indiscriminately or ignoring opportunities entirely. The boss must be genuinely dangerous on its own terms — otherwise the party can simply ignore all opportunities and fight it directly, which collapses the encounter's puzzle into a standard fight.

#### Actions

**The Randomizer should have:**

- An **opportunity seeding action** that places one or more new opportunities on the battlefield, positions them according to the generation logic, and narrates their apparent nature in terms designed to be tempting. This action fires every round and is the encounter's primary puzzle driver — the abundance of apparent resources is maintained by this action's reliability. The narration must be consistent with the tell: a genuine opportunity is described in one way, a false one in another, and those descriptions must be consistent enough that a party tracking the pattern can identify which is which before committing to engage.

- A **primary strike** at or above curve in damage, used against the party member who most recently engaged an opportunity — genuine or false. Engaging any opportunity draws the Randomizer's attention. This is not a punishment for wrong choices specifically; it is a cost of interaction generally. The party must weigh the value of any opportunity against the certainty of drawing a boss attack, which keeps even genuine opportunities from feeling free.

- A **Multiattack** that uses the opportunity seeding action and the primary strike once each. The seeding fires first — new opportunities are placed, the party must evaluate them — and then the primary strike fires against whoever interacted with an opportunity on their last turn. The order communicates the encounter's rhythm: the boss offers, the party responds, the boss punishes the response.

> **Randomizer-specific note:** The primary strike must target the most recent opportunity-engager regardless of whether the engagement was with a genuine or false opportunity. If the primary strike only fires when the party engages false opportunities, it becomes a punishment-for-wrong-choices mechanic, and the party will simply stop engaging entirely. The boss punishes engagement. The party must decide whether the benefit of the opportunity outweighs the certainty of the attack.

#### Bonus Actions

The Randomizer's Bonus Actions are its **pressure maintenance tools** — what ensures the abundance of apparent opportunities feels urgent rather than leisurely, and what makes deliberate observation costlier than it would otherwise be.

- A **consequence escalation bonus action** that advances the effect of any currently active false opportunity by one step — a Poisoned condition worsens, a collapsed terrain feature deals damage to creatures still in it, a weapon enhancement reversal applies its damage again. This ability fires on rounds where a party member engaged a false opportunity and is currently suffering its consequence. The escalation communicates that false opportunities are not merely setbacks — they are ongoing liabilities that compound until addressed. A party member dealing with a false opportunity's consequence is a party member who is not evaluating new ones.

- A **temptation placement bonus action** that places one additional false opportunity — not a genuine one — in the most tactically appealing position available, designed to look maximally attractive. This fires on rounds where the party has successfully identified and avoided false opportunities, responding to correct play by making the next false opportunity harder to resist. It should be limited in uses — the Randomizer cannot bait indefinitely — so the party can see the baiting budget depleting.

> **On the temptation placement bonus action:** This is the encounter's clearest expression of the Randomizer as an intelligent adversary rather than a random system. When it fires, the narration must communicate deliberateness — the opportunity that appears is placed, not generated, and its placement is specifically aimed at the party member who has been most disciplined about avoiding false opportunities. The most attentive player is the primary target, because their attention is the greatest threat to the Randomizer's logic.

#### Reactions

The Randomizer has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the Pattern Strength is most directly expressed — the moments where engaging before decoding produces the sharpest, most attributable consequences.

**Required:**

- **A false trigger reaction** *(trigger: a creature engages a false opportunity — uses it, picks it up, or activates it).* The Randomizer immediately applies the false opportunity's consequence to that creature and makes one primary strike against them at advantage. The party member who took the bait suffers both the consequence of the false opportunity and the Randomizer's personal attention simultaneously. This double consequence is the Pattern Strength's mechanical expression: engaging before decoding costs twice, from two directions at once.

- **A genuine suppression reaction** *(trigger: a creature successfully uses a genuine opportunity — gains its benefit without triggering a consequence).* The Randomizer immediately removes one genuine opportunity from the battlefield and replaces it with a false one at the same location. Using a genuine opportunity correctly makes the field slightly more dangerous — the boss responds to correct engagement by degrading the quality of what remains. The party cannot simply find the genuine opportunities and use them repeatedly; each correct engagement makes the next correct engagement harder to find.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A pattern disruption** *(trigger: a creature makes a skill check or uses an ability to investigate the logic distinguishing genuine from false opportunities).* The Randomizer immediately shuffles the positions of all active opportunities — genuine and false swap locations — and the tell shifts by one register. The logic does not change, but its surface expression does. Active investigation draws active disruption. The party must decode the Randomizer through engagement and observation, not through methodical analysis. This option works best for Randomizers with an adversarial intelligence — one that notices when it is being studied.
- **A consequence transfer** *(trigger: a creature attempts to remove or end a consequence imposed by a false opportunity through a healing ability, condition-removal spell, or similar effect).* The Randomizer immediately transfers the consequence to the creature nearest to the one attempting removal. The false opportunity's effect cannot simply be healed away — removing it redistributes it. This option makes false engagement a longer-lasting problem and raises the stakes of discrimination.
- **A manufactured gratitude** *(trigger: a creature engages the same genuine opportunity type for the second time in the encounter).* The Randomizer immediately turns that opportunity into a false one mid-engagement — the resource that was real has become a trap between when the creature committed to using it and when the use resolved. A party that finds a genuine opportunity and returns to the same type will discover that its reliability was temporary. This option works best for Randomizers with a deeply predatory identity — one that specifically targets learned behavior.

> **On the genuine suppression reaction:** This is the Randomizer's most important reaction for maintaining the Pattern Strength after the party has started decoding the logic. Without it, a party that identifies genuine opportunities would simply use them repeatedly until the encounter ends. With it, each correct engagement degrades the field — making the Pace Weakness accessible only to parties that decode the rule completely, not parties that identify one genuine opportunity and farm it.

---

### 4. The Opportunity Logic — Building the Discrimination Problem

The opportunity logic is the encounter's puzzle engine. Before any stat block entry is written, the DM must be able to state the distinguishing rule in one sentence, verify that every opportunity generated during the encounter is consistent with it, and confirm that the tell is present and observable from round one.

**The opportunity logic must have:**

**A single, statable rule.** "Genuine opportunities always appear adjacent to the most recent hazard zone. False ones appear in open ground." "Genuine opportunities always appear after the Randomizer uses a specific ability. False ones appear on any other trigger." One sentence. Every opportunity in the encounter is either genuine or false according to this rule and no other.

**A tell that is observable without engagement.** The party must be able to identify whether an opportunity is genuine or false before committing to it. The tell must be visible from a distance, present from round one, and consistent throughout. A tell that requires touching the opportunity to discover is not a tell — it is a consequence. The party should be able to look at an opportunity and form a hypothesis about its nature without having to find out the hard way.

**A consequence that is attributable.** When a false opportunity is engaged and its consequence fires, the party member who engaged it must be able to trace the consequence directly to that choice. An opportunity consequence that arrives one or two rounds later, or that applies to a different party member than the one who engaged it, breaks the attribution and makes the logic unlearnable. Consequences must be immediate and personal.

**Evidence accumulation that permits deduction within three rounds.** Round one: the party observes one or two opportunities and their outcomes. Round two: the party observes additional opportunities and begins forming a hypothesis. Round three: a party member tests the hypothesis by engaging a predicted genuine opportunity. The hypothesis should be testable — and a correct hypothesis should produce the expected outcome reliably.

---

### 5. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Randomizer's CR calibration follows the standard process with one Randomizer-specific adjustment: **effective difficulty scales with the party's discrimination accuracy, and CR benchmarks must account for an early encounter where many false opportunities are being engaged and a late encounter where none are.**

In the early encounter, false opportunity consequences and boss reactions compound — the party is taking consequences, drawing attacks, and losing resources to indiscriminate engagement. In the late encounter, the party is engaging only genuine opportunities, drawing only the cost-of-interaction primary strike, and accumulating genuine advantages. Calibrate for the middle encounter — partial decoding, some false engagement, some correct engagement.

> **A note on the Randomizer's binary difficulty curve.** The Randomizer has a steeper difficulty cliff than any other Shaper archetype. In most archetypes, partial decoding is useful — knowing two of three Form Shifter forms still improves the party's performance. In the Randomizer, partial understanding of the discrimination logic does not help: a party that suspects the rule but is not certain will still engage false opportunities while testing their hypothesis, and each wrong engagement costs the same regardless of how close the party was to the correct answer. The encounter effectively has two states — undecoded (active harm from false engagement) and decoded (only cost-of-interaction pressure) — with no meaningful middle ground. DMs should account for this when choosing this archetype. The Randomizer is appropriate for parties that enjoy deduction problems and are willing to accept that the early encounter may feel punishing until the rule clicks. It is a poor choice for parties that find puzzle-gating frustrating or for encounters where the DM wants consistent difficulty across the encounter's arc. If the binary curve is a concern, design the discrimination logic to be decodable in round one or two rather than three — the shorter the undecoded phase, the less the binary matters.

**Steps:**

1. **Set AC at baseline, HP at or slightly above baseline.** The Randomizer is not hard to hit — the party's engagement with opportunities is the real resource drain, not the boss's defenses. Slightly above baseline HP reflects that the late encounter, once the logic is decoded, should still require meaningful effort to close out.

2. **Set the primary strike at or above curve.** The cost-of-interaction primary strike must be a genuine threat. If it is weak, the party will engage every opportunity without calculation — the cost is trivial and the puzzle collapses into "use everything and find out." The primary strike must make each engagement a real resource decision.

3. **Design genuine opportunity benefits against the party's expected resource state.** A genuine opportunity that provides negligible benefit is not a genuine opportunity — it is a speed bump that happens not to be a trap. Each genuine opportunity should provide a meaningful advantage: a significant HP restoration, a damage boost that lasts multiple turns, a condition removal, a useful terrain feature. The genuine benefit must be worth the cost-of-interaction primary strike that engaging it will draw.

4. **Design false opportunity consequences against the party's expected durability.** A false opportunity consequence that deals trivial damage is not a consequence — it is a minor setback the party will accept as the cost of exploration. Each false opportunity consequence should impose a real cost: a significant condition, a meaningful HP drain, a resource expenditure. The false consequence must make indiscriminate engagement genuinely uneconomical.

5. **Calibrate the temptation placement bonus action's uses against the encounter's expected length.** Two to three uses is appropriate for most encounters — enough to fire on the rounds where the party first shows signs of decoding the logic, not enough to make every round of correct play immediately punished. Once exhausted, the field becomes increasingly clean as false opportunities are identified and avoided.

6. **Design the tell's visibility against the party's expected attention level.** A tell that requires a Perception check is inaccessible to players who are also managing combat. A tell that is narrated consistently in the opportunity seeding action's description is accessible to everyone. Default to narration-level tells — something the DM says every time they describe a genuine opportunity, and something different every time they describe a false one. The tell should be learnable through listening, not through rolling.

---

### The Randomizer's Tell

Every Randomizer encounter must have one — for the discrimination logic:

> *"In this encounter, genuine opportunities [specific observable characteristic — always appear in a specific location, always follow a specific trigger, always share a specific described quality]. False opportunities [specific observable characteristic — always appear differently, always follow a different trigger, always lack the specific quality]. A party member who [observes two or three opportunity placements without engaging] will have enough information to identify the rule. Players who [correctly apply the rule and engage only genuine opportunities] will find the encounter's pace becoming manageable — each decision that was previously a deliberation is now instant, and the tempo that felt overwhelming resolves into a rhythm they are running ahead of."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the discrimination logic has no observable tell — the distinction between genuine and false is invisible until after engagement, which makes the puzzle a trap rather than a puzzle. Go back to the opportunity logic and design a tell that is present before commitment. The Randomizer's power is in the party not knowing which offer to take. The DM's responsibility is to ensure the answer is always there — visible, consistent, and waiting to be noticed.

## The Adapter

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the party's own tactics are building the battlefield against them.

> **A note on classification.** The source archetypes document classifies the Adapter as Cascading — the flux is the system of change itself. This template treats it as a reactive Environmental archetype, because the terrain is the primary puzzle element and the party's movement and positioning are the central skills being tested. Both framings are defensible. What distinguishes the Adapter from both categories is that its terrain is *generated by the party*, not authored in advance (Environmental) and not following an internal logic the party must decode (Cascading pure). It sits at the intersection: the system of change is Cascading in character, but the expression is Environmental in form. DMs should treat it as the most cognitively demanding Environmental archetype — closer to the Transformer than to the Dynamic, but requiring the pattern-decoding work of a Cascading encounter because the pattern being decoded is the Adapter's response table, not a terrain cycle. Every effective approach they use teaches the Adapter something, and the Adapter uses that lesson to close off the angle that just worked. The puzzle is not finding a tactic that works — it is finding a sequence of actions that engineers the configuration the party actually wants to fight in.**

---

### 1. High Concept

An Adapter's high concept must communicate reactive intelligence — a boss that does not simply resist the party's tactics but specifically reconfigures the space in response to them. The Adapter is not hard because it has high defenses. It is hard because the party's competence is continuously generating new problems, and the more effectively they fight, the faster those problems arrive.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what does the Adapter learn from each tactic the party uses, and how does that learning manifest as terrain?* The reconfiguration should feel like an intelligent answer — not a random hazard that happens to appear, but a specific response to a specific input that a watching party can attribute to their own choices. The Adapter is not unpredictable. It is predictable in exactly the wrong direction.

**Strong examples:** "The Living Battlefield That Reads Your Moves," "The Strategist Who Reconfigures the Room Around Your Tactics," "He Who Turns Your Strengths Into the Next Problem," "The Mirror That Fights Back With Your Own Reflection."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Adapts to Everything," "Always Has an Answer," "Reactive Boss." These describe the party's experience of frustration, not the encounter's identity. The high concept should communicate *what the Adapter is responding to* — the specific input-output relationship between party tactics and terrain changes is what makes the encounter feel like an interaction with an intelligent system rather than a punishment engine.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Adapter template asks two questions simultaneously: **what triggers reconfiguration, and what configuration does each trigger produce?**

Both must be fully mapped before any stat block entry is written. The trigger-response table is the encounter's puzzle board. Every trigger must produce a predictable, consistent response. Every response must be attributable to a specific trigger. If the party cannot reverse-engineer the table from observed play, the Adapter is not a puzzle — it is a reactive hazard generator.

#### The Adapter's Tools

**Trigger Tools** — the specific party actions that generate terrain reconfigurations.
*(Examples: a ranged attack against the Adapter triggers walls or obstacles that interrupt lines of sight; a spell that deals area damage triggers the appearance of difficult terrain within the affected zone; a melee attack that lands cleanly triggers forced movement effects that push the attacker away from engagement; a condition successfully imposed on the Adapter triggers a terrain change that removes the condition's tactical benefit — blinding the Adapter summons darkness that blinds everyone, restraining it generates terrain that also restrains nearby creatures. Each trigger must be a specific, observable party action — not "dealing damage" generally, but "dealing fire damage" or "attacking from more than 30 feet away." The specificity is what makes the pattern learnable.)*

**Response Tools** — the specific terrain changes each trigger produces.
*(Examples: ranged attack trigger produces a wall of force segment at the point of origin; spell damage trigger produces a residual hazard zone at the spell's impact point; melee hit trigger produces a repulsion effect originating from the Adapter's position; condition trigger produces a symmetrical condition applied to the surrounding area. The response must be a concrete terrain change — a placed zone, a moved obstacle, a changed spatial feature — not an abstract effect. The party needs to see the terrain change happen and be able to map it back to the trigger.)*

**Limit Tools** — what constrains the Adapter's reconfiguration capacity.
*(Examples: a finite number of reconfiguration charges, each trigger consuming one; a cooldown that prevents the same trigger from generating a response twice in succession; a maximum number of simultaneous active terrain changes, beyond which new responses replace old ones rather than adding; a specific condition on the Adapter that suppresses its reconfiguration ability for one round when applied. The limits are critical — the party must believe the Adapter has a ceiling, and that ceiling is what makes engineering a desired configuration a viable strategy.)*

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Adapter's action economy is built around **maintaining the reconfiguration system and personal combat pressure** that ensures the party cannot simply ignore the terrain and fight the boss directly.

#### Actions

**The Adapter should have:**

- A **reconfiguration action** that either places a new terrain change in response to the last party trigger that has not yet been answered, or advances an existing terrain change one step further — extending a wall, deepening a hazard zone, increasing the severity of a repositioning effect. The narration must make the causal link explicit: "as you step back from your ranged shot, the air between you and the Adapter hardens — a barrier rising along the exact line you attacked from."

- A **primary strike** at or above curve in damage, used against the party member whose most recent action generated the most tactically significant terrain change. The Adapter is not simply reactive — it also punishes the party member whose actions have been most disruptive to its intended configuration.

- A **Multiattack** that uses the reconfiguration action and the primary strike once each. The reconfiguration fires first — the Adapter processes the last trigger and adjusts the terrain — then strikes the party member whose action caused it. Every turn is a demonstration of the input-output logic the party must decode.

> **Adapter-specific note:** On rounds where no party member has taken an action within the Adapter's trigger categories — a round of complete tactical neutrality — the Adapter uses its primary strike twice instead of its Multiattack. The absence of triggers is not safe for the party: the boss is more personally dangerous when it has nothing to respond to. This prevents passive play and ensures the party must engage with the terrain system.

#### Bonus Actions

The Adapter's Bonus Actions are its **configuration reinforcement and limit expression tools** — what deepens existing terrain changes and makes the reconfiguration charge count visible.

- A **configuration deepening bonus action** that intensifies one active terrain change without adding a new one — the existing wall grows taller, the existing hazard zone deals more damage, the existing difficult terrain becomes impassable. This fires on rounds where the Adapter processed a trigger and wants to signal that this terrain change is important — it is investing additional resources in a configuration it built in response to a specific tactic.

- A **charge display bonus action** that fires every round and makes the Adapter's remaining reconfiguration capacity visible through a physical change in the boss's appearance or a narrated shift in the encounter's spatial tension. The party must be able to track how many more reconfigurations the Adapter can generate. When the charges run low, the party knows engineering a desired configuration is now viable. When they run out, the Adapter is committed to whatever terrain it has built.

> **On the charge display bonus action:** This is the encounter's most important pacing tool. A party that cannot see the charge count does not know whether engineering a configuration is currently possible. Make the charge display explicit, consistent, and narratively integrated — something the boss does or shows that the party can track without a separate counter.

#### Reactions

The Adapter has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the Vulnerability Strength is most directly expressed — the moments where terrain generated by the party's previous actions closes off their current offensive options mid-action.

**Required:**

- **An immediate reconfiguration reaction** *(trigger: a creature uses a tactic that matches one of the Adapter's trigger categories for the first time this round).* The Adapter immediately generates the associated terrain change, placing it before the triggering action fully resolves. The wall appears before the ranged attack clears its path. The repulsion effect fires before the melee attacker reaches striking distance. The terrain change applies during the action rather than after — the tactic that would have worked instead finds its path already altered. This fires once per round on the first new trigger each round.

- **A reconfiguration reinforcement reaction** *(trigger: a creature attempts to use a tactic that would benefit from or exploit a terrain configuration the Adapter generated in a previous round).* The Adapter immediately modifies that terrain feature in the direction that most disadvantages the creature's intended use. The cover that was generated by a previous reconfiguration shifts to block the creature trying to use it. The terrain the Adapter built cannot be repurposed by the party — they must use a different approach entirely.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A mirror reconfiguration** *(trigger: a creature moves to the position that would give them the best tactical advantage in the current terrain configuration).* The Adapter immediately reconfigures the terrain to make that position the worst instead — the cover disappears, the hazard zone shifts into it, the forced movement effect now originates from there. The optimal position becomes the worst the instant the party identifies and occupies it. This option works best for Adapters with a deeply adversarial intelligence specifically competing for spatial control.
- **A trigger echo** *(trigger: a creature uses a tactic that matches a trigger category the Adapter has already responded to this encounter).* The Adapter immediately generates an additional minor terrain change of the same type as the original response — adding to it rather than repeating it. Repetition compounds rather than normalizing. This makes tactical diversity a structural necessity: the party cannot settle into any single approach without watching the terrain response to it accumulate.
- **A delayed echo** *(trigger: a creature uses a tactic that matches a trigger category the Adapter has not responded to in the last two rounds — a category it appeared to have stopped reacting to).* The Adapter immediately generates both the standard terrain response for that category and an additional terrain change of the same type in a second location. The category that seemed dormant was not — the Adapter was holding its response, and the party member who assumed the lull meant safety now finds the response doubled. This option works best for Adapters with a patient, deliberate identity — one that lets patterns appear to fade before reasserting them more forcefully.

> **On the immediate reconfiguration reaction:** This fires during the triggering action — before it resolves — which means the party member who initiated a tactic experiences the terrain change as part of committing to it, not as a consequence afterward. The wall rising in front of the archer as they loose the arrow, not after the arrow misses, is a fundamentally different experience. The former teaches that the tactic is being actively countered; the latter teaches only that it did not work.

---

### 4. The Reconfiguration Logic — Building the Pattern Problem

The reconfiguration logic is the encounter's puzzle engine. Before any stat block entry is written, the DM must be able to map every trigger to its associated response, verify that every terrain change in the encounter is generated by a specific party action, and confirm that each trigger-response pair is observable and attributable from round one.

**The reconfiguration logic must have:**

**A complete trigger-response table.** Every trigger category the Adapter responds to must have a specific, consistent terrain response mapped to it before the encounter begins. A trigger that produces different responses on different rounds is not a pattern — it is randomness with extra steps.

**A visible trigger-response connection.** The party must be able to observe the terrain change and attribute it to a specific action without being told. The reconfiguration action's narration is the primary attribution tool — it must describe the terrain change in terms that reference what just happened. "The space hardens where your arrow flew" is attribution. "A wall appears" is not.

**A finite charge count that is tracked visibly.** The charge count determines when deliberate trigger management becomes viable. A useful baseline: charges equal to two per trigger category plus two extra. A three-category Adapter should have approximately eight charges.

**A desired configuration the party can engineer.** The party's endgame is not avoiding triggers — it is engineering a specific configuration by deliberately triggering specific categories in a specific order. That target configuration must exist before the session begins: a terrain state that is genuinely favorable to the party and achievable through deliberate trigger management. If no such configuration exists, the Pattern Weakness cannot fire and the encounter has no resolution beyond depleting HP in an increasingly hostile space.

---

### 5. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Adapter's CR calibration follows the standard process with one Adapter-specific adjustment: **effective difficulty scales inversely with the party's pattern-decoding progress, more dramatically than any other Shaper archetype — because the party's tactical choices are the source of both the terrain problems and the terrain solutions.**

**Steps:**

1. **Set AC at baseline, HP above baseline.** The Adapter is not hard to hit — its terrain changes are the real durability. Above-baseline HP reflects that the engineered configuration phase should still require sustained effort.

2. **Set the primary strike at or above curve.** Tactical neutrality — doing nothing that triggers reconfiguration — must not be viable. The double primary strike on neutral rounds must hurt. Otherwise the party avoids all triggers and fights a standard boss fight.

3. **Design trigger categories to include the party's most reliable tactics.** Triggers that the party never invokes produce no terrain changes and no puzzle. Build the trigger table around the specific party composition — knowing their most common approaches and designing the triggers to capture at least two of them.

4. **Design responses to close off the triggering approach without eliminating all alternatives.** Each terrain response should make the triggering approach less viable going forward without completely preventing all party action. A wall blocking ranged sightlines should still leave melee possible. A repulsion effect pushing melee attackers away should still leave ranged options open.

5. **Set the charge count using the two-per-category-plus-two baseline.** Adjust based on the party's expected tactical variety: a diverse party spreads triggers across categories and potentially exhausts charges faster; a less diverse party repeats the same categories, building denser terrain in fewer areas.

6. **Design the target configuration explicitly before the encounter.** Know, before the session begins, what configuration the party should engineer and how they achieve it. The target configuration is the encounter's resolution condition. If the DM has not identified this before the session, the encounter cannot resolve through the Pattern Weakness.

---

### The Adapter's Tell

Every Adapter encounter must have one — for the reconfiguration logic:

> *"This Adapter responds to [specific trigger categories] by [specific terrain responses]. The party can identify the trigger-response relationships by [specific observable connection — the narration always attributes the terrain change to the triggering action, the terrain change always appears adjacent to or related to the trigger's location, the response always involves the same terrain type as the trigger's tactical category]. Players who [deliberately manage their trigger use to engineer a favorable configuration] will find the Adapter committed to terrain that serves the party rather than the boss — and fighting in a space it built against itself."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the reconfiguration logic has no observable attributions — the terrain changes appear but cannot be traced to specific party actions, which makes the pattern unlearnable and the encounter unsolvable through deliberate play. Go back to the trigger-response table and build narration for each pair that makes the connection explicit. The Adapter's power is in turning the party's choices against them. The DM's responsibility is to ensure those choices are always visible — so the party can start choosing differently.
