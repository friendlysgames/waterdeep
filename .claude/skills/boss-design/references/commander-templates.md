# Commander Templates

---

## The Warlord

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **there are too many meaningful targets on the field, each one enabling a different problem, and the party must decide which one to kill first while every round of indecision makes the answer harder to find.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A Warlord's high concept must communicate military authority — the sense of a commander whose power is expressed through the quality and coordination of what it has deployed, not solely through its own combat capability. The Warlord is not the most dangerous thing on the field. It is the reason everything else on the field is dangerous.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what does this commander's field look like, and how does it reflect the commander?* A Warlord's forces are an extension of its personality, doctrine, and purpose. A warlord general commands soldiers in coordinated formations. A vampire lord commands undead specialists with distinct and complementary functions. A crime boss commands lieutenants who each own a different piece of the operation. The field is the character — the high concept should evoke both.

**Strong examples:** "The Iron Strategos of the Burning Vanguard," "The Pale Throne at the Center of the Storm," "She Who Commands From the Rearguard," "The Architect of the Final Assault."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Boss With Minions," "Enemy Commander," "Supported Boss." These describe role, not identity. The high concept should communicate *what kind of commander this is* — the doctrine, the style, the logic of how the forces are arrayed. The field should feel authored, not assembled.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Warlord template asks two questions simultaneously: **what does the Warlord do, and what does the field do?** These cannot be designed independently. The Warlord's stat block and the field units must be developed together, because the puzzle lives in their interaction.

#### The Warlord's Tools

Organize the Warlord's own tools into three categories:

**Command Tools** — what allows the Warlord to direct and amplify the field.
*(Examples: a bonus action that grants a unit an immediate additional attack; a reaction that redirects damage from the Warlord to a nearby unit; a trait that grants all units within 60 feet advantage on their first attack each round; a legendary action that orders a unit to move and act outside its normal turn. Command tools are what make the Warlord a Commander rather than just a boss with bodyguards — without them, the Warlord and its units are simply co-combatants.)*

**Personal Combat Tools** — what the Warlord contributes as a direct combatant.
*(Examples: a primary strike that is at or above curve, representing the Warlord's personal lethality; an area attack that punishes the party for clustering; a defensive ability that keeps the Warlord functional while its units are being eliminated. The Warlord should be genuinely dangerous in its own right — a party that focuses the Warlord and ignores the field should feel the cost of that choice. But the Warlord's personal combat output should not so dominate the encounter that the field becomes irrelevant.)*

**Adaptation Tools** — what the Warlord does when its units start to fall.
*(Examples: a reaction that calls in a replacement unit when one falls; a trait that grants the Warlord a damage bonus for each unit that has been eliminated; a phase transition that changes the Warlord's tactics when its field drops below a specific threshold. Adaptation tools are what give multi-phase Warlords their second and third phases — the encounter does not end when the party finds the kill order, it escalates.)*

#### The Field's Tools

Organize the field units into the Warlord's Targeting problem. Each unit should have:

**A primary function** — the specific problem it enables if left alive.
*(Examples: a healer that restores the Warlord's HP at the start of each round; an artillery unit that deals persistent area damage from a fixed position; a suppressor that imposes disadvantage on attacks against the Warlord; a charger that locks down a party member with a grapple or condition; a buffer that grants the Warlord or other units a stacking bonus each round it survives. Each primary function must create a specific, predictable cost for ignoring it.)*

**A secondary function** — what the unit contributes beyond its primary function.
*(Secondary functions create the texture of the encounter beyond the targeting puzzle. Examples: a healer that also applies a poison condition on melee attacks; an artillery unit that, when killed, deals area damage to creatures adjacent to it; a buffer that imposes a brief Stunned condition on whoever strikes the killing blow. Secondary functions should not overshadow the primary function but should make the field feel like a living system rather than a list of targets with HP.)*

**A legibility signal** — how the party identifies the unit's function within the first two rounds.
*(A legibility signal is not a visual effect — it is a behavior the DM describes and the party can attribute a function to through observation. The healer is the unit the Warlord looks toward when it takes damage; the suppressor is the unit that stays within arm's reach of the Warlord at all times; the artillery unit is the one that hangs back and never advances into melee. These are things the DM narrates and the players notice. If a unit's function can only be identified after it has used its ability once, that is also a valid legibility signal — but the ability should fire in round one so the party has the information they need before round two's decisions.)*

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Warlord's action economy is built around **command amplification on the boss's turn and personal lethality as a reliable secondary threat**. The field units handle the ongoing pressure; the Warlord's actions calibrate and accelerate that pressure.

#### Actions

**The Warlord should have:**

- A **primary strike** that is at or above curve in damage, used against the party member who has most aggressively targeted the Warlord's units. This attack should impose a condition that punishes target switching — Frightened, a debuff that imposes disadvantage on attacks against units, or forced movement that pushes the attacker away from the unit they just killed. The strike says: *eliminating my forces costs you, and I will make sure you feel that cost personally.*

- A **command strike** that is lower in direct damage but amplifies the field — it may order a unit to make an immediate attack, refresh a unit's special ability, or grant a unit temporary HP that keeps it functional for one more round. This ability blurs the line between the Warlord's offensive turn and its command function, reinforcing that every action the Warlord takes serves the field.

- A **Multiattack** that lets the Warlord use the primary strike and the command strike once each, or use the primary strike twice. The Warlord should prefer the mixed Multiattack when units are alive and functional — its identity is command, not pure personal combat. When units are gone, it should shift to the double primary strike, expressing the phase transition into a more personally dangerous combatant.

> **Warlord-specific note:** The Warlord's personal stat block should be roughly one CR tier below what it would be as a solo encounter at the same difficulty. The field units carry a meaningful portion of the encounter's threat, and a Warlord whose personal combat is balanced as a solo monster will feel overwhelming combined with a functional field. The field is part of the challenge rating calculation, not a bonus on top of it.

#### Bonus Actions

The Warlord's Bonus Actions are its **field management tools** — what it uses to sustain, redirect, and reinforce its forces between its main action and its reactions.

- A **field order bonus action** that issues a standing command to all units within 60 feet: move toward a specific target, focus attacks on the most isolated party member, or fall back to a defensive position around the Warlord. This ability does not require individual units to take actions — it shifts their behavior for the remainder of the round, affecting their positioning and target selection. The order should be characterful: the Warlord shouts a command, signals with a weapon, gestures toward a target. The units visibly respond by moving or reorienting.

- A **rapid reinforcement bonus action** that is limited in uses — typically 2–3 per encounter — and either calls in a new unit from a predetermined entry point or restores a fallen unit to partial HP. This ability is the Escalation Strength made active: the party eliminates a unit and the Warlord replaces it, resetting the targeting problem. The replacement must arrive from somewhere logical and pre-established — a door, a tunnel entrance, a summoning circle described in the encounter setup — so the party can anticipate and potentially move to deny future reinforcements.

> **On rapid reinforcement:** This is the most dangerous tool in the Warlord's kit and the most likely to make an encounter feel unfair if overused. The limited uses are critical — the party must believe that killing units is meaningful progress, even if the first elimination is immediately answered by a replacement. After the last reinforcement use is expended, every unit killed is permanent progress, and the party should feel the field shrinking.

#### Reactions

The Warlord has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the command identity is most directly expressed — the Warlord protecting its forces, punishing the party for targeting them, and sustaining its position when threatened.

**Required:**

- **A command intercept reaction** *(trigger: a unit within 60 feet of the Warlord would be reduced to 0 HP by an attack).* The Warlord expends a reaction to grant that unit temporary HP equal to the killing blow's damage, keeping it at 1 HP instead. Alternatively, the Warlord orders a different unit to interpose, taking the damage on behalf of the target unit. This reaction communicates two things: that the Warlord is watching the entire field, and that eliminating units requires overcoming the Warlord's active protection — the party cannot simply burst one unit down without accounting for this response.

- **A retaliatory order reaction** *(trigger: a creature reduces one of the Warlord's units to 0 HP).* The Warlord immediately orders all surviving units within 60 feet to move up to half their speed toward the creature that dealt the killing blow, and each unit that can reach that creature makes one attack against it. This reaction ensures that killing units has a cost — the party member who eliminates the artillery unit draws the immediate attention of every other unit on the field. The cost is real, and it is the primary mechanical expression of the Escalation Strength: letting units survive is sometimes safer than killing them in the wrong order.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A warlord's ward** *(trigger: the Warlord takes damage from a melee attack while at least one unit is within 5 feet of the attacker).* One of those units immediately makes one attack against the attacker. The Warlord is protected by proximity to its forces — engaging it directly while ignoring its bodyguards is actively dangerous.
- **A tactical withdrawal** *(trigger: the Warlord is reduced below half HP for the first time).* The Warlord moves up to its speed without provoking opportunity attacks and issues a standing order that changes all surviving units' behavior — they become more aggressive, collapse into a tighter defensive formation, or begin pursuing a secondary objective. This reaction signals the phase transition and buys the Warlord a moment to reposition while the party processes the behavioral shift.
- **A last command** *(trigger: the Warlord is reduced to 0 HP).* Before falling, the Warlord issues one final order: all surviving units gain advantage on all attacks until the end of their next turn, or they immediately move to surround a specific party member, or one designated unit uses its most powerful ability immediately. The Warlord's death is not the end of the encounter — it is the signal that the encounter's last phase has begun.

> **On the retaliatory order reaction:** This is the Warlord's most important reaction for communicating the Escalation Strength and the Targeting puzzle simultaneously. Every time it fires, it must do two things: punish the party for the kill they just made, and teach them that kill order matters. The narration should make the cause-and-effect immediate and specific — "the Warlord's head snaps toward you as the archer falls, and its voice carries the command across the field." Players who hear that command know they have drawn the field's attention, and they must decide whether to stand their ground or pass the pressure to an ally.

> **On the command intercept and retaliatory order stacking:** Used together reflexively, these two reactions compound into a reliable double cost on every unit kill — command intercept forces a second attack to finish the unit, and retaliatory order fires on that second kill. At high unit counts this becomes prohibitively expensive. Use command intercept selectively: protect units whose survival matters strategically (the unit the party has not yet noticed, the unit in the correct kill position) rather than reflexively on every threatened unit. The reactions should feel like the Warlord playing the field intelligently, not like a tax on every action the party takes.

### 4. The Field — Building the Targeting Problem

The field is the encounter's puzzle board. Every unit the Warlord commands is a question the party must answer: *do we kill this now, or can we afford to let it act one more round?* A field that does not pose meaningful targeting questions is not a Warlord encounter — it is a boss fight with decorative enemies.

**The Targeting problem must have:**

A **correct kill order** that is discoverable through play. There must be a sequence in which the units are most efficiently eliminated, and that sequence must be readable from the field's observed behavior. Players do not need to find the optimal order on round one — they need to form a hypothesis by round two and test it by round three.

A **cost for incorrect kill order** that is specific and attributable. When the party kills units in the wrong sequence, the consequences must be legible: the healer tops off the Warlord before being eliminated, so the party must fight through those restored HP. The artillery unit applies another round of its debuff before falling. The suppressor keeps the party at disadvantage for one more crucial round. The cost must be *connected to the specific unit left alive*, not generic "the fight got harder."

A **minimum of three distinct units** with meaningfully different primary functions. Two units create a binary — kill A or kill B — which is a decision but not a puzzle. Three or more create a genuine triage problem: the party must rank all three, and the correct ranking is not immediately obvious.

A **maximum field complexity appropriate to the encounter's CR**. As CR increases, the field can sustain more units with more complex functions. At lower CRs, three units with clear functions is the right ceiling. At higher CRs, five or six units with interlocking functions — where killing one changes the optimal order for the others — is appropriate.

**Recommended field compositions by encounter complexity:**

- **Standard (3 units):** One support unit (healer or buffer), one ranged threat (artillery or suppressor), one melee threat (charger or lockdown). Kill order is typically ranged threat first (easiest to isolate and most sustained in its damage contribution), melee threat second (most immediately dangerous once the suppressor is gone), support unit last (now has nothing to protect and can be finished at leisure). This is the Warlord's cleanest expression and the best starting point for DMs new to the archetype.

- **Intermediate (4–5 units):** Add one additional unit whose function disrupts the standard kill order — a second melee threat that protects the support unit, a unit with a secondary function that changes if it is the last to be killed, or a unit whose elimination triggers a behavior change in the remaining units. Intermediate compositions reward the party for remembering and adapting to what they observe, not just for executing a standard priority pattern.

- **Advanced (5–6 units):** All units have interlocking secondary functions. The kill order affects not just the difficulty of subsequent eliminations but the nature of the Warlord's threat in the encounter's final phase. Advanced compositions are appropriate for climactic encounters where the party has had meaningful preparation time and the DM has designed the field with full intentionality.

### 5. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Warlord's CR calibration differs from Tyrant archetypes in one fundamental way: **the CR is the sum of the Warlord and the field, not the Warlord alone.**

This is the most common error in Commander encounter design: building a Warlord boss at the intended final CR, then adding field units on top. The result is an encounter that runs two to three difficulty tiers above its nominal CR. The Warlord's personal stat block should be calibrated assuming the field is functional, and the field units' combined threat should account for a meaningful portion of the encounter's total challenge.

**Steps:**

1. **Allocate the field's threat budget.** Typically, field units should account for 40–60% of the encounter's total challenge. A Warlord encounter where the boss is 80% of the challenge and the field is 20% will feel like a boss fight with cosmetic minions. Conversely, a Warlord encounter where the field is 80% of the challenge and the boss is a 20% cleanup will feel anticlimactic when the boss falls.

2. **Set the Warlord's personal AC above baseline, HP at baseline.** The Warlord is a priority target the party may attempt to focus immediately. High AC makes ignoring the field and rushing the boss feel appropriately costly — the Warlord can sustain that pressure for a few rounds. HP at baseline means it falls reasonably quickly once the field is gone and the party commits.

3. **Set the Warlord's primary strike at or above curve, command strike below curve in direct damage.** The Warlord hits hard when it attacks personally — this communicates its own lethality. The command strike's value is in field amplification, not in its direct damage contribution.

4. **Assign field unit HP and AC at approximately 50–60% of the Warlord's values.** Units should be meaningful targets that require real effort to eliminate, not speed bumps that fall to a single attack. But they should not have enough HP to absorb sustained focus from the whole party — a unit should fall in 1–2 focused rounds, not 4–5.

5. **Calibrate the reinforcement ability against the expected encounter length.** If the encounter runs 5–6 rounds, 2–3 reinforcement uses is appropriate — enough to meaningfully challenge the party's kill order, not enough to make elimination feel pointless. If the encounter runs 3–4 rounds, 1–2 uses. Reinforce the same unit type the party has already killed when possible — this teaches the party that the reinforcement has a predictable source and can be anticipated.

### The Warlord's Tell

> **A note on Tell complexity across Commander archetypes.** The Tell requirement varies significantly in what it demands. The Warlord's Tell is a single sentence — a completion check that confirms a design decision already made. The Ritualist's Tell is four sentences that together constitute a full encounter pre-design commitment: if you cannot complete them, the encounter is not designed yet. A DM who treats the Ritualist's Tell as a final check rather than a design commitment will arrive at the table with an incomplete encounter. When a Tell has multiple required sentences, treat it as the design requirement, not as the final step.

Every Warlord encounter must have one. Write it in plain language before finalizing any stat block:

> *"This Warlord's field has a correct kill order. The [specific unit] enables [specific problem] that compounds every round it survives. Players who [identify and execute the correct kill order] will find the remaining threats naturally clustering, and the final fight simplifying into something they can win decisively."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the Targeting puzzle has no solution — the field is a collection of threats, not a prioritization problem. Go back to the field design and ensure each unit has a specific, attributable cost for surviving and a specific, attributable benefit for being eliminated in the right order. The field must be readable through the DM's narration and the units' observed behavior. Players need to be able to form a hypothesis about kill order by round two.

## The Puppet Master

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **not everything the party can see actually matters, and the things that do matter are not the ones that look most dangerous. The puzzle is reading the hierarchy — and reading it fast enough, because the field is hurting the party while they think.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A Puppet Master's high concept must communicate concealed control — the sense of a figure whose power is not expressed directly but through a web of relationships, dependencies, and hidden authority. The Puppet Master may or may not be the most dangerous combatant on the field. It is certainly the one whose removal changes everything — but the party may not be able to see that until they understand what the field actually is.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what is the Puppet Master hiding, and what does it look like from the outside?* The concealment is the identity. A mind flayer whose thralls appear to be acting independently. A hag matriarch whose coven operates through proxies. A spymaster whose network of agents looks like a random assortment of fighters until the party realizes they are all receiving silent instruction. The surface appearance and the underlying reality must both be present in the high concept.

**Strong examples:** "The Unseen Architect of the Amber Court," "She Whose Hands Are Always Someone Else's," "The Voice Behind the Masks," "The Mind That Wears Twelve Bodies."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Mind Controller Boss," "Hidden Boss," "Boss With Secret Minions." These describe mechanics, not identity. The high concept should communicate *what the Puppet Master's control looks like from the outside*, because that surface appearance is what the party spends the first part of the encounter misreading.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Puppet Master template asks two questions simultaneously: **what is the real hierarchy, and what does the field look like before the party understands it?**

These two pictures must be designed together. The surface appearance — what the party sees in round one — and the underlying reality — what the party deduces through engagement — must both be present in the encounter design before a single stat block entry is written.

#### The Puppet Master's Tools

**Control Tools** — what maintains the Puppet Master's authority over the field.
*(Examples: a trait that grants all controlled nodes advantage on attacks while the Puppet Master is conscious; a bonus action that issues a silent command, repositioning a node or granting it an immediate reaction; a reaction that redirects an attack intended for the Puppet Master to a controlled node; a sustained aura or ability that the Puppet Master must maintain each round or lose control of a node. Control tools are what make the Puppet Master a Commander rather than just a boss with bodyguards — the nodes' behavior is visibly governed by the boss's choices, not operating independently.)*

**Concealment Tools** — what obscures the hierarchy's true structure.
*(Examples: nodes that behave as though independently motivated, with their own apparent goals; a field arrangement that puts visually impressive but strategically secondary nodes in prominent positions while the critical nodes appear incidental; communication between the Puppet Master and its nodes that is not visible to the party — silent telepathy, written commands, pre-established signals. Concealment tools are what create the epistemic puzzle — the party is misreading a social and tactical structure that has been deliberately designed to be misread.)*

**Escalation Tools** — what the Puppet Master activates when the party starts finding the right nodes.
*(Examples: a reserve node that was not visible on the field and becomes active when a key node falls; a behavior change in surviving nodes when their hierarchy is disrupted — they become more dangerous, more erratic, or begin pursuing a previously secondary objective; the Puppet Master personally entering the fight in response to progress, revealing capabilities it had concealed while its field was intact. Escalation tools are the Division Strength made active — the party's success triggers consequences that demand additional attention.)*

#### The Field's Tools

The Puppet Master's field differs from the Warlord's in one critical way: **the nodes are not equally important, and that inequality is not immediately visible.**

Each node should have:

**A surface role** — what the node appears to be doing from the party's initial perspective.
*(Examples: a large armored fighter that appears to be the field's primary melee threat; a robed figure hanging back that appears to be providing magical support; a fast-moving skirmisher that appears to be targeting the squishiest party members. The surface role is designed to mislead — or at least to not immediately reveal the node's actual importance in the hierarchy.)*

**A structural role** — what the node is actually doing in the Puppet Master's hierarchy.
*(Examples: the large armored fighter is actually the communication anchor — while it stands, the Puppet Master can issue commands silently without being traceable; the robed figure is a decoy whose only actual function is to absorb targeted spells; the fast skirmisher is the real control node, whose continued presence grants all other nodes a stacking attack bonus each round it survives. The structural role is the answer to the targeting puzzle — it is what makes this node matter, or not, to the hierarchy.)*

**A discovery tell** — how the party can deduce the structural role through engagement.
*(Not a visible effect or a glowing marker — a behavior the DM narrates and the players can attribute meaning to. The Puppet Master looks toward a specific node when it gives commands. The robed figure never uses its most powerful ability even when presented with obvious openings. The skirmisher is always the node the Puppet Master uses its protective intercept reaction to defend. These are things the party notices through observation and engagement — the hierarchy is readable, but only to a party that is watching for it.)*

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Puppet Master's action economy is built around **control maintenance and selective personal intervention**, not sustained offensive output. The Puppet Master's personal combat capability is real but deliberately held in reserve — revealing it is itself information the party can use.

#### Actions

**The Puppet Master should have:**

- A **control action** that maintains or extends the hierarchy — issuing commands that grant nodes additional actions, repositioning nodes to maximize the Division Strength, applying a buff or condition through a controlled node that the Puppet Master could not apply directly. This action should be the Puppet Master's default on most turns, used whenever the field is functional. Its use communicates that the Puppet Master is actively directing rather than simply present.

- A **personal strike** that is at or above curve in damage, used only when the Puppet Master is directly threatened or when a specific node has been eliminated and the Puppet Master is compensating for its loss. This attack reveals the Puppet Master as a genuine combatant in its own right — a party that assumed the Puppet Master was fragile because it had been directing rather than fighting will be surprised by the quality of this strike. The personal strike should impose a condition that feeds back into the control structure: Frightened, a Charmed effect, or a brief Dominated condition that makes the target briefly act against their allies.

- A **Multiattack** that lets the Puppet Master use the control action and the personal strike once each, available only once the field has been reduced to a specific threshold — typically when fewer than half the original nodes remain. The Multiattack signals phase transition: the Puppet Master is no longer primarily a commander, it is a combatant with remaining assets.

> **Puppet Master-specific note:** The Puppet Master should use its personal strike rarely in the early encounter. A Puppet Master that fights personally from round one looks like a Tyrant with minions, not a Commander whose power is relational. Reserve the personal strike for the mid-encounter, when a key node has fallen and the party has begun reading the hierarchy. Its appearance should feel like a response to the party's progress, not a default offensive choice.

#### Bonus Actions

The Puppet Master's Bonus Actions are its **hidden command tools** — what issues rapid instructions, repositions nodes outside their normal turn, and conceals the hierarchy's communications from the party.

- A **silent command bonus action** that orders one node to take a specific action outside its normal turn — move to a new position, make an opportunity attack, use a special ability, or take the Dodge or Disengage action. This command should have no visible origin: the node simply acts, and attentive players must infer that something directed it. The node's response to the silent command is a discovery tell — a party that notices that a specific node always acts when the Puppet Master uses its bonus action has identified the communication channel and can begin watching for what triggers it.

- A **hierarchy adjustment bonus action** that changes which node is currently the active control anchor — the node whose survival enables the Puppet Master's most powerful command abilities. This ability has a limited number of uses — two to three per encounter — and cannot relocate the anchor to a node it has already occupied during the current encounter. When the party begins targeting the current anchor, the Puppet Master quietly shifts authority to a different node. The adjustment should be visible in the nodes' subsequent behavior — the former anchor becomes less reactive, the new anchor becomes more protected — but the shift itself should require deduction to identify. Once the adjustment uses are exhausted, the anchor is fixed and the puzzle resolves: the party that has been tracking the pattern now knows exactly where the anchor will remain.

> **On the hierarchy adjustment:** This is the Puppet Master's primary defense against being solved too quickly. A party that correctly identifies the current anchor and commits to destroying it should find the Puppet Master responding by moving the target — but the response must be observable and finite. The adjustment is not a way to make the puzzle permanently unsolvable; it is a way to make the puzzle require two rounds of correct deduction rather than one, and to make that requirement apply only as many times as the adjustment budget allows. The new anchor must have a legible discovery tell, must be reachable once identified, and must never be a node the anchor has already occupied — so each adjustment removes one node from the pool and the correct target becomes progressively more obvious.

#### Reactions

The Puppet Master has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the concealed hierarchy becomes most directly visible — the moments when the Puppet Master's protective instincts reveal which nodes actually matter.

**Required:**

- **A protective intercept reaction** *(trigger: a node that is a structural anchor in the hierarchy would be reduced to 0 HP, or a node the Puppet Master has not previously prioritized is targeted for the first time).* The Puppet Master expends a reaction to either redirect the attack to a different node, grant the targeted node a defensive buff that keeps it alive, or make a personal strike against the attacker as retaliation. This reaction is the encounter's primary discovery tell: the Puppet Master defends nodes in proportion to their actual importance, not their apparent importance. A party that watches which nodes trigger this reaction has a direct window into the hierarchy.

- **A misdirection reaction** *(trigger: a creature successfully identifies a node's structural role through a skill check, or a creature targets the Puppet Master directly while nodes are still functional).* The Puppet Master immediately issues a silent command that repositions two nodes, swaps the current anchor's apparent behavior, or creates a new apparent threat by ordering a previously passive node into aggressive action. This reaction is the Division Strength made reactive — every time the party makes progress on the cognitive puzzle, the field reconfigures to demand their attention elsewhere. The misdirection should not invalidate the party's deduction; it should cost them an additional round to confirm it.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A revealed hand** *(trigger: the Puppet Master is reduced below half HP for the first time).* The Puppet Master drops all pretense of concealment: it reveals a previously hidden node that was not visible on the field, makes its control abilities visible and audible for the rest of the encounter, or personally enters the fight in an unambiguous way. This reaction signals the phase transition and resets the Division pressure with a new asset — but it also strips away concealment, meaning the party now has full information about the hierarchy for the remainder of the fight.
- **A severed string** *(trigger: a controlled node is reduced to 0 HP).* The Puppet Master immediately uses one of its control tools on a surviving node at no action cost — granting it an additional attack, repositioning it, or applying a buff. The elimination of one node does not simply reduce the field; it briefly improves a surviving node as the Puppet Master redistributes the severed node's authority. This reaction makes kill order more important: eliminating the wrong node first may temporarily strengthen the nodes that remain.
- **A last puppet** *(trigger: only one controlled node remains).* The Puppet Master channels its full control into the surviving node, doubling that node's maximum HP for the remainder of the encounter and granting it advantage on all attacks and saving throws. The Puppet Master that commanded twelve has collapsed to a direct symbiosis with one — and that one is now expressing the full force of the hierarchy's remaining authority.

> **On the protective intercept reaction:** This is the Puppet Master's most important reaction for communicating the hierarchy. It must fire in a way that reveals the boss's priorities — and therefore the hierarchy's structure — to attentive players. A Puppet Master that uses this reaction equally on all nodes teaches the party nothing. A Puppet Master that uses it specifically and consistently on the nodes that actually matter is teaching the party which nodes those are.

### 4. The Field — Building the Hidden Hierarchy

The Puppet Master's field is an epistemic puzzle, not a tactical one. The party is not primarily asking "what order do I kill these in?" — they are asking "which of these actually matter, and why?" The field must be designed to pose that question clearly and to reward the party for answering it through observation.

**The hidden hierarchy must have:**

A **clear structural reality** that the DM knows completely before the encounter begins. Every node's role in the hierarchy must be defined: which nodes are anchors, which are decoys, which are secondary threats that matter only if the anchors survive, which are escalation assets held in reserve. The DM cannot discover this during play — the hierarchy must be fully mapped before the first round.

A **surface appearance that genuinely misleads** on first read. The most important nodes should not look most important. The decoys should be visually prominent, behaviorally aggressive, or positioned in ways that draw focus. The anchors should appear secondary, incidental, or simply not the most pressing problem.

A **minimum of two genuine discovery tells** in the field — behaviors, patterns, reactions that the party can observe and attribute meaning to without being explicitly told. The Puppet Master defending a node the party was not prioritizing. A node that repositions whenever the Puppet Master takes damage. A node that changes behavior when a specific other node falls. These tells are the thread the party pulls to unravel the hierarchy.

A **consequence for correct deduction** that lands immediately. When the party identifies and eliminates a genuine anchor node, something visible should change: the Puppet Master reacts in a way it has not reacted before, surviving nodes briefly lose coordination, a buff that was applying to the whole field drops. The payoff for correct deduction must be visible and attributable — the party should know that what they just did worked.

---

### 5. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Puppet Master's CR calibration follows the same Commander principle: **the CR is the sum of the Puppet Master and the field, not the Puppet Master alone.** It adds one Puppet Master-specific adjustment: **the field's effective threat is higher than its stat block suggests, because the hierarchy multiplies the nodes' individual contributions.**

**Steps:**

1. **Allocate the field's threat budget at 50–60%.** The hierarchy multiplies the field's output beyond what the individual nodes' stat blocks suggest. The Puppet Master's personal combat capability can be somewhat lower than a Warlord's to compensate.


2. **Set the Puppet Master's AC above baseline, HP at or below baseline.** High AC discourages early direct focus while the field is functional. Below-baseline HP reflects that the Puppet Master's survivability comes from its field, not its personal durability — once the hierarchy falls, the Puppet Master should fall quickly.

3. **Design anchor nodes at full CR-appropriate values; design decoy nodes at 50–60%.** Anchors are real threats requiring real effort to eliminate. Decoys are designed to absorb attention — they can be undertuned, because their purpose is misdirection rather than genuine danger.

4. **Calibrate discovery tells against the expected encounter length.** A party of four will typically need 2–3 rounds to form a hypothesis about the hierarchy and another 1–2 rounds to test it. The tells must be noticeable within that window. A tell that only becomes visible after the party has already guessed correctly is confirmation, not a tell. It must fire early enough to be actionable.

---

### The Puppet Master's Tell

Every Puppet Master encounter must have one — for the hierarchy, not for any individual node. Write it in plain language before finalizing any stat block:

> *"This Puppet Master's hierarchy is structured so that [specific node or relationship] is the actual anchor. The tell that reveals this is [specific behavior the DM narrates and the party can observe and deduce from]. Players who [track this tell and act on it] will find the field rapidly losing coherence once the anchor falls — and the Puppet Master, isolated, far more manageable than the field made it appear."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the hierarchy has no readable structure — it is a collection of threats with a hidden answer the party must guess rather than deduce. Go back to the field design and build at least two legible discovery tells into the nodes' narrated behaviors. The hierarchy must be solvable through observation. Players who are paying attention should feel the shape of it by round two, even if they are not certain of it until round three.

## The Coordinator

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the party cannot focus because every position they abandon feeds every position they are trying to address. The puzzle is finding the load-bearing point — the one front whose collapse causes the others to lose their mutual reinforcement — and reaching it while the network is actively working against them.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A Coordinator's high concept must communicate a system, not a collection of individual threats. The Coordinator's forces are not simply present at multiple locations — they are operating as a single distributed machine, each part depending on the others to function. The boss is the architect of that machine, present somewhere within it, and the encounter is the process of pulling the machine apart before the party is consumed by it.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what is the system these positions form, and what is the logic of their interdependence?* The interdependence should feel purposeful and specific — a defensive formation where each position covers the angles the others cannot, a ritual network where each component feeds the next, a tactical advance where each front pins the defenders while the others maneuver. The logic of the system is the identity of the encounter.

**Strong examples:** "The Pale General at the Center of the Iron Formation," "The Architect of the Overlapping Siege," "She Who Holds All Three Gates at Once," "The Last Strategos of the Unbroken Line."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Multi-Front Boss," "Flanking Commander," "Position Holder." These describe tactics, not identity. The high concept should communicate *what the system is built for* — the purpose the interlocking positions serve — because that purpose is what the party must understand to find the load-bearing point.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Coordinator template asks a specific question before anything else: **what is each position doing, and how does each position enable every other position?**

Every front must have a connection to every other front, and those connections must be legible through play. Design the connections before designing the stat blocks.

#### The Coordinator's Tools

**Network Maintenance Tools** — what the Coordinator does to sustain the mutual reinforcement across positions.
*(Examples: a trait that grants all positions within 60 feet a bonus as long as two or more positions are simultaneously active; a bonus action that re-establishes a severed connection between two positions that would otherwise lose their mutual reinforcement; a reaction that temporarily bridges the gap when a position is destroyed, keeping the network functional for one additional round. Network maintenance tools are what make the encounter feel like fighting a system rather than a collection of targets.)*

**Personal Combat Tools** — what the Coordinator contributes as a direct combatant.
*(Examples: a primary strike that is at-curve and used when the Coordinator's position is under direct pressure; a repositioning ability that moves the Coordinator to whichever position in the network is most threatened; a defensive ability that is significantly stronger while the network is intact than when it is broken — expressing that the Coordinator is drawing personal strength from the system. The Coordinator should be harder to kill while the network stands, and noticeably more vulnerable once it begins to fall.)*

**Structural Anchor Tools** — what identifies one specific position as the load-bearing point the party must find.
*(Examples: a trait that visibly connects the Coordinator's combat effectiveness to one specific position; a behavior pattern in the Coordinator's actions that reveals which position it prioritizes when threatened; a network effect that requires all other positions to route through one specific point — destroying any other position degrades the network, but destroying this position collapses it. The structural anchor must be discoverable through play, not randomly guessable.)*

#### The Field's Tools

The Coordinator's positions differ from the Warlord's units and the Puppet Master's nodes in one critical way: **each position's function cannot be fully understood in isolation — it only makes sense in the context of what the other positions are doing.**

Each position should have:

**A direct function** — what the position does to the party when they engage it.
*(Examples: a position that deals ranged damage from elevation; a position that locks down party movement with difficult terrain or a grappling unit; a position that applies a persistent debuff that accumulates while the party ignores it. The direct function is why the party cannot simply ignore this front.)*

**A network function** — what the position does for the other positions while it remains active.
*(Examples: the elevated position provides flanking advantage to the melee position; the lockdown position prevents the party from reaching the elevated position efficiently; the debuff position applies a condition that makes the Coordinator's personal attacks more effective. The network function is the Coordinator's structural logic — each position is both a threat and a support pillar.)*

**A collapse tell** — how the network visibly changes when this position falls.
*(A collapse tell is a narrated change in the field's behavior that the party can attribute to the position's elimination. When the elevated position falls, the melee position stops receiving flanking advantage and becomes noticeably less accurate. When the lockdown position falls, the network's spatial control degrades and the party can reach positions they previously could not. The collapse tell is what teaches the party that positions are connected, and what confirms that they have found the load-bearing point when they hit it.)*

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Coordinator's action economy is built around **network management on the boss's turn and personal combat effectiveness that scales with the network's integrity**. The boss hits harder when the system is intact and becomes more manageable as the party tears it down.

#### Actions

**The Coordinator should have:**

- A **network action** that maintains, extends, or restores the mutual reinforcement between positions — re-establishing a connection the party severed, granting a position an immediate additional effect, or moving a position's unit to a new location within the network's coverage. This action should be the Coordinator's default when the network is functional, communicating that the boss's primary concern is the system's integrity, not personal combat.

- A **primary strike** that is at or above curve in damage and visibly stronger while the network is intact — expressed as a bonus to attack or damage tied to the number of active positions, or access to an additional effect (a condition, forced movement, or area component) that the strike loses when the network begins to fall. The degradation of the primary strike as positions fall is the Targeting Strength made mechanical: the party cannot just outlast the network, they feel the difference between fighting the Coordinator with the network behind it and fighting it alone.

- A **Multiattack** that lets the Coordinator use the network action and the primary strike once each. The Coordinator should shift to using the primary strike twice once the network falls below a specific threshold — the phase transition expressed in action economy terms. A Coordinator that has lost its network is no longer primarily a commander; it is a combatant fighting with personal resources only.

> **Coordinator-specific note:** The Coordinator's primary strike should have a conspicuous advantage when the network is fully intact — something players notice immediately in round one — and a conspicuous degradation when the first position falls. The contrast teaches the party that the network is doing something to the Coordinator's personal power, not just to the field. This is the clearest possible communication of the archetype's core mechanical identity.

#### Bonus Actions

The Coordinator's Bonus Actions are its **field adjustment tools** — what it uses to keep the network's coverage complete when the party begins finding gaps.

- A **position reinforcement bonus action** that grants one active position a temporary buff on the Coordinator's turn — additional HP, a damage bonus for one round, resistance to the next attack made against it. The Coordinator uses this on whichever position is most structurally important to the network's integrity in the current round, communicating where its priorities lie. The reinforcement is noticeable but finite — the party should feel that they are making the Coordinator spend something to keep the position viable, and that the resource has a limit.

- A **network repair bonus action** that, in limited uses, partially restores a severed connection — granting a destroyed position's network function to a surviving position for one round, or bridging two positions that have been separated by party movement. This ability gives the Coordinator mid-encounter resilience: the party breaks a connection and the Coordinator reroutes around it. Both abilities should have clear use limits so the party can track the Coordinator's remaining capacity to sustain the system. When both are expended, what falls next falls permanently.

#### Reactions

The Coordinator has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the network's mutual support is most directly felt — the moments where attacking one position triggers responses from others, and where the party learns that the fronts are not independent.

**Required:**

- **A network response reaction** *(trigger: a creature reduces a position's unit below half HP, or a creature moves to a location that breaks line of reinforcement between two positions).* One or more other positions immediately respond — the flanking position shifts angle to cover the threatened front, a surviving unit from another position moves to reinforce, a buff transfers from the threatened position to a position the party is not currently addressing. This reaction is the structural logic made visible: attacking one position triggers the network. The narration must be specific and attributable — "as you press the left flank, the unit at the center immediately shifts to cover the gap" — so the party understands that the positions are communicating, not coincidentally repositioning.

- **A load-bearing reveal reaction** *(trigger: a creature destroys any position that is not the structural anchor).* The Coordinator immediately makes visible which connection in the network was most strained by the loss — it visibly reinforces the load-bearing position, moves toward it, or its behavior changes in a way that a perceptive player can attribute to that position's importance. This reaction fires on every non-anchor elimination, not only when the Coordinator takes damage. The Targeting Strength's discovery mechanism depends on accumulating evidence: each wrong position the party destroys must make the right one more visible, and that visibility can only compound if the reveal fires every time. A party that eliminates three non-anchor positions in quick succession should have received three reactions that increasingly narrow the answer — not one reaction that happened to coincide with an HP threshold.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A structural pivot** *(trigger: a position is reduced to 0 HP).* The Coordinator immediately reconfigures the surviving positions — each active position moves up to half its unit's speed and assumes a new network role, compensating for the loss. The network does not collapse when a position falls; it reorganizes. This reaction makes each elimination feel significant without being decisive — the party has degraded the network, but the network has adapted. The Weakness opens only when the structural anchor falls and the pivot has nothing to build around.
- **A commander's presence** *(trigger: the Coordinator moves to a position's location or ends its turn adjacent to one of its units).* All remaining positions gain advantage on their next attack. The Coordinator's physical presence within the network amplifies it — a boss that relocates to threatened positions becomes a moving problem, demanding the party track both the network and the commander simultaneously.
- **A last formation** *(trigger: fewer than two positions remain active).* The Coordinator orders all surviving units to converge on its own location, forming a final clustered defensive position around itself. The Division problem collapses — there is only one front now — but the combined defensive profile of the Coordinator and its remaining units is formidable. The Crisis Weakness fires here: the remaining threats are now visible, readable, and on a clock.

---

### 4. The Field — Building the Mutual Support Network

The Coordinator's field is a geometric problem, not an epistemic one. Unlike the Puppet Master's hierarchy — where the puzzle is discovering which targets matter — the Coordinator's connections are visible from round one. The puzzle is spatial and structural: *which connection, when severed, causes the others to fail?*

**The mutual support network must have:**

**Visible connections between every pair of positions.** The party should be able to see, from the start of the encounter, that positions are covering each other. A ranged unit at elevation that grants flanking advantage to a melee unit below it. A support unit whose healing reaches any position within 30 feet. A buffing unit whose aura extends to the boss's location. These connections should be narrated in the encounter's setup description so the party enters with a working picture of the system.

**At least one connection that is less obvious than the others.** The most visible connections are not the load-bearing ones — the party will target them first. The structural anchor should be the connection that takes a round or two of engagement to identify, not because it is hidden, but because its importance only becomes clear once the party has seen what the network does when it is intact.

**A clear degradation sequence.** When positions fall, the network should degrade in a specific, predictable way the party can observe. Eliminating the flanking unit removes flanking advantage from the melee unit's attacks — and the party can see the melee unit becoming less effective as a result. The degradation tells the party exactly what each position was contributing, which retroactively tells them which positions were most worth targeting.

**A load-bearing point whose collapse is unmistakable.** When the structural anchor falls, the network should collapse in a way that is visually and mechanically distinctive from the ordinary degradation of other positions. Other positions' elimination degrades the network. The anchor's elimination breaks it. The difference must be felt immediately.

---

### 5. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Coordinator's CR calibration follows the Commander principle — **the CR is the sum of the Coordinator and the field, not the Coordinator alone** — with one Coordinator-specific adjustment: **the Coordinator's personal combat power scales with the network's integrity, and CR benchmarks must account for both the network-intact and network-broken states.**

**Steps:**

1. **Allocate the field's threat budget at 40–50%.** The Coordinator's positions are somewhat less individually threatening than the Warlord's units, because their individual threat is amplified by the network. The combined multiplier brings the field's effective threat to the target budget.


2. **Set the Coordinator's personal combat effectiveness visibly different in network-intact versus network-broken states.** The primary strike should deal meaningfully more damage when the network is fully active than when it is partially or fully collapsed. The contrast must be immediately perceptible — the party should feel when they have degraded the network because the boss hits differently.

3. **Set AC above baseline, HP at baseline.** The Coordinator's personal durability comes from the network's protective coverage and its ability to reposition to protected locations. High AC reflects this coverage. HP at baseline reflects that once the coverage is gone, the Coordinator falls at a rate appropriate to its actual difficulty.

4. **Assign field unit HP and AC in proportion to their role in the network.** The load-bearing position's unit should be at or slightly above standard values for its CR — it is the most important target and should feel appropriately difficult to eliminate. Positions that are primarily about network function can be slightly below standard values, since their value comes from the connection they maintain rather than from direct combat output.

5. **Design the network's degradation curve deliberately.** The encounter has two distinct halves: network-intact, where the Division problem dominates and the Targeting puzzle is being solved; and network-broken, where the Crisis Weakness fires and each remaining threat is on a readable clock. The transition should occur roughly two-thirds of the way through the encounter's expected duration. Earlier, and the second half drags; later, and the first half exhausts the party before the payoff lands.

---

### The Coordinator's Tell

Every Coordinator encounter must have one — for the structural anchor, not for the network as a whole. Write it in plain language before finalizing any stat block:

> *"This Coordinator's network is load-bearing at [specific position or connection]. The tell that reveals this is [specific narrated behavior — the Coordinator's response when this position is threatened, the change in other positions' behavior when this connection is stressed]. Players who [identify the anchor and commit to severing it] will find the remaining positions losing their coordination — and the Coordinator, stripped of the network, fighting on terms the party finally controls."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the Division puzzle has no structural solution — every position is equally important, which means none of them are the anchor, which means there is no discovery moment and no decisive payoff. Go back to the field design and designate one connection as load-bearing. Then ensure the Coordinator's narrated behavior reveals it within three rounds of engagement. The network must have a keystone. Players need to be able to find it.

## The Ritualist

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **too many things are happening at once, each one developing toward a different disaster, and the party does not have the resources to address all of them. The puzzle is not which target to kill — it is which crisis to let run, and what the cost of that choice will be.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A Ritualist's high concept must communicate a conductor in motion — a boss whose threat is not primarily its own combat power but the processes it has set running and is actively sustaining. The Ritualist is not standing still waiting to be killed. It is working. Every round it acts, something it is building gets closer to completion.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what is the Ritualist building, and what happens when each component finishes?* The processes should feel like expressions of a single underlying purpose — not three random catastrophes, but three facets of one grand design. A necromancer raising an army, corrupting the local water, and opening a gate to the plane of death is not running three unrelated rituals. It is building a single catastrophe through parallel channels. The high concept should communicate that purpose.

**Strong examples:** "The High Celebrant of the Unfinished Apocalypse," "The Architect Whose Work Must Not Be Completed," "She Who Builds Toward the Final Verse," "The Engine at the Center of the Storm."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Multi-Process Boss," "Ritual Caster," "Distraction Enemy." These describe mechanics, not identity. The high concept should communicate *what the Ritualist is trying to accomplish* — the purpose behind the processes is what makes interrupting them feel meaningful rather than mechanical.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Ritualist template asks three questions simultaneously: **what are the processes, how fast are they developing, and what does each one cost the party to interrupt?**

These three questions must be answered before any stat block entry is written. The encounter's triage problem is entirely determined by the relationship between process speed, process consequence, and interruption cost.

#### The Ritualist's Tools

**Sustaining Tools** — what the Ritualist does to keep its processes running and to restart them when the party intervenes.
*(Examples: a bonus action that advances one process by one step regardless of what the party does; a reaction that partially restores a disrupted process when the Ritualist is not currently targeted; a trait that causes one process to automatically restart at half-progress if the Ritualist is not below a specific HP threshold when it is disrupted. Sustaining tools are what make the Ritualist feel like a conductor rather than a combatant — its primary concern is the work, not the fight.)*

**Personal Combat Tools** — what the Ritualist contributes as a direct combatant.
*(Examples: a primary strike that punishes the party member who most recently interrupted a process; a defensive ability that becomes available when the Ritualist is threatened directly, slowing the party's ability to disrupt the most advanced process; an area effect that makes the space around the Ritualist's primary process costly to enter. The Ritualist's personal combat capability should create real pressure — a party that focuses it entirely and ignores all processes should feel the cost. But its personal combat should not dominate the encounter.)*

**Completion Tools** — what happens when a process completes.
*(Examples: a summoned entity that the party must now also address; an environmental change that persists for the rest of the encounter; a buff to the Ritualist that makes it noticeably more dangerous; a cascade that accelerates the remaining processes. Completion tools are the stakes made mechanical — they answer the question "what happens if we let this one run?" with something specific, visible, and consequential.)*

#### The Processes

Each process is a clock running in parallel with the combat encounter. Every process should have:

**A timeline** — how many rounds until it completes, expressed in visible, trackable terms.
*(A process that completes in 3 rounds reads differently from one that completes in 6. Short timelines force immediate intervention. Long timelines tempt the party to delay — which is exactly the Sacrifice pressure. Express the timeline visibly: the ritual circle fills clockwise by one-third each round, the portal crackles with one additional arc of lightning at the start of each Ritualist turn, the infected creature makes a Constitution saving throw that it will eventually fail. The party must be able to see where each process is on its clock at any given moment — through the DM's narration, not a mechanical counter.)*

**A consequence** — what happens when the timeline expires.
*(The consequence must be specific, visible, and asymmetric from the other processes' consequences. Three processes all summoning additional enemies is not a Crisis — it is a volume problem. Three processes that each impose a different kind of ongoing problem — one that deals persistent damage, one that restricts movement, one that degrades the party's ability to heal — is a Crisis, because the consequences are distinct enough to require different responses. Each consequence should be narrated the moment a process completes, so the party understands what they just let happen.)*

**An interruption cost** — what the party must spend to stop or delay this process.
*(The interruption cost is the Sacrifice made mechanical. It might be a skill check that costs an action, a burst of damage to a specific object or creature sustaining the process, a required position that puts the party member in danger, or a resource expenditure — a spell slot, a use of an ability, a hit point toll. The cost must be real and specific: "one action spent adjacent to the ritual circle" is specific; "somehow disrupt the ritual" is not. Each process's interruption cost should be different, because uniform costs make all processes equally tractable and destroy the triage decision.)*

**A visible tell** — how the party knows this process is running and how far along it is.
*(Not a mechanical announcement but a narrated description the DM updates each round: the shadows in the corner grow longer, the temperature in the room drops by another degree, the third candle has lit itself. The tell is what the party uses to form their triage strategy — they must be able to see the relative progress of all active processes without requiring a mechanical tracker.)*

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Ritualist's action economy is built around **process advancement on the boss's turn and personal lethality as a punishing secondary threat** that makes ignoring the boss entirely a costly mistake.

#### Actions

**The Ritualist should have:**

- A **sustaining action** that advances the most developed process by one additional step, or restores a disrupted process to partial progress, or spawns a new subsidiary threat tied to one of the active processes. This action should be the Ritualist's default when it has not been directly threatened in the previous round. Its use communicates that the boss's primary concern is the work — and that every round the party is not pressuring the Ritualist directly, the processes are accelerating.

- A **primary strike** that is at or above curve in damage, used when a party member has just disrupted a process or is adjacent to a process the Ritualist is actively sustaining. This attack should impose a condition that creates pressure on the party member who intervened — forced movement away from the disrupted process, a debuff that makes the next intervention more costly, or a condition that punishes the party member for committing to process disruption over direct combat. The strike says: *intervening has a cost, and I am that cost.*

- A **Multiattack** that lets the Ritualist use the sustaining action and the primary strike once each. The Ritualist should use the sustaining action component to advance the most critical process and the primary strike component against whoever most recently disrupted a process. When no processes are active or disrupted, the Ritualist shifts to using the primary strike twice — the phase transition into direct combat expressed in action economy terms.

> **Ritualist-specific note:** The Ritualist should almost never use its primary strike against a party member who is ignoring all the processes. Attacking the passive party member rewards passivity — the correct answer to the party ignoring processes is for those processes to complete, not for the Ritualist to deal more damage. Reserve the primary strike for party members who are actively intervening. This ensures the encounter's pressure comes primarily from the processes, not from the boss's personal combat.

#### Bonus Actions

The Ritualist's Bonus Actions are its **process management tools** — what sustains the work between turns and what the Ritualist does when the party has forced it to prioritize combat over ritual.

- A **process maintenance bonus action** that fires every round regardless of what the party does, advancing one process by one step or applying one tick of a process's ongoing effect. This ability is what makes the processes feel relentless — even if the Ritualist spent its action on combat, the ritual does not stop. The maintenance fires on the process the Ritualist most wants to complete, which is a tell: attentive players who track which process receives the maintenance bonus action will identify the Ritualist's priority.

- A **crisis escalation bonus action** that, in limited uses, causes a completing or nearly-complete process to skip ahead one step, or causes two processes to each advance simultaneously. This ability is the Sacrifice Strength made active — when the party is successfully managing the processes, the Ritualist can force a crisis by spending this ability. The limited uses are critical: the party must believe that their management of the processes is meaningful progress, and that the escalation has a ceiling.

> **On the process maintenance bonus action:** This is the encounter's most important communication tool for the Ritualist's identity. Every round, the DM should narrate the bonus action's effect specifically — "the ritual circle flares as [Ritualist] completes another verse" or "the third candle ignites, and the portal crackles with fresh energy." The narration updates the party's picture of the processes' relative progress and ensures they always have current information for their triage decisions.

#### Reactions

The Ritualist has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the cost of intervention is most directly communicated — and where the Sacrifice Strength is most mechanically expressed.

**Required:**

- **A process defense reaction** *(trigger: a creature takes an action to interrupt, disrupt, or damage one of the Ritualist's active processes).* The Ritualist immediately makes one primary strike against that creature, or applies a condition that imposes disadvantage on the disruption attempt, or causes the process to partially resist the disruption — requiring an additional action to complete the interruption. This reaction is the Sacrifice Strength made visceral: intervening in a process draws the Ritualist's immediate, focused attention. The party member who stopped the ritual is now also dealing with a boss attack. The cost of the right action is real and immediate.

- **A process transfer reaction** *(trigger: one of the Ritualist's processes is fully disrupted — its progress reset to zero or the process ended).* The Ritualist immediately redirects the disrupted process's remaining energy into one of the surviving processes, advancing it by two steps. The party's success is not cost-free — stopping one process feeds the others. This reaction is what makes the Sacrifice genuinely difficult: disrupting the fastest-developing process correctly may cause the most dangerous process to lurch toward completion. Players who account for this when planning their intervention order will sequence disruptions to minimize the transfer's impact.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A process resurrection** *(trigger: a creature ends its turn adjacent to a disrupted process).* The Ritualist immediately begins restoring the disrupted process, returning it to partial progress. The party cannot simply disrupt a process and move on — they must maintain pressure on a disrupted process, or the Ritualist will begin restoring it. This reaction creates a secondary resource commitment: disrupting a process means committing to staying near it, which means that party member is not addressing the other processes.
- **A completing surge** *(trigger: a creature takes the Help action or uses a non-damaging ability on their turn rather than attempting to disrupt a process or attack the Ritualist).* The Ritualist immediately gains advantage on all attack rolls until the start of its next turn. A party member who spends their turn supporting an ally rather than applying pressure is giving the Ritualist a moment of undivided focus. This reaction makes passive support turns carry a direct cost — the Ritualist notices when the pressure on it eases, and responds by hitting harder while it lasts.
- **A ritual sacrifice** *(trigger: the Ritualist is reduced below half HP for the first time).* The Ritualist voluntarily disrupts one of its own processes, consuming its progress to restore a significant amount of HP and advancing the remaining processes by one step each. The Ritualist sacrifices the work to survive — and the party must reckon with the fact that their most effective damage-dealing has made the remaining processes more dangerous.

> **On the process defense reaction:** This is the Ritualist's most important reaction for communicating the Sacrifice Strength. Every time it fires, the narration must be immediate and attributable: "the [Ritualist] turns from its work, blade already moving as you reach for the ritual circle." The party member who just intervened understands they have drawn the boss's focus — and that the resources they spent on the intervention may now also be needed to deal with this response. The triage decision has compounded.

---

### 4. The Processes — Building the Crisis Problem

The Ritualist's processes are the encounter's puzzle board. Each one is a question the party must answer: *do we stop this now, or can we afford to let it develop one more round?* A set of processes that does not generate genuine triage decisions is not a Ritualist encounter — it is a boss fight with flavored countdowns.

**The Crisis problem must have:**

**At least three processes with meaningfully different timelines.** Two processes create a binary. Three or more create genuine triage: the party must rank all of them, and the correct ranking is not immediately obvious from the timelines alone — because timeline is only one factor. A fast process with a manageable consequence might be less urgent than a slow process with a catastrophic one.

**Asymmetric consequences.** Each process's completion must produce a different kind of problem. The party should not be able to apply the same response to all three consequences — if they could, the processes are effectively identical and the triage decision is false. Differentiate across dimensions: damage type, positioning restrictions, resource taxation, party capability degradation.

**Asymmetric interruption costs.** Some processes should be cheap to interrupt and some expensive. The triage decision is most interesting when the cheap-to-interrupt process has the highest consequence and the expensive-to-interrupt process has the lowest — the party must decide whether spending the resources to stop the dangerous one is worth the sacrifice of the resources they cannot now spend elsewhere.

**Interaction effects.** At least one pair of processes should interact in a way that makes the triage decision non-obvious. The first process's completion might accelerate the second. Disrupting the third might transfer progress to the first. The process transfer reaction handles one version of this interaction — but the processes themselves should have at least one additional interaction built into their design, so the triage problem has a hidden dimension that rewards attentive players who notice it.

---

### 5. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Ritualist's CR calibration follows the Commander principle — **the CR is the sum of the Ritualist and the processes, not the Ritualist alone** — with one Ritualist-specific adjustment: **the processes contribute threat through resource taxation rather than direct damage, and their CR contribution must be calculated differently from field units.**

Field units deal direct damage and impose direct conditions. Processes deal opportunity cost — they consume the party's action economy, force resource expenditures, and impose consequences when they complete. Calculating their CR contribution requires estimating what each action spent on process management would otherwise have accomplished.

**Steps:**

1. **Allocate the processes' threat budget at 40–50%.** The processes are the encounter's primary pressure mechanism, but their contribution is in action economy and resource taxation rather than direct damage. A useful baseline: assume that each process disruption costs one party action per round, and calculate the DPR the party loses by committing those actions to process management rather than direct combat. That lost DPR, compounded across the encounter's expected duration, is the processes' effective threat contribution.


2. **Set AC above baseline, HP at or slightly below baseline.** The Ritualist is a priority target the party will want to focus when processes are under control. High AC makes ignoring the processes and rushing the boss feel costly — the Ritualist can sustain that pressure for several rounds. Below-baseline HP reflects that once the processes are dismantled and the party commits, the encounter should resolve quickly.

3. **Set the primary strike at or above curve.** The Ritualist must be genuinely dangerous when it makes its personal combat relevant. A Ritualist whose primary strike is weak will be safely ignored, destroying the encounter's triage tension. The party's decision about whether to spend an action on process disruption or on the boss must have real stakes on both sides.

4. **Design process timelines against the encounter's expected length.** The encounter should run 5–7 rounds for a complete Ritualist experience. Process timelines should be staggered: one process should threaten to complete in rounds 2–3, one in rounds 4–5, one in rounds 5–6. The staggering ensures the party always has an active crisis decision — there is never a round where no process is approaching completion. Each process's timeline can be extended by one round if the party disrupts it without fully stopping it.

5. **Design process consequences against the party's remaining resources.** Process consequences that land early in the encounter are less impactful than the same consequences landing in round 5 when the party's resources are depleted. Design consequences that scale — or design the most damaging consequence to attach to the process the party is most likely to let run, based on the relative interruption costs.

6. **Calibrate the process transfer reaction against the disruption pattern.** The transfer reaction fires every time a process is fully disrupted. If the party disrupts processes efficiently, it may fire 2–3 times per encounter. The amount transferred should be meaningful but not catastrophic — a two-step advancement is appropriate for a standard encounter. Adjust up or down based on the expected disruption frequency.

7. **Verify the process transfer and crisis escalation cannot combine to push two processes to near-completion in a single round.** If a party disrupts a process and the transfer reaction fires at the same time the Ritualist uses its crisis escalation bonus action, two surviving processes may each advance by two steps simultaneously. Against processes with short timelines — three to four steps — this single round could bring two of them within one step of completion, effectively ending the encounter's management phase prematurely and uncontrollably. Either extend process timelines to absorb a combined four-step advancement, or add a constraint that the crisis escalation bonus action cannot fire on the same round as a process transfer reaction. Design this interaction explicitly before the encounter begins.

---

### The Ritualist's Tell

Every Ritualist encounter must have one per process, plus one for the triage priority. Write them in plain language before finalizing any stat block:

> *"Process [name] signals its progress by [specific narrated description updated each round]. It completes in [N] rounds without intervention and produces [specific consequence]. Interrupting it costs [specific action/resource]. The tell that this process should be prioritized is [observable condition — fastest timeline, worst consequence, interaction effect with another process]."*

> *"The correct triage order for this encounter is [process A] first, then [process B], then [process C], because [specific reason rooted in the asymmetric timelines, consequences, and costs]. A party that executes this order will find the encounter's back half is a clean direct confrontation with a Ritualist that has run out of processes to sustain."*

If you cannot complete both of these sentences, the Crisis problem is not a puzzle — it is chaos. Go back to the process design and differentiate the timelines, consequences, and interruption costs until the correct triage order is genuinely different from random order and genuinely better. The crisis must be solvable through reasoning. Players who think carefully about the processes should outperform players who act on instinct — and both should outperform players who ignore the processes entirely.

## The Swarm Lord

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the field is not a fixed problem — it is a growing one, and it is growing faster than the party can address it one piece at a time. The puzzle is finding the engine, not fighting the output.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A Swarm Lord's high concept must communicate directed growth — a boss whose threat is not the size of its force right now but the rate at which that force is expanding. The Swarm Lord is not simply a Warlord with more units. It is a Warlord whose units are a symptom, not the disease. The disease is the engine.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what is the engine, and why does it compound?* The engine should feel specific and purposeful — not "it spawns more enemies" but a coherent fiction for why the growth accelerates. A necromancer whose fallen enemies rise again. A hive queen whose workers are bred from the corpses of their kills. A siege commander whose supply lines bring more troops with every moment the defenders fail to cut them. A demon whose fear feeds it enough power to tear another rift. The engine is the identity; the swarm is its expression.

**Strong examples:** "The Brood Mother at the Heart of the Rising Tide," "The Unending General Whose Army Feeds on Defeat," "He Who Speaks and the World Answers in Multitudes," "The Rift Keeper Whose Gate Will Not Stop Opening."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Summons Minions," "Spawns Adds," "Boss With Reinforcements." These describe mechanics, not identity. The high concept should communicate *why the swarm grows* — the logic behind the engine is what makes finding and destroying it feel like an act of comprehension, not just an act of violence.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Swarm Lord template asks two questions simultaneously: **what is the engine, and how does the party destroy it while managing the swarm's output?**

These two questions must be designed together. The engine's accessibility — how hard it is to reach and damage — determines the encounter's pacing. The swarm's output — how much pressure it generates each round — determines what the party is managing while they work toward the engine. Both must be calibrated against each other before any stat block entry is written.

#### The Swarm Lord's Tools

**Engine Tools** — the specific mechanism through which the swarm grows.
*(Examples: a trait that causes one new unit to spawn at the start of each round at a location tied to the Swarm Lord's position; a bonus action that sacrifices a destroyed unit to spawn two replacements at the cost of one action; a sustaining aura that grants all units within 30 feet regeneration and causes any unit that drops to 0 HP within the aura to immediately be replaced; a lair feature — a portal, a breeding pit, a corruption node — that must be destroyed to stop the spawning. The engine must be specific enough to be targetable and destroyable. "It just spawns more enemies" is not an engine; "the rift in the eastern wall produces one unit at the start of each of its turns and can be sealed by dealing 30 damage to it" is.)*

> **The boss can be the engine.** The engine does not have to be a separate object or lair feature — the Swarm Lord itself can be the source of the swarm's growth. A necromancer who raises fallen enemies as undead at the start of each of its turns, a hive queen whose aura spawns drones when she is not Stunned or Incapacitated, a warlord whose rallying cry produces reinforcements each round it is not silenced — in all of these cases, the engine is the boss, and destroying the engine means killing the boss. This changes the encounter's structure significantly: the puzzle is not "reach the engine across the swarm" but "deal enough damage to the boss fast enough, while the swarm it is generating keeps making that harder." When the boss is the engine, the three-phase structure compresses — Phase 1 and Phase 3 merge into a single sustained pressure problem, and the Sacrifice Weakness expresses as the swarm becoming finite once the boss falls rather than as a separate engine-destruction moment. This version of the archetype runs leaner and is easier to design, but it loses the spatial puzzle of reaching a protected engine. Use it when the boss's identity demands it; use a separate engine when you want the full three-phase arc.

**Personal Combat Tools** — what the Swarm Lord contributes as a direct combatant.
*(Examples: a primary strike that is at or above curve, representing the Swarm Lord's personal lethality; a positioning ability that keeps the Swarm Lord adjacent to or within the engine's protective range; a defensive ability that scales with the number of active swarm units — harder to damage when the swarm is large, easier when it has been thinned. The Swarm Lord should be genuinely dangerous personally — a party that ignores it in favor of the engine should feel its full attention. But the Swarm Lord's personal damage should not exceed the swarm's aggregate pressure.)*

**Escalation Tools** — what makes the engine's output accelerate as the encounter progresses.
*(Examples: a trait that increases the number of units spawned per round by one after round three; a condition applied to the party that worsens with each unit that reaches them; a compounding buff that surviving units accumulate each round they are not destroyed. The escalation must be visible — the party must be able to feel the curve steepening. If the swarm just adds one unit per round at a constant rate, the encounter is linear and the urgency is theoretical rather than felt.)*

#### The Swarm Units

The Swarm Lord's units differ from the Warlord's and Coordinator's in one critical way: **they are not individually meaningful — they are collectively dangerous.** No single unit is the priority. The aggregate is the problem, and the aggregate is growing.

Each unit type should have:

**A threat profile that scales with numbers.** One unit of this type is a nuisance. Three is a real problem. Five is a crisis. The design should make this scaling legible: a unit that imposes disadvantage on attacks against adjacent creatures is merely irritating when solo, devastating when three of them are adjacent to the same party member.

**A relationship to the engine.** Some units are spawned directly by the engine and dissolve when the engine is destroyed. Others are autonomous and persist after the engine falls. The distinction is important for the encounter's third phase — when the engine is gone, the party should be able to see which units will remain and which will dissolve, and plan accordingly.

**A legible behavior pattern.** Unlike the Puppet Master's nodes, Swarm units should be immediately readable. Their behavior should be predictable and consistent so the party can manage them without devoting cognitive bandwidth to understanding them. The cognitive demand of the Swarm Lord encounter is the engine, not the units.

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Swarm Lord's action economy is built around **engine maintenance and personal lethality**, with the swarm itself generating pressure outside the boss's own turn. The Swarm Lord does not need to attack constantly to be threatening — the swarm is attacking constantly, and the Swarm Lord's turn is about sustaining and accelerating it.

#### Actions

**The Swarm Lord should have:**

- An **engine sustaining action** that directly interacts with the escalation mechanic — activating a spawn, reinforcing a rift, sacrificing a destroyed unit to produce two, or advancing the escalation curve by one step. This action should be the Swarm Lord's default when the engine is functional and the swarm is below maximum density. Its use communicates that the Swarm Lord's primary commitment is to the growth, not the fight.

- A **primary strike** that is at or above curve in damage, used against the party member who is currently closest to the engine or who most recently dealt damage to it. This attack should impose a condition that makes reaching or damaging the engine harder on the next turn — forced movement away from the engine, a condition that restricts the attacker's mobility, or a brief incapacitation that costs the party member their next action. The strike says: *the engine is behind me, and reaching it goes through me.*

- A **Multiattack** that lets the Swarm Lord use the engine sustaining action and the primary strike once each. As the swarm is thinned and the engine approaches destruction, the Swarm Lord should shift to using the primary strike twice — entering a more desperate personal combat mode that expresses the Sacrifice Weakness beginning to open. A Swarm Lord that is still using the Multiattack's sustaining component when the engine is down to its last hit points has not yet adapted; this transition should be mechanical as well as narrative.

> **Swarm Lord-specific note:** The Swarm Lord should almost never target a party member who is managing the swarm rather than threatening the engine. Managing the swarm is correct secondary behavior — the Swarm Lord's pressure should come from the swarm, not from punishing the party for doing the right thing. Reserve the primary strike for party members actively engaging the engine. This ensures the encounter's cognitive load is correctly distributed: the swarm demands reactive management, the engine demands deliberate strategy, and the Swarm Lord's personal combat is the cost of prioritizing the engine.

#### Bonus Actions

The Swarm Lord's Bonus Actions are its **escalation acceleration tools** — what makes the engine run faster when the party is successfully managing its output, and what generates the crises that pull attention away from the source.

- A **spawn burst bonus action** that immediately produces additional units outside the engine's normal spawn cycle — a sudden surge of new threats that demands immediate reactive management. This ability should fire when the party has successfully thinned the swarm to below a specific threshold, representing the Swarm Lord responding to the party's success with escalation. The burst has a small number of uses, making each activation feel like the Swarm Lord drawing on a reserve that will eventually run dry.

- A **crisis generation bonus action** that directs a portion of the existing swarm toward a secondary objective or party member — isolating a healer, threatening a non-combat objective the party cares about, or flanking a party member who has been managing the swarm safely. This ability is the Crisis Strength made active: the swarm is not just a wall of threats, it is a directed instrument. The crisis it generates should demand an immediate decision — respond to the crisis and let the engine run, or maintain pressure on the engine and accept the crisis's cost.

> **On the crisis generation bonus action:** This is the Swarm Lord's most important tool for creating the encounter's characteristic tension. The party is working toward the engine. The swarm generates constant reactive pressure. The crisis generation ability creates a specific, acute emergency that demands a response now — and the response pulls someone away from the engine. The ability should fire when the party is making meaningful progress toward the engine, not at random. It is the Swarm Lord using its swarm as a deliberate instrument.

#### Reactions

The Swarm Lord has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the engine's protection is most directly expressed — and where the party learns that the engine has a cost of access beyond just the Swarm Lord's personal attacks.

**Required:**

- **An engine defense reaction** *(trigger: a creature deals damage to the engine or to a unit directly adjacent to or sustaining the engine).* The Swarm Lord immediately directs the nearest 3 swarm units to converge on that creature, each making one attack against it. The unit count is fixed — not variable — so the cost of pressing the engine is painful and predictable rather than swingy. A party member who correctly identifies that the engine is the target should know exactly what they are absorbing before they commit. The aggregate damage from multiple unit attacks makes breaching the engine's perimeter genuinely costly. Players who thin the swarm before approaching the engine are solving the correct problem.

- **An escalation response reaction** *(trigger: a creature reduces one of the Swarm Lord's units to 0 HP).* The engine immediately produces one additional unit at the destroyed unit's location or at the engine's location. The swarm cannot simply be attrited — killing units feeds the engine. This reaction is the Crisis Strength made explicit: the party's direct combat effort does not reduce the swarm's size at the rate they expect. The only way to reduce the swarm permanently is to destroy the engine. Until then, every kill is temporary.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A distributed retaliation** *(trigger: a creature reduces the engine's HP below half for the first time).* Every unit currently active immediately makes one attack against the nearest creature. The engine being damaged triggers a swarm-wide response that represents the Swarm Lord's defense mechanism activating. This reaction signals that the party has found the right target and is close to the breakthrough, while imposing the maximum cost of that proximity.
- **A reclamation** *(trigger: a creature ends its turn more than 30 feet from the engine).* The Swarm Lord immediately directs one unit to move up to its full speed toward that creature and make one attack. The swarm does not allow the party to disengage and heal freely — retreating from the engine means being followed. The encounter's terrain is the space around the engine, and the Swarm Lord ensures it stays relevant even to party members trying to recover.
- **A surge of growth** *(trigger: a creature ends its turn without having dealt damage to the engine or a swarm unit that round).* The engine automatically advances by one step — producing an additional unit, accelerating the spawn rate, or applying the next tier of escalation. The engine does not need the Swarm Lord's active management to grow; it only needs the party to hesitate. A party member who spends a round doing anything other than managing the swarm or pressing the engine finds the situation measurably worse.

> **On the escalation response reaction:** This is the Swarm Lord's most important reaction for communicating the encounter's core logic. Every time a unit is killed and immediately replaced, the narration must make the replacement visible and attributable to the engine: "as the creature falls, the rift behind the Swarm Lord widens by a finger's breadth, and something else tears through." Players who see this repeatedly will understand that attriting the swarm is not the solution — and will begin looking for the engine.

---

### 4. The Engine — Building the Escalation Problem

The engine is the encounter's central design decision. Everything else — the swarm's composition, the Swarm Lord's personal combat, the crisis generation tools — is downstream of it. Before building any other element, the DM must be able to answer: *what is the engine, how does it work, and how does the party destroy it?*

**The engine must have:**

**A specific, destroyable form.** The engine is not an abstraction — it is something with HP that can be targeted and reduced to zero, or an interactable object that can be manipulated through a specific sequence, or a Swarm Lord ability that can be suppressed through a specific condition. "The source of the spawning" is not an engine; "the rift in the east wall, which has AC 12 and 40 HP, and which produces one unit at the start of each round until destroyed" is.

**A location that requires crossing the swarm to reach.** The engine should not be immediately accessible from the party's starting position. Reaching it means moving through the swarm, which means managing the swarm's pressure while also making progress. The engine's location should make approaching it a deliberate strategic decision, not an automatic first action.

**A visible escalation output.** Every round the engine is active, something should change on the battlefield that the party can see and attribute to the engine. A new unit appearing in a specific location. The existing units gaining a visible buff. The terrain changing in a specific way. The party should be able to track the engine's output through the DM's narration of its visible effects, not a mechanical counter.

**A destruction consequence.** When the engine is destroyed, the encounter should change in a specific, immediately visible way. Spawned units dissolve. The swarm freezes for one round. The Swarm Lord loses access to its engine sustaining action and escalation response reaction. Whatever the consequence, it must land immediately and be narrated clearly so the party understands the engine is gone and the encounter has entered its second phase.

> **When the boss is the engine, these requirements apply differently.** The engine has no separate location to reach — it is the boss. The "destruction consequence" is the boss falling. The "visible escalation output" is the boss's own actions and bonus actions, which the party can already see. What this version of the template loses is the spatial puzzle; what it gains is clarity of focus — the party always knows where the engine is, and the encounter's entire pressure comes from whether they can damage it fast enough while managing what it produces. When designing a boss-as-engine Swarm Lord, replace "reaching the engine" with "pressuring the boss consistently" as the encounter's core skill demand. The engine defense reaction still applies — the swarm converges on whoever is dealing the most damage to the boss — and the escalation response reaction still applies — killing swarm units produces more. The three-phase arc condenses to two: engine-active (the boss is alive and generating the swarm) and engine-destroyed (the boss is dead and the remaining swarm is finite).

---

### 5. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Swarm Lord's CR calibration follows the Commander principle — **the CR is the sum of the Swarm Lord and the engine, not the Swarm Lord alone** — with one Swarm Lord-specific adjustment: **the engine's threat contribution compounds over the encounter's duration, and its effective CR value is higher in a longer encounter than in a shorter one.**

**Steps:**

1. **Allocate the engine's threat budget at 50–60%.** The engine is the encounter's primary threat mechanism, and its escalating output means its effective contribution grows over time. At the encounter's peak — typically round four or five, before the engine is destroyed — the swarm should represent the majority of the encounter's active threat. Calibrate against that peak, not against the opening round.


2. **Set AC above baseline, HP at or slightly below baseline.** The Swarm Lord is protected by its swarm, which means reaching it is already costly. High AC rewards the party for thinning the swarm before engaging the Swarm Lord directly. Below-baseline HP ensures the encounter ends decisively once the engine is down and the party commits.

3. **Set the primary strike at or above curve.** The Swarm Lord's personal strike must be punishing enough that approaching the engine while the Swarm Lord is uncontested is genuinely dangerous. A weak personal strike means the party can ignore the Swarm Lord while destroying the engine — which collapses the encounter's tension into a simple obstacle-clearing exercise.

4. **Design the engine's spawn rate against the encounter's expected length.** A useful baseline: the engine should produce enough units across the encounter's duration that the swarm peaks at approximately 1.5–2 units per party member at maximum density. Below this threshold, the swarm is insufficient pressure. Above it, the party becomes overwhelmed before reaching the engine. The spawn rate should be staggered — slower in early rounds, faster in later rounds — to express the compounding escalation.

5. **Design swarm units at significantly below CR-standard values.** Individual swarm units should fall to 1–2 hits from any party member. Their threat comes from aggregate numbers and from the engine defense reaction's ability to direct them collectively, not from individual combat capability. A swarm unit that requires the full party's attention to destroy has become a priority target, which breaks the encounter's logic.

6. **Calibrate the engine's HP against the encounter's expected pacing.** The engine should be destroyable in 2–3 focused rounds from a party member with the others managing the swarm. An engine that requires 5+ focused rounds will never be reached before the swarm overwhelms the party. An engine that falls in one hit ends the puzzle phase before the Crisis Strength has fully expressed. The engine's HP should create enough resistance to feel like a genuine objective without becoming a second boss fight.

---

### The Swarm Lord's Tell

Every Swarm Lord encounter must have one — for the engine, not for the swarm. Write it in plain language before finalizing any stat block:

> *"This Swarm Lord's engine is [specific form and location]. The party can identify it by [specific observable tell — what happens when it spawns, how it looks, what the Swarm Lord does in relation to it]. Destroying it requires [specific method and cost]. Players who [thin the swarm sufficiently and commit a party member to the engine] will find the swarm freezing and the Swarm Lord exposed — fighting personally without the growth that made it overwhelming."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the engine has no specific form and the encounter is unwinnable through strategy — the party can only attrit a field that is growing faster than they can reduce it. Go back to the engine design and give it a specific location, a specific HP value or destruction condition, and a specific observable tell that announces its presence and operation in round one. The engine must be findable and destroyable. Players who prioritize correctly should feel the moment the swarm stops growing.

## The Saboteur

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the party is not just fighting a boss — they are fighting a boss while things they care about are actively being destroyed on the same battlefield. The puzzle is deciding what to protect and what to sacrifice, under the pressure of a fight that punishes splitting attention.**

> **A note on scale.** The Saboteur's Division pressure works at the scale of a single combat encounter on a single battlemap. The "positions" and "targets" in this template are things present in the fight — NPCs on the battlefield, objects being destroyed in the room, mechanisms being activated within the arena, allies being attacked by agents already engaged in combat. The Saboteur is not a strategic challenge spread across locations; it is a tactical challenge that forces triage *during* a fight. If your design requires the party to physically leave the combat to address a threat somewhere else, you are designing a multi-encounter structure, not a Saboteur stat block.

---

### 1. High Concept

A Saboteur's high concept must communicate deliberate, targeted destruction — a boss that came to this fight knowing exactly what it would break and in what order. The Saboteur is not attacking the party. It is attacking everything the party is trying to protect, using the party's need to respond as a screen against being stopped.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what is on this battlefield that the Saboteur is trying to destroy, and why must the party prevent it?* The targets should be things with immediate, visible stakes — an NPC fighting alongside the party who will die if the Saboteur's agents reach them, a mechanism that will trigger a catastrophe if the Saboteur activates it, a prisoner being executed in the corner of the room while the fight happens. The Saboteur's identity is in its targeting logic: it knows what the party values, and it is hitting all of it at once.

**Strong examples:** "The Wrecker Who Knows What You Cannot Afford to Lose," "The Agent Whose Plan Was Already Executing When You Arrived," "She Who Came to Burn What You Came to Save," "The Dismantler at the Center of the Room."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Splits Party," "Attacks Multiple Targets," "Multi-Location Boss." These describe spatial distribution, not identity — and as noted above, spatial distribution across locations is the wrong frame for this template. The high concept should communicate *why* the Saboteur is targeting these specific things in this specific room. Its selectivity is its character.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Saboteur template asks a specific question before anything else: **what is on the battlefield that can be threatened, and what does losing each thing cost the party in this fight?**

Every target must have a named, immediate consequence. If the consequence cannot be stated before the encounter begins, the target is not a Sacrifice — it is a distraction.

#### The Saboteur's Tools

**Operation Tools** — how the Saboteur threatens targets on the battlefield without committing to direct combat.
*(Examples: agents already deployed in the room — assassins targeting a protected NPC, saboteurs rigging a mechanism, enforcers executing a prisoner — who act on their own initiative each round the Saboteur does not direct them otherwise; a bonus action that activates a trap or mechanism within line of sight; a reaction that redirects an agent from a secured target to an unsecured one; an aura or command ability that accelerates every active agent's work by one step. The Saboteur's operations should feel pre-planned — it deployed its agents before the party arrived, and it is now managing them remotely while the party fights it directly.)*

**Personal Combat Tools** — what the Saboteur contributes as a direct combatant.
*(Examples: a primary strike that is at or above curve, used specifically against party members who have moved to protect a target rather than engage the boss; a mobility ability that keeps the Saboteur out of concentrated engagement while redirecting party attention; a defensive ability that activates when the party focuses it to the exclusion of its operations. The Saboteur's personal combat is a deterrent and a tax on the party's attention — it should be real enough that ignoring it is costly, without being so dominant that the operations become irrelevant.)*

**Pivot Tools** — what the Saboteur does when the party secures a target.
*(Examples: a reaction that directs an agent from a secured target toward a new one elsewhere on the battlefield; a bonus action that activates a secondary mechanism when the primary one is disabled; a trait that generates a secondary consequence from a partially completed operation — the target the party secured still took some damage before containment, and that damage is applied now. Pivot tools are what maintain Division pressure after the party begins succeeding — the Saboteur adapts within the combat rather than being cleanly neutralized one target at a time.)*

#### The Battlefield Targets

Each target is a thing present on the battlefield that the Saboteur's agents are attacking, and that the party must decide whether to protect. Every target should have:

**A visible agent.** The threat to each target is represented by a specific creature already on the battlefield — an assassin targeting an NPC, a cultist activating a ritual, an enforcer approaching a prisoner. The agent is the mechanical expression of the operation: the party can see it, target it, and must deal with it to secure the target. The agent's stat block should be simple and below CR-standard — it is an obstacle, not a combatant. Defeating the agent secures the target.

**A per-round cost.** Every round the agent is not defeated, the target suffers a visible, narrated consequence: the NPC loses HP, the mechanism advances one step toward activation, the prisoner's execution timer ticks down. The per-round cost is what creates urgency — the party cannot defer every target to later, because later means more damage.

**A permanent consequence.** If the agent completes its task — if the party never addresses it, or addresses it too late — something is permanently lost within the fight. The NPC dies and cannot be revived during combat. The mechanism activates and changes the battlefield condition for the rest of the encounter. The prisoner is executed and the information they held dies with them. The permanent consequence is the Sacrifice Strength expressed: some losses cannot be recovered within the fight itself.

**A triage signal.** Something about the target or its agent that tells the party, within round one, roughly how urgent this threat is. The assassin targeting the NPC is moving quickly — three rounds until contact. The cultist activating the mechanism is moving slowly — five or six rounds until completion. The enforcer has already begun the execution — one or two rounds at most. The triage signals do not need to be exact timers; they need to be readable from the DM's narration so the party can form a rough priority order without stopping the fight to calculate.

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Saboteur's action economy is built around **agent management and personal deterrence** — using its own turn to redirect and accelerate its deployed assets while its personal combat capability taxes the party members who try to address them.

#### Actions

**The Saboteur should have:**

- An **operation action** that either deploys a new agent from a prepared position on the battlefield, directs an existing agent to accelerate by one step, or activates a mechanism or trap within line of sight. This action should be the Saboteur's default whenever agents are active and targets remain unsecured. Its use communicates that the Saboteur's primary concern is the work, not the fight — and that every round the party does not pressure the Saboteur directly, the operations are advancing.

- A **primary strike** that is at or above curve in damage, used against the party member who most recently moved to address one of its agents or secure a target. This attack punishes the correct behavior — going to protect a target — and is the core expression of the Division pressure. The party member who goes to save the NPC is the one who gets hit. The party member who stays to fight the boss watches the NPC die. Neither choice is free.

- A **Multiattack** that lets the Saboteur use the operation action and the primary strike once each. As targets are secured and agents are defeated, the Saboteur shifts to using the primary strike twice — transitioning into direct combat once the Division pressure can no longer be sustained. This transition is the Targeting Weakness opening: the Saboteur is now just a fight.

> **Saboteur-specific note:** The Saboteur should prioritize its primary strike against whoever most recently moved away from it — not whoever is dealing the most damage to it. A party member who stayed to fight the boss is doing exactly what the Saboteur wants: leaving the agents uncontested. Do not punish that. The primary strike exists to make addressing agents costly, not to make ignoring agents rewarding.

#### Bonus Actions

The Saboteur's Bonus Actions are its **within-round operation tools** — what advances the agents' work between the Saboteur's main action and its reactions, and what pivots the operation when the party begins to succeed.

- A **remote direction bonus action** that advances one active agent's progress by one step — the agent deals its per-round cost to its target immediately, outside of that agent's normal initiative position. This ability fires every round and is the encounter's clearest expression of the Saboteur's preparedness: the agents do not need the Saboteur's physical presence to work, they need only its direction. The remote direction always targets the most advanced agent — the one closest to completing its task — communicating to the party which threat the Saboteur is most invested in finishing.

- A **pivot bonus action** that redirects a defeated agent's task to a surviving one — the completed agent's operation is not cleanly neutralized, it is absorbed into another agent's workload, accelerating that agent by one step. This ability has a limited number of uses and fires in response to the party securing a target. It is the encounter's adaptation mechanism: the party cannot simply address agents in sequence and expect smooth progress, because each secured target briefly worsens the remaining ones.

> **On the remote direction bonus action:** The Saboteur pointing its remote direction at the most advanced agent every round is a tell the party can read. If they see the boss consistently directing the same agent, they know that agent is either the most dangerous or the one the boss most wants to complete. This information is useful — and the Saboteur using it consistently is part of the design, not a flaw. Players who notice the pattern have found the triage signal the template is built around.

#### Reactions

The Saboteur has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the cost of protecting targets is most directly felt — the moment a party member commits to an agent, the Saboteur responds.

**Required:**

- **A deterrence reaction** *(trigger: a creature moves adjacent to one of the Saboteur's active agents or begins an action targeting an agent).* The Saboteur immediately makes one primary strike against that creature. This reaction is the encounter's central tension mechanism: going to protect a target means absorbing a boss attack. The party must budget for this cost when deciding which party members to commit to agent containment — sending the fighter to protect the NPC means the fighter is taking a boss attack; sending the cleric means the healer is taking a boss attack. No assignment is free.

- **A consequence surge reaction** *(trigger: a creature reduces one of the Saboteur's agents to 0 HP).* Before the agent falls, it completes one final action against its target — dealing its full per-round cost as a parting action, or advancing the mechanism one step, or applying a condition to the target it was attacking. The party cannot cleanly neutralize an agent without absorbing one last consequence. This is an optional reaction — use it at tables where the Division pressure needs additional weight, and omit it at tables where the deterrence reaction alone provides sufficient cost. When both reactions are active, every agent elimination becomes a triple cost: the deterrence strike on approach, the kill investment itself, and the consequence surge on death. That layering is appropriate for a climactic Saboteur encounter; it may price correct behavior out of the party's resource budget in shorter or lower-stakes encounters.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A failsafe** *(trigger: a creature secures a target — fully neutralizing the threat to it).* The Saboteur immediately activates a secondary consequence at that target's location — a trap, a residual effect, a brief hazard. The target is secure, but the position that secured it is briefly dangerous. Containment is never perfectly clean, and party members who move to secure targets must account for the cost of doing so.
- **A redirect** *(trigger: a creature ends its turn adjacent to a secured target rather than returning to engage the Saboteur or other agents).* The Saboteur opens a new agent operation targeting that creature's position — a trap springs, a hidden agent reveals itself, a mechanism activates nearby. Staying at a secured position to protect it is not free: the Saboteur treats it as a new front.
- **A final order** *(trigger: the Saboteur is reduced below half HP for the first time).* All surviving agents immediately advance by two steps simultaneously. The Saboteur's desperation does not manifest as retreat — it manifests as accelerating everything at once, forcing the party to triage under maximum pressure at the exact moment they think they are winning.

> **On the deterrence reaction:** This is the encounter's load-bearing piece. Every time it fires, the narration should be immediate and specific: "as you reach for the assassin, the Saboteur is already moving — you feel the blade before you see it." The party member who went to protect the target must feel the cost of that decision physically, not just abstractly. Players who feel this reaction fire consistently will begin planning for it — budgeting HP, using defensive abilities before committing, or deciding the target is not worth it. All of those are correct responses to the puzzle, and the deterrence reaction is what makes the puzzle real.

---

### 4. The Battlefield — Building the Division Problem

The Saboteur encounter begins with a battlefield design decision, not a stat block decision. Before anything else is built, the DM must answer: *what is on this battlefield, and which of those things will the Saboteur's agents threaten?*

**The battlefield must have:**

**At least two active agents deployed before initiative is rolled.** The Saboteur's agents are not summoned during the fight — they were already in position when the party arrived. The party enters a room where things are already going wrong: the assassin is already moving toward the NPC, the cultist is already halfway through the ritual. This entry condition is the most important expression of the Saboteur's identity. It arrived prepared.

**Targets that are spatially distributed within the combat space.** The targets should be at different locations within the same room or arena — spread enough that one party member cannot address two simultaneously, close enough that the party does not need to leave the combat to reach them. A target in the far corner and a target near the boss and a target in the middle of the room are correctly distributed. A target in an adjacent dungeon room is not.

**At least one target the party should sacrifice.** The encounter must have a correct answer to the question "which of these is it acceptable to let go?" A party that tries to protect everything will exhaust itself and protect nothing well. A party that correctly identifies the most sacrificeable target and accepts that loss in exchange for better coverage elsewhere is solving the puzzle. Design for this — name the most sacrificeable target before the encounter and know why it is the correct sacrifice.

**A consequence when an agent is not addressed.** The agents' per-round cost must be narrated visibly every round — not just tracked mechanically. The DM should announce the NPC's HP each round, describe the mechanism advancing, narrate the prisoner being moved closer to the edge. The Division pressure is built from these narrated consequences as much as from the stat blocks.

---

### 5. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Saboteur's CR calibration follows the Commander principle — **the CR is the sum of the Saboteur and its agents, not the Saboteur alone** — with one Saboteur-specific adjustment: **the agents' threat is not in their combat capability but in the Division pressure they create, and their CR contribution is determined by the triage cost they impose rather than their DPR.**

**Steps:**

1. **Allocate the agents' threat budget at 30–40%.** Agents are mechanically simple — their job is to be present and advancing, not to fight. Their CR contribution is lower than Warlord units or Coordinator positions, but the Division pressure they create is disproportionate to their stat blocks because each one is pulling a party member away from the boss fight. Account for the action economy cost, not the DPR.

2. **Set AC above baseline, HP at baseline.** The Saboteur needs to be difficult to engage directly while its agents are active. High AC makes focusing the boss instead of addressing agents a genuinely costly choice — the party spends multiple rounds of attacks to make meaningful progress on the Saboteur while agents advance unchecked. HP at baseline ensures that once agents are cleared and the party commits, the encounter resolves promptly.

3. **Set the primary strike at or above curve.** The deterrence reaction's damage is the cost of addressing agents. If the primary strike is weak, the deterrence reaction is trivially ignorable and the Division pressure collapses — party members will simply absorb the hit and address every agent without meaningful cost. The primary strike must hurt enough that sending a party member to an agent is a genuine resource decision.

4. **Design agents at significantly below CR-standard values.** An agent that requires serious combat effort to defeat has become a priority target in its own right, which breaks the encounter's logic. Agents should fall to 2–3 hits from any party member. Their threat is positional and temporal, not combat-based.

5. **Design the number of active agents against the party's coverage capacity.** A party of four, with one member typically managing the boss directly, has three members available for agent coverage. Design for four to five active agents across the encounter's peak — one more than the party can cleanly address in a single round. Adjust based on how action-intensive the individual containment methods are.

6. **Calibrate the pivot ability's uses against the number of targets.** The pivot should fire roughly once per two agents secured. If there are five targets, two to three pivot uses is appropriate — enough to maintain Division pressure through the encounter's middle phase without making secured targets feel permanently unsecured.

---

### The Saboteur's Tell

Every Saboteur encounter must have one — for the triage priority, written before the encounter begins:

> *"The target the party should sacrifice is [specific target] because [specific reason — its permanent consequence is recoverable, its per-round cost is manageable, its agent is the most action-intensive to address]. The target that must not be lost is [specific target] because [specific reason — its permanent consequence changes the fight irrecoverably, its per-round cost compounds fastest]. Players who [correctly identify the sacrificeable target and commit elsewhere] will find the Division pressure manageable and the back half of the encounter a clean direct fight."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, every target feels equally important, which means no sacrifice is acceptable, which means the party will try to cover everything, fail at all of it, and feel the encounter was unfair. The Saboteur's power is in the party's uncertainty about what to let go. The DM's responsibility is to ensure there is a right answer — even if the party does not find it until round three.

## The Field Marshal

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the party is not fighting a boss — they are fighting a boss while the boss's forces hold things the party needs at knifepoint. Every action spent fighting the boss is an action not spent preventing a loss. Every action spent preventing a loss is an action not spent fighting the boss. The puzzle is deciding which loss is acceptable, and committing to that decision before the answer becomes obvious.**

> **A note on what "objectives" means here.** The Field Marshal's objectives are not locations across a map — they are things present on the battlefield: captured allies being executed in the corner of the room, a ritual being completed at the far end of the arena, a hostage behind the boss's vanguard, a device being activated by the boss's subordinates. The stakes are immediate and spatial. The party can see them happening. They just cannot stop all of them and fight the boss at the same time.

---

### 1. High Concept

A Field Marshal's high concept must communicate deliberate leverage — a commander who entered this fight knowing exactly what the party would be forced to choose between, and has structured the encounter to make every choice cost something real. The Field Marshal is not simply strong. It is *prepared*.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what has the Field Marshal brought into this room as leverage, and why did it choose these specific things?* The answer reveals the commander's intelligence. A war criminal who enters the throne room with captured members of the party's order as shields. A tyrant who has chained the city's hostages to pillars throughout the arena. A warlord whose lieutenants are simultaneously executing prisoners at three corners of the battlefield while the warlord holds the center. The leverage should feel chosen, not incidental.

**Strong examples:** "The General Who Entered Holding Everything You Love," "The Warlord Whose Battle Plan Is Your Hesitation," "She Who Turned the Room Into a Hostage Situation," "The Commander Whose Strategy Is Guilt."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Boss With Minions," "Threatens Multiple Targets," "Hard to Focus." These describe mechanics, not identity. The high concept should communicate *what the Field Marshal has turned this fight into* — the party is not in a battle, they are in a forced choice, and the boss engineered that choice deliberately.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Field Marshal template asks a specific question before anything else: **what is physically present on this battlefield that the party will feel compelled to protect — and how many of those things can the boss threaten simultaneously?**

The answer to the first part determines whether this archetype can work at this table. The answer to the second determines the encounter's difficulty.

#### The Field Marshal's Tools

**Leverage Tools** — how the Field Marshal applies simultaneous pressure to multiple objectives on the battlefield.
*(Examples: lieutenants already positioned at objectives when the encounter begins, each performing a visible and advancing action against them; an ability that redirects a lieutenant from a secured objective to a new one when the party succeeds; a command trait that causes all lieutenants to advance one step faster whenever the Field Marshal itself takes damage. The leverage should feel pre-arranged — the Field Marshal brought its lieutenants to specific positions because it anticipated the party's commitments.)*

**Personal Combat Tools** — what the Field Marshal contributes as a direct combatant.
*(Examples: a primary strike at or above curve; a command aura that makes the Field Marshal's personal position a force multiplier for nearby lieutenants; a defensive ability that activates when the party concentrates on it to the exclusion of its lieutenants, punishing single-target focus. The Field Marshal must be threatening enough personally that the party cannot simply send everyone to the objectives and leave one member to stall it — it demands real attention.)*

**Exploitation Tools** — what the Field Marshal does the moment the party makes a visible commitment decision.
*(Examples: a reaction that immediately accelerates an undefended objective's clock when a party member moves away from it; a bonus action that orders a lieutenant to complete its task immediately when a second party member commits elsewhere; a trait that grants the Field Marshal advantage on its next attack whenever one of its objectives is abandoned. Exploitation tools are what make the sacrifice feel real — the moment the party decides what to let go, the Field Marshal acts on that gap immediately.)*

#### The Lieutenants and Their Objectives

The Field Marshal's lieutenants are the encounter's mechanical expression of the Sacrifice problem. Each lieutenant is a creature already on the battlefield, performing a specific action against a specific objective, advancing one step each round toward an irreversible consequence.

Lieutenants differ from the Saboteur's agents in one key way: **their objectives carry immediate, in-fight consequences when lost, not just narrative permanence.** When a lieutenant completes its task, the fight changes mechanically — making the boss stronger, the terrain worse, the party's resources thinner.

Each lieutenant should have:

**A simple, readable action.** The lieutenant is executing a prisoner, activating a mechanism, performing a ritual, or advancing toward a specific target. Whatever the action, it should be describable in one sentence and visible from anywhere on the battlefield.

**A per-round advancement.** Each round the lieutenant is not engaged, its objective advances one step toward completion. Three to four steps is a useful default.

**An in-fight permanent consequence.** When the lieutenant completes its task, something changes for the rest of this combat. The executed prisoner was an ally NPC fighting alongside the party. The activated mechanism changes the terrain to benefit the Field Marshal. The completed ritual grants the Field Marshal a persistent buff. The consequence must be felt immediately within the encounter.

**A triage signal.** Something that tells the party, within round one, roughly how urgent this lieutenant is — its current advancement step, the visible severity of its consequence, its distance from completion. The signals do not need to be numerical; they need to be narrated clearly enough that the party can rank lieutenants by urgency without pausing to calculate.

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Field Marshal's action economy is built around **personal lethality that taxes commitment to addressing lieutenants**, and **exploitation of the gaps commitment decisions create**.

#### Actions

**The Field Marshal should have:**

- A **command strike** that is at or above curve in damage, used specifically against the party member most recently seen moving to address one of its lieutenants. The correct behavior — going to stop a lieutenant — draws the boss's personal attention. The command strike should impose a condition that taxes the party member's ability to continue: forced movement back toward the boss, a debuff that slows their advance, or a condition that costs them their next action.

- A **personal assault** at or slightly below curve, used against whoever is directly engaging the Field Marshal. This keeps the boss threatening to party members fighting it directly, ensuring the party cannot assign one member to stall it while everyone else addresses lieutenants freely.

- A **Multiattack** that uses the command strike and personal assault once each, or uses the command strike twice when no party member is currently moving toward a lieutenant. When the party ignores lieutenants entirely, the Field Marshal hits harder — expressing that unchallenged leverage costs the party in action economy as well as consequences.

> **Field Marshal-specific note:** The command strike must always target movement toward lieutenants, never a party member fighting the boss directly. A party member fighting the boss is doing something the Field Marshal is comfortable with. A party member moving to address a lieutenant is the threat the Field Marshal cannot afford. The targeting logic of the command strike is the encounter's most important piece of DM behavior.

#### Bonus Actions

The Field Marshal's Bonus Actions are its **real-time exploitation tools** — what converts the party's commitment decisions into immediate consequences on the same round they are made.

- A **gap exploitation bonus action** that fires whenever a party member visibly commits to one objective, advancing an undefended lieutenant's progress by one step outside its normal initiative. It should always advance the lieutenant whose objective the party has most recently left undefended — the Field Marshal sees the commitment as it is made and responds before the round is over. This ability and the completion exploitation reaction are mutually exclusive within a single round: if the completion exploitation reaction has already fired this round, the gap exploitation bonus action does not fire, and vice versa. On a round where the party simultaneously secures one objective and commits to another, only one of these mechanics activates — the Field Marshal responds to the most strategically significant event, not both simultaneously.

- A **command reinforcement bonus action** that grants one lieutenant a temporary buff — a bonus to its advancement, resistance to the next attack against it, or a brief burst of movement that advances it one extra step. Applied to whichever lieutenant is most threatened by the party, representing the Field Marshal personally shoring up its most contested point.

#### Reactions

The Field Marshal has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**.

**Required:**

- **A pursuit reaction** *(trigger: a creature moves more than 10 feet toward one of the Field Marshal's lieutenants).* The Field Marshal immediately makes one command strike against that creature. Moving toward a lieutenant means eating a boss attack before arriving. This is what makes the division of attention genuinely costly — the party member going to stop a lieutenant is hurt in the process of doing the right thing.

- **A completion exploitation reaction** *(trigger: a creature moves away from a lieutenant they were contesting — breaking off an engagement without reducing it to 0 HP).* The Field Marshal immediately moves that lieutenant by up to its full speed toward its objective and grants it advantage on its next attack. A party member who commits to a lieutenant and then withdraws has spent resources without securing the result — and the Field Marshal ensures that withdrawal has immediate consequences. The lieutenant surges forward, closer to its objective, more dangerous than before the party engaged it.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A redirect** *(trigger: a lieutenant is reduced to 0 HP).* The Field Marshal immediately orders a different lieutenant to abandon its current objective and advance toward the one just secured, picking up at half the previous progress. The party cannot simply clear lieutenants sequentially — defeating one redirects another.
- **A personal investment** *(trigger: a lieutenant reaches its final step before completing its objective).* The Field Marshal makes one immediate personal assault against the nearest creature to that lieutenant, creating a final moment of direct threat that makes the party choose between finishing the fight and sprinting to stop the completion.
- **A calculated loss** *(trigger: the Field Marshal is reduced below half HP for the first time).* The Field Marshal immediately orders one of its own lieutenants to stand down — abandoning its objective, which the party can now claim freely — and uses the released lieutenant to reinforce a surviving one, advancing it two steps. The Field Marshal makes a sacrifice of its own, demonstrating the logic it is imposing on the party.

> **On the pursuit reaction:** Every time it fires, the party member going to address a lieutenant must feel the boss's attention on them specifically. Narrate it accordingly. The pursuit reaction is why the party cannot simply send everyone to the lieutenants and ignore the boss — doing so means everyone is absorbing command strikes en route.

---

### 4. The Lieutenants — Building the Sacrifice Problem

Before the lieutenants have stat blocks, they need tasks. Before the tasks have mechanics, they need stakes. The design sequence is: stakes first, mechanics second, stat blocks last.

**The Sacrifice problem must have:**

**More active lieutenants than the party has members to spare.** If the party has four members and three lieutenants, and one member must manage the boss, two lieutenants go unaddressed each round. That is the pressure. Increase the number of lieutenants or decrease the party's effective coverage until there is always at least one lieutenant advancing without opposition.

**A correct sacrifice.** One lieutenant's objective must be clearly less important to address than the others — either its consequence is less severe, its timeline is longer, or the resources required to stop it are disproportionate to the benefit. The party that correctly identifies this lieutenant and ignores it in favor of the others has made the right strategic decision. The DM must know which lieutenant this is before the encounter begins.

**Consequences that change the encounter immediately.** When a lieutenant completes its task, the fight changes for everyone still in it — the terrain shifts, the boss gains a buff, an ally is removed from play. The consequence must be felt in the rounds immediately following completion.

**Lieutenants that are genuine combatants.** Unlike the Saboteur's agents, which are primarily obstacle-clearers, the Field Marshal's lieutenants should require a real combat investment to address — 2 to 3 rounds of focused effort from a single party member. They are not elite enemies, but they are not speed bumps. The time required to address a lieutenant is what creates the Division pressure.

---

### 5. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Field Marshal's CR calibration follows the Commander principle: **the CR is the sum of the Field Marshal and its lieutenants, not the Field Marshal alone.**

**Steps:**

1. **Allocate the lieutenants' threat budget at 40–50%.** Account for both their DPR and the action economy they consume. A lieutenant that requires 2 rounds of a party member's attention is effectively removing that party member from the boss fight for 2 rounds — that action economy loss is the lieutenant's real CR contribution.

2. **Set AC above baseline, HP at baseline.** The Field Marshal must be hard to fight so the party cannot trivially ignore it. HP at baseline ensures the encounter ends quickly once lieutenants are resolved and the party commits.

3. **Set both strikes at or above curve.** The command strike and pursuit reaction together are the mechanical cost of addressing lieutenants. If both are weak, the party addresses all lieutenants with minimal consequence and then kills the boss — collapsing the encounter's triage tension entirely.

4. **Set lieutenant HP to require 2–3 focused rounds to drop.** Too fragile and the party clears them sequentially without real commitment. Too durable and addressing a lieutenant becomes a full combat engagement pulling a party member out of the fight for the encounter's entire duration.

5. **Stagger lieutenant advancement rates.** Different lieutenants advance at different speeds. The stagger is the triage signal — the fastest-advancing lieutenant is not always the most important to stop, and the party must weigh advancement rate against consequence severity to find the correct priority order.

6. **Design the correct sacrifice lieutenant to advance slowly and have a mild consequence.** This ensures ignoring it does not catastrophically hurt the party, and that the time cost of stopping it is high relative to the benefit. The party that correctly identifies this lieutenant and leaves it alone has more resources available for the others.

---

### The Field Marshal's Tell

Every Field Marshal encounter must have one — for the correct sacrifice, written before the encounter begins:

> *"The lieutenant the party should sacrifice is [specific lieutenant] because [its consequence is least severe / its clock is slowest / its timeline makes it addressable after the others are resolved]. The lieutenants that must be stopped are [specific lieutenants] because [their consequences change the fight irrecoverably / their clocks are fastest]. Players who [correctly identify the sacrifice and commit accordingly] will find they have enough coverage for the others — and the back half of the encounter is a direct fight against a Field Marshal that has run out of leverage."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, no lieutenant is sacrificeable, all consequences feel equally severe, and the party will try to address everything simultaneously, exhaust themselves, and feel the encounter was designed to be unwinnable. The Sacrifice must have a right answer. Design it in deliberately.

## The Spymaster

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the party cannot tell what is real. Some of what they see on this battlefield is a genuine threat. Some of it is misdirection designed to pull them away from the genuine threat. The puzzle is reading the difference — under combat pressure, with imperfect information, and with the Spymaster actively maintaining the confusion.**

> **A note on scale.** Like the Saboteur and Field Marshal, the Spymaster is a single-encounter boss on a single battlefield. Its "crises" are things happening in the room — not operations running across a city. The misdirection is physical and present: apparent threats that turn out to be decoys, agents performing actions that look urgent but are designed to be noticed, false signals that draw the party's attention away from what the Spymaster is actually doing. Everything the party must sort through is visible from where they are standing.

---

### 1. High Concept

A Spymaster's high concept must communicate constructed confusion — a boss that has deliberately made this fight hard to read. The Spymaster is not simply powerful. It has engineered an information environment in which the party's own competence becomes a liability: the more they try to respond to what they see, the more they play into the Spymaster's hands.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what does the Spymaster want the party to be looking at, and what is it actually doing while they look there?* The misdirection and the real threat should be clearly distinct in the DM's mind before the encounter begins. The Spymaster's identity is the gap between the two.

**Strong examples:** "The Deceiver Who Made This Fight Happen on Its Terms," "The Manipulator Whose Best Weapon Is Your Attention," "She Who Designed the Confusion You Are Standing In," "The Shadow at the Center of Everything You Think You See."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Hard to Read Boss," "Sends Decoys," "Uses Misdirection." These describe tactics, not identity. The high concept should communicate *what the Spymaster is actually trying to accomplish* — the real goal hidden behind the apparent crisis field is what makes solving the puzzle feel like comprehension rather than luck.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Spymaster template asks two questions simultaneously: **what does the party think is happening, and what is actually happening?**

Both must be fully designed before any stat block entry is written. The false picture and the real picture must be specific, distinct, and present on the battlefield simultaneously.

#### The Spymaster's Tools

**Misdirection Tools** — what creates the false crisis picture that pulls the party's attention away from the real threat.
*(Examples: apparent threat agents — creatures performing visible, alarming actions that look urgent but are either incapable of completing their tasks or designed to dissolve when engaged; environmental false signals — a mechanism humming with apparent power that is actually inactive, a hostage situation that is staged, an alarm indicating a threat that does not exist; behavioral misdirection — the Spymaster itself performing actions that suggest it is advancing toward one goal while actually advancing toward another. Critically: each false signal must have a tell. A misdirection with no tell is an unfair trap.)*

**Real Threat Tools** — what the Spymaster is actually doing while the party manages the misdirection.
*(Examples: a genuine ritual or mechanism advancing in a less-visible location; an actual ally or objective being threatened by an agent the party has not yet noticed; the Spymaster itself advancing toward a goal the party cannot see because they are focused on the false picture. The real threat must be discoverable through observation — the party should find it by watching what the Spymaster attends to, by noticing what the misdirection is drawing them away from, or by testing false signals and watching them fail to respond realistically.)*

**Confusion Maintenance Tools** — what the Spymaster does to keep the false picture intact when the party begins to see through it.
*(Examples: a reaction that regenerates a dissolved false signal when a party member moves away from it; a bonus action that shifts the apparent location of a false crisis to keep it in the party's focus; a trait that imposes disadvantage on checks to investigate an apparent threat's authenticity. These are what prevent the encounter from resolving too quickly — the party cannot test one signal and conclude they understand the whole picture.)*

#### The Crisis Field

The crisis field is everything the party can see on the battlefield that appears to demand their attention. It contains both real threats and misdirection, mixed in a ratio that creates genuine uncertainty without making the puzzle unresolvable.

Each element of the crisis field — real or false — should have:

**A visible, alarming presentation.** Every element must look urgent from a distance. False signals must be convincing enough that the party cannot immediately dismiss them. The misdirection must hold up to first glance.

**An observable tell.** Every false element has something wrong with it — something a party member who investigates or watches carefully can identify as inconsistent. The tell must be discoverable through in-combat observation, not through specific spell access.

**A real consequence if genuine.** If a party member investigates a crisis and it turns out to be real, addressing it must matter. If every real crisis is trivial, the party will rationally ignore all of them and focus the boss — collapsing the puzzle into a straightforward fight.

**A different consequence if false.** If a party member commits resources to a false crisis, something should communicate the misdirection — the agent dissolves, the mechanism is revealed as inert, the apparent emergency stops developing the moment party attention arrives. The difference between engaging a real crisis and a false one should be legible within one round of commitment.

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Spymaster's action economy is built around **maintaining the false picture and advancing the real threat simultaneously**, with personal combat serving as the cost of getting close enough to sort the two apart.

#### Actions

**The Spymaster should have:**

- A **misdirection action** that either creates a new false signal, advances an existing one to look more urgent, or reinforces a false signal the party has begun to see through. The Spymaster's default when the confusion apparatus is intact and the party is still responding to the false picture.

- A **real advancement action** that advances the Spymaster's actual goal — the ritual, the mechanism, the extraction, the escape — by one step. Used when the party has committed enough attention to the false crisis field that the real threat is unobserved. This is what the misdirection is buying time for.

- A **primary strike** at or above curve in damage, used against party members investigating the crisis field rather than fighting the boss. Investigating a false signal draws the Spymaster's personal attention. The Targeting Strength made mechanical: the boss is hard to locate and engage because engaging it means first navigating through misdirection, and the Spymaster punishes navigation.

- A **Multiattack** that combines the misdirection action and primary strike once each, or uses the primary strike twice when the party has decoded the false picture and is advancing directly. The shift to double primary strikes signals that the misdirection apparatus is exhausted.

> **Spymaster-specific note:** The real advancement action is the Spymaster's highest priority. It uses the misdirection action only when the real threat is currently unobserved, and shifts back to real advancement immediately when party attention returns to the false picture. A Spymaster that advances its real goal every round the party is misdirected creates the encounter's authentic urgency.

#### Bonus Actions

The Spymaster's Bonus Actions are its **confusion maintenance tools** — what keeps the false picture from collapsing the moment a party member looks at it critically.

- A **signal reinforcement bonus action** that fires whenever a party member moves to investigate a false signal, immediately making that signal more convincing — a staged agent performs a more alarming action, a false mechanism produces a new visible effect, a false crisis escalates in apparent severity. Each individual signal can be reinforced a maximum of two times — after which that signal is either confirmed false or its reinforcement budget is exhausted and further investigation yields no additional resistance. The cap ensures investigation eventually produces a conclusive result. The Spymaster may still reinforce other signals using this ability; the cap is per-signal, not per-use.

> **On the signal reinforcement bonus action:** The Spymaster fights hardest to maintain its confusion exactly where the party is closest to seeing through it. The party member who persists through the reinforcement has found the right thread — the reinforcement is the tell that they are looking in the correct direction. When reinforcement is exhausted on a signal, narrate the exhaustion: the staged performance becomes obviously forced, the mechanism sputters, the false crisis visibly deflates. The party member who stayed long enough to see reinforcement run dry has received the clearest possible signal that this was a false lead.

> **On the misdirection response count as the primary safety valve:** The per-signal reinforcement cap and the turning reaction's unlimited use mean the Spymaster can still cycle parties across false signals if the misdirection response reactions don't run dry on schedule. The misdirection response use count is the encounter's primary guarantee that the confusion eventually ends. Design the use count against the encounter's expected length — the misdirection responses should run out within one to two rounds of when the party correctly identifies the real threat, not before.

#### Reactions

The Spymaster has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**.

**Required:**

- **A misdirection response reaction** *(trigger: a creature succeeds on a check to identify a false signal as false, or a false signal is revealed through direct interaction).* The Spymaster immediately creates a new false signal at a different location, maintaining the crisis field's complexity. This reaction has a **finite number of uses** — track them visibly and narrate their depletion. When the uses run out, the apparatus is exhausted.

- **A deflection reaction** *(trigger: a creature moves to within 10 feet of the Spymaster's actual position or attacks the Spymaster directly).* The Spymaster immediately creates a displacement effect — a brief illusion, a smokescreen, a sudden repositioning — that imposes disadvantage on the triggering attack or forces the attacker to reroll a movement decision. The Spymaster is not simply hard to find; it is hard to reach even once found.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A turning** *(trigger: a creature commits to addressing what turns out to be a false signal — moves adjacent to it or spends an action on it).* The Spymaster immediately advances its real threat by two steps. The party member who invested in the wrong crisis has not just wasted their action — they have actively given the Spymaster time to advance its real goal.
- **A last veil** *(trigger: the Spymaster is reduced below half HP for the first time).* The Spymaster collapses all its false signals and simultaneously creates one large, highly convincing false signal in the center of the battlefield. This fires once. After it resolves, the misdirection apparatus is empty and the Spymaster is fighting directly.
- **A redirect** *(trigger: a creature correctly identifies the Spymaster's real goal and announces it or moves toward it).* The Spymaster immediately changes its real goal — pivoting to a secondary objective held in reserve — and deploys a new false signal to cover the transition. Limited to one use to avoid making the puzzle feel unresolvable.

> **On the misdirection response reaction:** The finite use count is the encounter's central clock. When the party sees this reaction fire, they learn something: the Spymaster has a limited number of replacement false signals. Narrate the depletion — the Spymaster's gestures becoming less elaborate, its agents seeming thinner, a moment's hesitation before it responds. The party needs to know they are winning even while the crisis field is still active.

---

### 4. The Crisis Field — Building the Epistemic Problem

The crisis field is what makes this encounter different from every other Commander archetype. In a Warlord encounter, the party sees the field clearly and must rank it. In a Ritualist, they see all the processes and must triage them. In a Spymaster encounter, the party sees a field whose composition they do not yet know — and must figure it out while fighting.

**The epistemic problem must have:**

**A ratio of false to real that creates genuine uncertainty.** Two false signals to one real threat is a useful baseline. Adjust based on how elaborate each false signal is: very convincing false signals need fewer of them; obviously staged ones need more.

**Tells that are consistent and discoverable, not random.** Every false signal shares the same tell — the Spymaster is not creative enough to make each misdirection unique, because tells are a function of its operational limits, not its choices. An agent that always maintains exactly the same distance from the Spymaster. A mechanism that hums at a frequency slightly off from an active one. A staged crisis that does not escalate when the party escalates their response to it. The consistency of the tells is what makes the puzzle solvable: the party is learning the Spymaster's operational signature, not individual false signals.

**A real threat whose identity the Spymaster's behavior reveals.** The Spymaster cannot perfectly maintain its false picture and advance its real goal simultaneously. There are moments — typically when it uses its real advancement action — where its attention is visibly elsewhere. A party that watches the Spymaster's behavior rather than reacting to the crisis field will notice what the Spymaster is actually attending to.

**A resolution that feels like comprehension.** When the party correctly identifies the real threat and commits to it, the misdirection response reaction starts firing rapidly, the false signals begin collapsing, and the apparatus's depletion becomes visible. The party should feel the moment of understanding as a mechanical shift.

---

### 5. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Spymaster's CR calibration follows the Commander principle — **the CR is the sum of the Spymaster and its crisis field apparatus, not the Spymaster alone** — with one Spymaster-specific adjustment: **the crisis field's threat contribution is weighted toward the early encounter, when the party is still sorting real from false, and diminishes as the puzzle is solved.**

**Steps:**

1. **Allocate the crisis field's threat budget at 40–50%.** False signals contribute threat through the action economy they consume. Estimate the rounds the party will spend on investigation and weight accordingly. Add the real threat's conventional threat contribution to get the total field budget.

2. **Set AC above baseline, HP at baseline.** High AC reflects the cost of navigating through the crisis field to engage the Spymaster. HP at baseline ensures the encounter ends decisively once the party commits with full information.

3. **Set the primary strike at or above curve.** Investigation draws boss attacks. If the primary strike is weak, investigation is nearly free — the party will assess every crisis signal without meaningful consequence, solving the puzzle through brute-force testing rather than observation.

4. **Design false signals to require one round of investigation to definitively identify.** One close look, one skill check, one direct interaction — the correct investment for a definitive answer on whether a signal is real or false. More than one round and the encounter bogs down; less than one round and the puzzle dissolves immediately.

5. **Set the misdirection response reaction's use count against the encounter's expected length.** One use per two rounds of expected encounter duration. An encounter running five to six rounds: two to three replacement false signals. When this count reaches zero, narrate the thinning explicitly.

6. **Design the real threat's advancement rate against the encounter's expected length.** A party that immediately identifies the real threat stops it with rounds to spare. A party that spends three rounds on misdirection finds it at a critical juncture. Calibrate for the average party, not the optimal one.

---

### The Spymaster's Tell

Every Spymaster encounter must have two — one for the false signals, one for the real threat:

> *"The false signals in this encounter all share [specific consistent tell — a behavioral pattern, a physical inconsistency, a failure to respond realistically to escalation]. A party member who [specific investigation action — watches for a full round, interacts directly, makes a specific check] will identify it within one round."*

> *"The real threat is [specific thing the Spymaster is actually doing]. The Spymaster's behavior reveals it when [specific observable moment — it uses the real advancement action, it attends to a specific location, it reacts protectively when a party member approaches a specific area]. Players who watch the Spymaster rather than react to the crisis field will find it within two rounds."*

If you cannot complete both sentences, the puzzle is not solvable through play — either the tells are not consistent enough to be found, or the real threat is not visible in the Spymaster's behavior. Both must be present. The Spymaster's power is in the party looking at the wrong things. The DM's responsibility is to ensure the right things are always visible to a party that knows how to look.

## The Taskmaster

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the party is winning the fight they started, but the fight is changing into one they cannot win. Every round of delay makes the Taskmaster's forces more capable, better coordinated, and harder to address. The puzzle is not what to kill — it is when to interrupt the learning before the forces become something the party cannot manage.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A Taskmaster's high concept must communicate active adaptation — a commander whose forces are not simply present but are visibly improving in real time, responding to what the party is doing and becoming harder to fight against with every round they observe it. The Taskmaster is not waiting to be killed. It is studying.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what is the Taskmaster learning, and from what?* The adaptation should feel grounded in something specific — a tactical doctrine it is optimizing, a behavioral pattern it is developing in response to the party's actions, a coordination protocol it is executing across its forces that becomes more refined each round it runs uninterrupted. The learning is the identity.

**Strong examples:** "The General Whose Strategy Improves With Every Exchange," "The Architect of Coordination That Tightens With Each Round," "She Who Studies You While You Fight Her," "The Commander Whose Forces Are Better at the End of This Fight Than They Were at the Start."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Gets Stronger Over Time," "Buffing Boss," "Escalating Enemy." These describe mechanical outcomes, not identity. The high concept should communicate *how* the Taskmaster learns and *what* it is learning — the specific nature of the adaptation is what makes disrupting it feel like a genuine insight rather than just a mechanical interaction.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Taskmaster template asks a specific question before anything else: **what does the efficiency engine look like, and how does the party disrupt it?**

The engine is the encounter's central design element. Everything else is downstream of its operation and disruption.

#### The Taskmaster's Tools

**Efficiency Engine Tools** — the specific mechanism through which the Taskmaster's forces improve.
*(Examples: a command action that grants all forces within 60 feet a stacking coordination bonus — better flanking, tighter formations, improving attack accuracy — that accumulates each round the Taskmaster uses it; a trait that causes each surviving unit to learn from attacks made against it, gaining resistance to that damage type until disrupted; an aura that causes forces to synchronize their initiative, eventually allowing them to act in concert. The engine must be observable from the outside — the party should see the forces improving round by round — and interruptible through a specific action or condition.)*

**Personal Combat Tools** — what the Taskmaster contributes as a direct combatant.
*(Examples: a primary strike at or above curve; a repositioning ability that places the Taskmaster where its command authority is most effective; a defensive ability reflecting the Taskmaster's own tactical acuity — harder to hit using predictable attack patterns, easier to hit using novel approaches. The Taskmaster's personal combat should reflect that it applies the same learning principles to itself that it applies to its forces.)*

**Coordination Expression Tools** — how accumulated efficiency manifests as Division pressure.
*(Examples: a trait that unlocks new tactical behaviors at each efficiency tier — tier one forces only attack, tier two flanks and suppresses, tier three executes complex combined maneuvers; an ability allowing coordinated forces to act as a unit; a passive effect causing forces to automatically reinforce each other when any one is targeted. Division pressure should grow as a direct function of the engine's output — the party should feel the field becoming harder to manage as escalation progresses.)*

#### The Forces

The Taskmaster's forces differ from every other Commander archetype's field in one critical way: **they are the same creatures throughout the encounter, but they are not the same threat.** A Taskmaster's forces in round five are objectively more dangerous than in round one, using the same stat blocks, because the efficiency engine has changed how they operate.

Each force type should have:

**A baseline behavior.** What the force does in round one — straightforward attacks, simple positioning, predictable patterns. This is also the state it returns to when the engine is disrupted.

**A coordinated behavior at each efficiency tier.** What the force does at tier two and tier three — flanking with adjacent allies, suppressing targets who approach the engine, covering the most-threatened position automatically. Each tier is a qualitative shift, not a numerical buff: the force is doing something it could not do before.

**A disruption tell.** How the force's behavior visibly changes when the engine is interrupted — flanking stops, covering positions break, it returns to baseline attacks. The party must see the disruption land in the forces' behavior, confirming the intervention worked.

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Taskmaster's action economy is built around **engine advancement and personal lethality that punishes disruption attempts**, with the forces' escalating capability generating Division pressure as a background effect.

#### Actions

**The Taskmaster should have:**

- An **engine advancement action** that advances the efficiency engine by one tier, applies the next coordination behavior to all forces in range, or issues a tactical directive that unlocks a new coordinated capability for the round. This is the Taskmaster's default. Every round it fires uninterrupted, the field gets harder. Narrate its effect specifically — units shifting into flanking positions, coordinating fire onto a single target, tightening their defensive formation — so the party can see the advancement happening.

- A **primary strike** at or above curve, used against the party member most recently seen moving toward or interacting with the efficiency engine. Attempting to interrupt the engine draws the Taskmaster's personal attention.

- A **Multiattack** that uses the engine advancement action and primary strike once each. As the engine reaches full efficiency, the Taskmaster shifts to using the primary strike twice — it no longer needs to manage the engine because its work is done. This shift signals the encounter's most dangerous phase: the Taskmaster fights with full attention while its fully optimized forces hold the field.

> **Taskmaster-specific note:** The engine advancement action fires before the primary strike in the Multiattack — the Taskmaster advances the engine first, then attacks. This order communicates its priorities: the learning comes before the fighting.

#### Bonus Actions

The Taskmaster's Bonus Actions are its **within-round coordination tools** — what causes new capabilities to activate immediately rather than waiting for the forces' next initiative.

- A **tactical directive bonus action** that immediately applies one coordinated behavior to a specific force or group — without waiting for their next turn. Fires after the engine advancement and expresses the real-time nature of the Taskmaster's command. Always targets whichever force is most exposed or most positioned to capitalize on the round's events.

- An **efficiency maintenance bonus action** that, in limited uses, prevents the engine from being disrupted by the next interruption attempt. Fires in response to a party member moving toward the engine, representing the Taskmaster reading their intentions. The limited uses are critical: the party must believe that interruption is possible and that these uses will eventually run dry. **The depletion must be visible.** Each use should produce a narrated change in the Taskmaster's behavior or the engine's appearance — gestures becoming less precise, the engine's output visibly flickering, a component visibly stressing. The party cannot see a use count on a stat block; they must be able to infer depletion from what they observe. A party committing to sustained disruption attempts while seeing no visible change in the engine or the Taskmaster will feel their efforts are arbitrary rather than costly. The narration of depletion is the mechanic.

> **On the tactical directive bonus action:** The forces should not improve invisibly between rounds. The Taskmaster should be visibly issuing commands with immediate observable effects. Narrate each directive specifically. The party should feel that the Taskmaster is thinking, not just ticking.

#### Reactions

The Taskmaster has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**.

**Required:**

- **An engine protection reaction** *(trigger: a creature takes an action or uses an ability that targets the efficiency engine directly).* The Taskmaster immediately makes one primary strike against that creature and the engine advancement is not interrupted this round — it fires again on the Taskmaster's next turn at the same tier. This is the cost of disruption: the attempt draws an immediate boss attack and does not guarantee success on the first try. The party must commit to sustained pressure.

- **A coordination reinforcement reaction** *(trigger: a creature successfully disrupts the efficiency engine — forcing it back to a lower tier or suspending coordination behavior).* The Taskmaster immediately issues emergency directives to all forces in range, granting them advantage on their next attack and moving each up to 10 feet toward the most advantageous position. The disruption is real — the engine has been set back — but the Taskmaster's personal command authority fills the gap for one round. This reaction communicates that disruption is the right approach while ensuring it does not instantly win the encounter.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **An adaptive counter** *(trigger: a creature uses the same attack type, ability, or tactic against the Taskmaster or its forces for the second consecutive time).* The Taskmaster immediately grants all forces resistance to that damage type or immunity to that condition until the start of their next turn. The field adapts in real time to patterns it observes. Best for Taskmasters whose identity emphasizes direct learning from combat inputs. **Before choosing this reaction, confirm the party has access to at least three distinct damage types or tactical approaches** — if the party's output is largely homogeneous, the adaptive counter will price correct behavior out of the encounter before they can meaningfully adapt. This reaction rewards tactical variety; do not use it for parties with limited tactical range.
- **A redistribution** *(trigger: one of the Taskmaster's forces is reduced to 0 HP).* The Taskmaster immediately redistributes that force's accumulated coordination to two surviving forces, granting each a stacking coordination bonus. Defeating a unit makes the remaining units slightly smarter. Rewards parties who address the engine before depleting the forces.
- **A masterwork** *(trigger: a creature uses the Dodge, Disengage, or Help action rather than attacking on their turn).* The Taskmaster immediately advances the engine by one additional step. A party member who steps back from direct combat — even to help an ally — gives the forces an uncontested round of operation. The encounter's clock accelerates when the pressure on the engine slackens, and the party watching this reaction fire understands that passive turns carry a cost beyond the lost damage output.

> **On the engine protection reaction:** When it fires, narrate specifically: the Taskmaster's head snaps toward the party member threatening the engine, the strike is already moving before the disruption attempt completes, and the engine continues its advancement. The party member who absorbed this reaction understands they are fighting the right battle — and that it requires sustained commitment. The engine protection reaction is the Taskmaster saying: *you are correct, and it will still cost you.*

---

### 4. The Efficiency Engine — Building the Escalation Problem

The efficiency engine is the encounter's central design decision. Design it before statting the forces or finalizing the Taskmaster's combat abilities. Everything flows from what the engine does, how it does it, and what disrupting it costs.

**The engine must have:**

**A specific, observable mechanism.** The engine is not abstract — it is something the party can see and target. A Taskmaster who issues verbal commands has a voice that can be silenced. A Taskmaster who maintains a telepathic coordination link has a concentration that can be broken. A Taskmaster whose forces adapt by observing each other has a formation that can be disrupted by separating units. The mechanism determines the disruption method, and both must be clear before the encounter begins.

**A visible tier progression.** The engine advances in discrete, narrated steps rather than a continuous gradient. Each tier unlocks a specific new behavior for the forces — something qualitatively different from the previous tier, not just a numerical buff. Tier one: attack. Tier two: flank and suppress. Tier three: execute combined arms maneuvers and cover the engine protection zone. The party should be able to identify the current tier from the forces' behavior alone.

**A disruption cost requiring sustained pressure.** The engine protection reaction ensures a single disruption attempt does not succeed — the party must commit across multiple rounds, absorbing repeated boss strikes. This sustained cost creates the encounter's central triage decision: is disrupting the engine worth the HP investment, or should the party manage the escalated field instead?

**A reset consequence that is immediately visible.** When successfully disrupted, every force on the field visibly returns to baseline behavior in the same round — flanking stops, formations break, coordinated positions dissolve. The party sees the disruption land in the forces' behavior. The visible reset is what makes disruption feel decisive.

---

### 5. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Taskmaster's CR calibration follows the Commander principle — **the CR is the sum of the Taskmaster and its forces, not the Taskmaster alone** — with one Taskmaster-specific adjustment: **the forces' effective CR contribution increases over the encounter's duration, and CR calibration must account for their escalated state rather than their baseline state.**

**Steps:**

1. **Allocate the forces' threat budget at 40–50%, calibrated against their mid-escalation state.** Calculate threat contribution from their tier-two behavior — their partial coordination state — not their baseline stat blocks. That is the average state across the encounter's expected duration.

2. **Set AC above baseline, HP at or slightly below baseline.** High AC reflects the Taskmaster's personal adaptive capability. Below-baseline HP ensures the encounter ends before the forces reach maximum efficiency more than once or twice.

3. **Set the primary strike at or above curve.** The engine protection reaction fires on disruption attempts and deals the primary strike's damage. If weak, disruption is nearly free — the party interrupts the engine every round without meaningful cost, preventing any escalation from developing.

4. **Design the engine's tier progression against the encounter's expected length.** Tier two should be reached in round two or three, tier three in round four or five. Too fast and the party cannot learn the disruption method before the field is overwhelming. Too slow and the escalation never feels urgent.

5. **Set the efficiency maintenance bonus action's uses at two to three.** This limits the Taskmaster's ability to protect the engine from the first attempt. Two to three uses means the party absorbs two to three primary strikes before the maintenance runs out — at which point the disruption should succeed on the next committed attempt.

6. **Design force behaviors at each tier to be qualitatively distinct.** The difference between tier one and tier two must be visible in their actions. If the only difference is a +1 to attack rolls, the escalation is invisible and the encounter loses its tension. Each tier change should feel like fighting a different version of the same enemies.

---

### The Taskmaster's Tell

Every Taskmaster encounter must have one — for the efficiency engine's disruption method:

> *"This Taskmaster's efficiency engine advances when [specific observable trigger — the Taskmaster uses the engine advancement action, a specific command gesture, a visible coordination signal]. The party can interrupt it by [specific disruption method — silencing the Taskmaster, breaking formation between specific units, destroying a command node, imposing a specific condition]. Disrupting it costs [specific sustained investment — the party member must commit X rounds of focused action while absorbing engine protection reaction strikes]. Players who [identify the disruption method and commit to the sustained cost] will find the forces returning to baseline — and the Taskmaster's remaining operation, stripped of its coordination, a manageable triage problem rather than an overwhelming one."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the engine has no specific disruption method — either the party cannot find the mechanism or cannot interrupt it once found. Define the disruption method concretely enough that a party member who watches the Taskmaster for two rounds has a working hypothesis, and a party member who commits to disruption for two rounds has a definitive result. The escalation must be interruptible. The party that figures out how should be able to do it.
