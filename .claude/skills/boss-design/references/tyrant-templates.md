# Tyrant Templates

---

## The Hunter

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the Hunter chooses the fight. The party's job is to take that choice away.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A Hunter's high concept must communicate active, predatory control — not simply speed or evasiveness. The Hunter is not a creature that avoids the party. It is a creature that **hunts** them, on its own terms.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what does this creature do between engagements?* The answer should be active — it is tracking, circling, or positioning. Not hiding.

**Strong examples:** "Ambush Predator of the Thornwood," "Apex Stalker of the Deep Wastes," "Relentless Pursuer in the Dark."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Fast Monster," "Sneaky Boss," "Hit-and-Run Fighter." These describe tactics, not identity. The high concept should be an epithet you'd whisper in fear, not a tactical summary.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Hunter template asks a specific question before anything else: **what tools does this creature use to control when and where the fight happens?**

Organize your tools into three categories before writing a single stat block entry:

**Engagement Tools** — how the Hunter initiates contact on its terms.
*(Examples: charges from concealment, attacks from above or below, always approaches from a specific angle the party can learn to predict.)*

**Disengagement Tools** — how the Hunter breaks off when threatened.
*(Examples: speed bursts, teleportation, terrain use, abilities that create distance. Critically: these must have limits. The Hunter must always be reachable — the puzzle is* how*, not* whether.*)*

**Pursuit Tools** — what ensures the party cannot simply wait it out.
*(Examples: a mark that the Hunter can always locate, abilities that find isolated targets, pressure that builds if the Hunter is ignored.)*

The tell that players must discover is embedded in these tools: the Hunter always retreats when X, always attacks from Y, always targets Z first.

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Hunter's action economy is built around **dictating range and selecting targets**, not raw damage output.

#### Actions

**The Hunter should have:**

- A **primary strike** designed to be used from the Hunter's preferred engagement range — melee if it hunts up close, ranged if it hunts from distance. This strike should impose **forced movement or a condition that interferes with the party's ability to pin the Hunter down** (pushed back, knocked Prone, briefly Restrained, etc.).

- A **secondary strike** at the opposite range — slightly weaker, used when the Hunter is forced out of its comfort zone. This attack should impose a **debilitating condition** rather than forced movement. The condition signals that the Hunter is not helpless in this range; it just prefers the other one.

- A **Multiattack** that lets the Hunter use either strike twice, or both strikes once. The Hunter should almost never use Multiattack to hit the same target twice. A Hunter that hits one person twice is concentrating — a Hunter that hits two different people is **selecting targets**, which is characterful and more dangerous.

#### Bonus Actions

The Hunter's Bonus Actions are its **pursuit and exposure tools** — what it uses to maintain the engagement conditions it prefers.

- A **short-range ability** that deals damage and imposes a condition that limits the target's options (Restrained, Grappled, Slowed, forced into a specific space). This is how the Hunter isolates a target or prevents clustering.

- A **longer-range ability** that either deals damage, imposes a debilitating condition, or repositions the Hunter into a more favorable position. This is what the Hunter uses when the party is successfully pressuring it — not to escape, but to reset the field.

> **Hunter-specific note:** One of these Bonus Actions should have a tell. The Hunter uses it the same way every time — always targeting the most isolated party member, always moving to the same relative position, always triggering before its primary strike. Players who notice this in rounds 1–2 have found the thread they need to pull.

#### Reactions

The Hunter has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the Hunter's engagement control lives mechanically.

**Required:**

- **A disengagement reaction** *(trigger: the Hunter is surrounded, or a second creature enters its melee reach, or it takes damage from multiple sources in one turn).* The Hunter moves up to its speed without provoking opportunity attacks and resets to its preferred range. This is not a punishment for attacking it — it is the Hunter choosing not to be cornered. It has a cost: the Hunter must end this movement in a specific kind of position (in cover, at a specific distance, adjacent to the terrain feature it prefers). Players who control those positions have found the solution. **If no such position is available, the reaction does not fire.** A party that has successfully denied all of the Hunter's preferred endpoints has solved the puzzle — the Hunter cannot disengage, and the encounter resolves on the party's terms. This is the intended outcome; do not allow the reaction to fire to an arbitrary location simply because the preferred position is unavailable.

- **A pursuit reaction** *(trigger: a creature moves away from the Hunter, uses the Disengage action, or the Hunter's chosen target breaks line of sight).* The Hunter moves up to half its speed toward that creature and makes one weapon attack. The party cannot simply run — the Hunter always finds them.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A predator's focus** *(trigger: a hostile creature ends its turn without having targeted the Hunter).* The Hunter marks that creature or moves toward it, telegraphing that ignoring it is not a safe strategy.
- **A counterambush** *(trigger: the Hunter takes damage from a creature it cannot see).* The Hunter immediately locates that creature and moves toward it. Invisible attackers do not break its pursuit.
- **A frightful presence** *(trigger: the Hunter is reduced below half HP for the first time).* Creatures within 30 feet must make a Wisdom saving throw or become Frightened of the Hunter until the end of their next turn. This signals the phase transition and buys the Hunter a moment to reset.

> **On retaliation reactions:** The Hunter's reactions should be about **movement and target selection**, not punishment. A Hunter that retaliates against every hit sends the wrong message — it implies the correct answer is "don't attack it," which is not solvable. The Hunter's reactions should punish **clustering** and **stationary play**, not attacking itself.

---

### 4. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Hunter's CR calibration follows the standard process with one Hunter-specific adjustment: **the Hunter's effective AC is higher than its stat block suggests.**

Because the Hunter controls engagement range and dictates when it can be targeted, players will spend rounds unable to attack it effectively. This functions as a defensive multiplier — similar to how Legendary Resistances inflate the effective durability of solo monsters. When calibrating, account for this:

**Steps:**


1. **Set defensive stats conservatively.** Because the Hunter's evasion adds effective durability, do not max both AC and HP. A Hunter with high AC *and* high HP that *also* evades freely will feel unkillable once cornered. Choose one:
   - High AC, moderate HP — it is hard to hit, and not impossible to drop once cornered.
   - Moderate AC, high HP — it is easy to hit once cornered, but there is a lot of it to burn through.
   The second option better expresses the Weakness (Access — once cornered, surprisingly easy to hit) without requiring a low-AC stat block.

2. **Assign ability scores from to-hit and Save DC.** Prioritize Dexterity or the ability score governing its primary strike, then Constitution for durability. The Hunter should not be a frontline brawler — its physical presence in melee is its vulnerability, not its strength.

3. **Distribute damage across categories with Hunter priorities:**
   - **Actions:** Moderate damage. The Hunter hits reliably, but its action damage is not its main threat. It is what happens *because* of the positioning that hurts.
   - **Bonus Actions:** This is where the Hunter's pressure lives. Higher expected damage here than most archetypes, because Bonus Actions drive the pursuit and isolation loop.
   - **Reactions:** Lower expected damage than most archetypes. The Hunter's reactions are primarily movement and control, not retaliation. If a reaction deals damage, it should be incidental to a repositioning effect.

4. **Assign attack damage with the tell in mind.** One of the Hunter's attacks or Bonus Actions should deal damage only when the Hunter is in its preferred position. If that ability is unavailable because the party has successfully cornered it, the Hunter's DPR drops noticeably — this is intentional. Players who solve the puzzle should feel the difference immediately. The Weakness (Access) means attacks land more reliably; the DPR drop means the Hunter is also less deadly. Both together make the solved encounter feel decisively won.

---

### The Hunter's Tell

> **A note on Tell quality across Tyrant archetypes.** The Tell varies in what it demands. The Hunter's and Executioner's Tells are single sentences — completion checks that confirm a design decision already made. The Phantom's Tell covers two conditions (deflection failure and withdrawal trigger) and requires pre-session work to define both specifically. The Shield Master's Tell is the most immediately actionable — its two blanks must be filled with precise mechanical conditions, not general descriptions. When a Tell has multiple required sentences or demands specific mechanical definitions, treat it as a design commitment that should be completed before any stat block work begins, not as a final check.

Every Hunter stat block must have one. Write it in plain language before finalizing the stat block, then make sure it is visible in play within two rounds:

> *"This Hunter always uses [disengagement reaction] when [specific trigger]. Players who [control this] will find the Hunter has nowhere to go."*

> *Example: "This Hunter always uses its disengagement reaction when two creatures are adjacent to it simultaneously. Players who position on both flanks before attacking will find the Hunter cannot retreat — it must fight in contact, where its preferred engagement range is unavailable and the DPR drop is immediate."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the puzzle is not solvable through play. Go back to the disengagement reaction and make the trigger more specific and observable.

## The Juggernaut

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the party is losing a damage race they cannot win by dealing more damage. The puzzle is finding what to stop, not how to hit harder.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A Juggernaut's high concept must communicate inevitability — not speed, not aggression, but the sense that something is keeping it going no matter what the party does. The Juggernaut is not a creature that is hard to hurt. It is a creature whose hurt does not stick.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what is the source of the loop?* Regeneration from a ritual? HP draining sustain? An environmental connection that restores it? The answer should be legible in the high concept itself — not mechanically spelled out, but present as flavor.

**Strong examples:** "Undying Vessel of the Rot God," "The Twice-Killed Warlord," "Flesh That Will Not Stay Dead."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Big Tough Monster," "Unkillable Boss," "Regenerating Enemy." These describe the mechanical effect, not the creature's identity. The high concept should raise an immediate question — *why won't it die?* — that the encounter answers through play.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Juggernaut template asks a specific question before anything else: **what is the sustain loop, and how does the party break it?**

Organize your tools into three categories before writing a single stat block entry:

**Sustain Tools** — what keeps the Juggernaut going despite the party's damage.
*(Examples: regeneration triggered by a lair feature, HP regained on each hit it lands, a curse that transfers damage back to the party, an environmental condition that must be disrupted. Critically: the source must be attributable. Players need to form a hypothesis within two rounds.)*

**Pressure Tools** — the infrequent but catastrophic attacks that create urgency.
*(Examples: a slow wind-up attack that hits for massive damage on a failed save, a grab that sets up a devastating follow-up, an area denial that forces the party out of optimal positions.)*

**Punishment Tools** — what makes the wrong approach feel hopeless without feeling unfair.
*(Examples: resistance to damage that bypasses the sustain rather than disrupting it, a reaction that reflects wasted effort back at the party, a trait that signals "this isn't working" before the party has fully committed.)*

The tell that players must discover is embedded in the sustain tools: the Juggernaut's loop has a source, and that source has a tell. It regains HP at a specific moment, in response to a specific trigger, from a specific direction. Players who identify the pattern have found the puzzle's solution.

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Juggernaut's action economy is built around **catastrophic infrequent pressure and a sustain loop that makes trading damage feel futile**, not sustained attack volume.

#### Actions

**The Juggernaut should have:**

- A **primary strike** that is slow, telegraphed, and hits for significantly above-curve damage when it lands. This attack should have a visible wind-up — a description, a restriction on targets, a saving throw rather than an attack roll — so the party can see it coming and choose how to respond. Landing this attack is not a surprise. Failing to prevent it is a consequence.

- A **secondary strike** that is weaker and more reliable — a fallback the Juggernaut uses when the primary is unavailable or its preferred target is out of reach. This attack should impose a debilitating condition rather than raw damage: Grappled, Prone, Frightened, or Stunned. It sets up the primary.

- A **Multiattack** that lets the Juggernaut use either strike twice, or use the secondary and then the primary in sequence. The Juggernaut does not need to spread its attacks — it is not trying to pressure multiple targets. It is trying to destroy one. Multiattack for the Juggernaut is about sequencing, not distribution.

> **Juggernaut-specific note:** The primary strike's telegraph is not a courtesy. It is the signal that teaches players what the Juggernaut values. A Juggernaut that always winds up its primary against the most isolated target teaches players to cluster. One that winds up against whoever just dealt the most damage teaches players something different. The behavior of the primary defines the encounter's tactical geometry.

#### Bonus Actions

The Juggernaut's Bonus Actions are its **sustain expression and pressure maintenance tools** — what keeps the loop running and the party from ever feeling like they've found breathing room.

- A **sustain bonus action** that visibly triggers the regeneration or healing loop. This should be the most legible ability in the stat block: when the Juggernaut uses it, something observable happens. It glows. It drains color from a nearby lair feature. It absorbs residual energy from damage it just received. Whatever the fiction, the mechanical effect is: **the party's previous damage is partially or fully negated, and this is visible.** This is what teaches players the puzzle exists.

- A **pressure bonus action** that deals damage or imposes a condition as a secondary threat — something that keeps the party from ignoring the Juggernaut while they try to solve the puzzle. This ability should be area-affecting or otherwise difficult to fully avoid, reinforcing that staying still and grinding damage is not a viable strategy.

> **On the sustain bonus action:** This is the encounter's central teaching tool. If players cannot see it happening, they cannot solve the puzzle. Write the ability so that the DM has an obvious cue to narrate — "the creature's wounds knit closed as it draws breath from the altar behind it" or "the damage seems to flow back into it from the ground." The fiction does not need to explain the mechanism fully. It needs to make the mechanism visible.

#### Reactions

The Juggernaut has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These reinforce the loop and punish strategies that try to circumvent it rather than solve it.

**Required:**

- **A sustain reaction** *(trigger: the Juggernaut is reduced to a specific HP threshold for the first time, or a creature succeeds on an attack that would deal its highest-damaging effect against it).* The Juggernaut immediately triggers its sustain source, regaining HP or negating a portion of the damage. This reaction should feel distinct from the Bonus Action sustain — it is the loop's emergency valve, not its normal operation. Players who see this fire know the puzzle is urgent: the loop can even intercept moments that should feel decisive.

- **A punishment reaction** *(trigger: a creature deals damage to the Juggernaut using an approach that cannot break the loop — the wrong damage type, a damage source the Juggernaut is resistant to, or an ability specifically telegraphed as ineffective).* The Juggernaut responds in a way that communicates failure without removing the party's agency: it regenerates a small amount of HP, it reflects a portion of the damage, or it gains a brief buff. The message is: *that is not the answer.*

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A rising tide** *(trigger: a creature ends its turn without having dealt damage to the Juggernaut).* It gains a stacking bonus to its next primary strike's damage or save DC. The party cannot simply kite it — every round a creature fails to deal damage, the next hit becomes worse.
- **An endurance statement** *(trigger: a creature successfully imposes the Paralyzed, Stunned, or Incapacitated condition on the Juggernaut).* The Juggernaut immediately makes one primary strike against that creature. The loop does not stop for crowd control — it retaliates. Spending a high-value ability to shut the Juggernaut down draws an immediate, personal response.
- **A drain reaction** *(trigger: a creature deals damage to the Juggernaut using the correct approach — the damage type or method that can break the loop).* One creature of the Juggernaut's choice within 30 feet loses HP equal to half the damage dealt. The sustain loop fights back hardest when threatened by the right answer. The party member who found the solution is the one the Juggernaut turns on. This option is best suited for high-CR encounters where the party has the resources to sustain the correct approach under additional pressure — at lower CRs, punishing the party member who found the right tool may make the correct solution feel unviable rather than costly.

> **On retaliation reactions:** The Juggernaut's reactions should communicate the loop's presence, not punish the party for trying. A reaction that deals massive damage when attacked sends the wrong message — it implies the correct answer is "don't attack it." The Juggernaut's reactions should punish **wrong approaches** and **passive play**, not effort itself.

---

### 4. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Juggernaut's CR calibration follows the standard process with one Juggernaut-specific adjustment: **the Juggernaut's effective HP is higher than its stat block suggests.**

Because the sustain loop partially negates the party's damage output during the puzzle phase, the Juggernaut functions as though it has more HP than written. When calibrating, account for this:

**Steps:**


1. **Set the sustain loop's magnitude deliberately.** The loop must be large enough to feel like a real problem but small enough to feel breakable. A useful baseline: the sustain should negate roughly 25–40% of the party's expected damage per round during the puzzle phase. If it negates more, the puzzle feels like a locked door. If it negates less, players may not notice it exists.

2. **Set attack stats below curve for the primary, at or above curve for accuracy on the secondary.** The Juggernaut's primary strike is not supposed to land every round — it is supposed to land when the party fails to prevent it. A below-curve attack bonus on the primary keeps the encounter from becoming a one-shot machine. The secondary's accuracy should be reliable, because it exists to set up the primary and maintain pressure.

3. **Assign ability scores from the secondary's to-hit and Save DC.** The Juggernaut's primary often uses a saving throw rather than an attack roll — size, strength, and inevitability rather than precision. Prioritize Strength or Constitution as the primary ability score. Wisdom is a useful secondary: the Juggernaut is patient, not reckless.

4. **Distribute damage across categories with Juggernaut priorities:**
   - **Actions:** High damage, low frequency. The primary strike should hit well above curve when it lands. The secondary should hit at curve. Average these together for your DPR calculation, weighted by how often each lands.
   - **Bonus Actions:** Moderate damage on the pressure ability. The sustain bonus action deals no direct damage — account for this when averaging. The Juggernaut's Bonus Action DPR will appear lower than other archetypes; this is correct.
   - **Reactions:** The punishment reaction deals minimal damage. The sustain reaction deals none. The Juggernaut's reaction DPR is the lowest of any archetype — its reactions are informational, not offensive.

5. **Assign attack damage with the loop break in mind.** When the party breaks the sustain loop, the Juggernaut's effective DPR should not change — but the encounter's math flips completely. The same primary strike that felt catastrophic when the party couldn't net progress now feels manageable, because they can now outlast it. No mechanical adjustment is needed at the moment of the loop break. The shift is entirely in the party's resource situation and their ability to respond to the telegraph.

---

### The Juggernaut's Tell

Every Juggernaut stat block must have one. Write it in plain language before finalizing the stat block, then make sure it is visible in play within two rounds:

> *"This Juggernaut's sustain loop triggers when [specific observable condition]. Players who [disrupt this] will find the damage they are dealing finally sticks."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the puzzle is not solvable through play. Go back to the sustain bonus action and make the trigger more specific, more visible, and more attributable. A loop players cannot see is not a puzzle — it is a mystery that will feel like cheating when it is finally revealed.

## The Executioner

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the party is being ground down by volume they cannot absorb indefinitely. The puzzle is not how to survive the hits — it is how to make the hits stop.**

---

### 1. High Concept

An Executioner's high concept must communicate relentless, mechanical pressure — not raw power, but *pace*. The Executioner is not a creature that hits the hardest in the room. It is a creature that never stops hitting, and that distinction is what makes it dangerous.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what does this creature's attack routine look like from the outside?* It should feel like a machine, a system, a pattern — something that operates on its own logic regardless of what the party does. The puzzle is finding the wrench that jams the works.

**Strong examples:** "The Unceasing Blade of the Iron Tribunal," "Inevitable Executioner of the Crimson Court," "The Mechanism That Does Not Rest."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Fast Attacker," "High Accuracy Fighter," "Multi-Attack Boss." These describe statistics, not identity. The high concept should evoke the feeling of fighting something that simply will not stop — not a creature with good numbers.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Executioner template asks a specific question before anything else: **what is the attack routine, and what disrupts it?**

Organize your tools into three categories before writing a single stat block entry:

**Volume Tools** — what generates the constant attack pressure.
*(Examples: a Multiattack with more hits than is typical for its CR, abilities that trigger additional attacks on specific conditions, bonus action strikes that fire reliably every round. The volume should feel sustainable — the Executioner is not burning resources to maintain its pressure, it is simply operating.)*

**Lock-down Tools** — what makes it hard for the party to act while absorbing the volume.
*(Examples: conditions imposed on hit that impose disadvantage on attacks or saves, abilities that interrupt the party's action economy, positioning effects that keep targets in the Executioner's optimal range. This is the Access Strength — the party is not just getting hit, they are getting hit while struggling to respond.)*

**Pattern Tools** — what gives the routine visible structure that players can read and disrupt.
*(Examples: the Executioner always uses a specific ability before its main strike, always targets the same condition, always approaches from the same direction. The pattern is what makes the puzzle solvable. Without it, disrupting the Executioner feels like luck rather than strategy.)*

The tell that players must discover is embedded in the pattern tools: the Executioner's routine has a shape, and that shape has a seam. Players who find the seam — the moment when the machine is between cycles, the condition it cannot maintain its volume through, the positioning that breaks its targeting logic — have found the solution.

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Executioner's action economy is built around **sustained above-average hit frequency at below-average damage per hit**, creating cumulative pressure that cannot be outlasted without disrupting the source.

#### Actions

**The Executioner should have:**

- A **primary strike** that is fast, accurate, and deals below-curve damage per hit. This attack should feel almost routine — not telegraphed, not dramatic, just reliable. Its danger is not what it does on any single hit but what it does across six hits in a round. The attack bonus should be at or above curve; the damage die should be modest.

- A **secondary strike** at a different range — slightly less accurate than the primary, with a debilitating condition on hit rather than additional damage. The condition should make the party's next round harder: Blinded, Slowed, Frightened, or a penalty to their attack rolls or saving throws. This is the Access Strength made concrete — the Executioner is not just dealing damage, it is making the party's responses less effective.

- A **Multiattack** that uses the primary strike more times than standard for its CR — three or four hits rather than two — with the option to substitute one primary strike for the secondary. The Executioner's Multiattack is the defining mechanical statement of the archetype: the number of hits per round is the puzzle's pressure, and every round the party fails to disrupt it, that pressure compounds.

> **Executioner-specific note:** The secondary strike's condition should interact with the primary. A party member who is Blinded is easier to hit with the primary on subsequent turns. A party member who has disadvantage on saves is more likely to fail the secondary's condition. The routine feeds itself — which is exactly what makes disrupting it feel decisive when the party finally manages it.

#### Bonus Actions

The Executioner's Bonus Actions are its **routine maintenance tools** — what it uses to keep the pressure cycle running between its main attacks and to close off the gaps that would otherwise give the party breathing room.

- A **gap-closer or reset bonus action** that keeps the Executioner in optimal range for its primary strike. This might be a movement ability, a forced repositioning of a target, or a condition that prevents a target from creating distance. The Executioner's volume only works if it can keep attacking — a party that creates distance has found a partial solution, and this bonus action is what punishes them for it without fully negating the strategy.

- A **pressure bonus action** that either adds an additional hit outside the Multiattack or deepens the condition imposed by the secondary strike. This ability should feel like the routine is overflowing — one more thing the party has to account for on top of everything else. It should not hit as hard as the primary, but it should contribute to the cumulative toll.

> **On pattern visibility:** One of these Bonus Actions should have a consistent tell in how the DM describes it. The Executioner uses it at the same point in its routine every round — after the first primary strike, or whenever a specific condition is in play, or whenever it has a target within a specific range. Players who track the tell have found the seam in the machine.

#### Reactions

The Executioner has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These close off the gaps that the party's action economy would otherwise create — and in doing so, they teach players that passive survival is not the solution.

**Required:**

- **A volume reaction** *(trigger: a creature within the Executioner's reach takes any action other than making an attack roll against the Executioner).* The Executioner immediately makes one primary strike against that creature. This reaction punishes the party for doing anything other than fighting back — healing, casting a utility spell, using an item, attempting to create distance. It is not a retaliation reaction; it is the routine extending itself into gaps it was not supposed to occupy.

- **A lock-down reaction** *(trigger: a creature succeeds on a saving throw against one of the Executioner's abilities, or a condition imposed by the Executioner ends on a creature).* The Executioner immediately imposes the secondary strike's condition on that creature again, or imposes disadvantage on their next attack roll. The party cannot simply shake off the Executioner's debuffs and return to baseline — the routine reasserts itself.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A pursuit reaction** *(trigger: a creature moves more than 10 feet away from the Executioner).* The Executioner moves up to half its speed toward that creature without provoking opportunity attacks. Creating distance is a partial solution — but it must be maintained, and the Executioner will close it every time it is offered.
- **A cycle reset** *(trigger: a creature ends its turn without having imposed a condition or dealt damage to the Executioner).* The Executioner gains a bonus to its next attack roll equal to the number of consecutive turns this has occurred, maximum +3. The routine rewards uninterrupted operation — every round a creature fails to slow it down, the next round becomes slightly harder.
- **An interrupt** *(trigger: a creature attempts to impose a condition on the Executioner that would reduce its hit frequency — Blinded, Restrained, or Paralyzed).* The Executioner makes a saving throw with advantage. If it succeeds, the condition is reduced to one round's duration regardless of the original effect. The machine resists being jammed; it just does not resist being jammed forever.

> **On the volume reaction:** This is the Executioner's most important reaction. It is also the most likely to feel unfair if the trigger is too broad. Restrict it carefully — "any action other than making an attack roll against the Executioner" is the ceiling; narrowing it to a specific action type (casting a spell, using an item, taking the Help action) is also valid. The reaction should feel like the Executioner exploiting a gap in a creature's attention, not like an arbitrary punishment.

---

### 4. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Executioner's CR calibration follows the standard process with one Executioner-specific adjustment: **the Executioner's effective DPR is higher than its per-hit damage suggests.**

Because the Executioner lands more hits per round than standard for its CR, the aggregate damage across a full round significantly exceeds what any single strike implies. When calibrating, always calculate DPR from the full Multiattack sequence — not from individual hit values.

**Steps:**


1. **Set hit frequency above curve, damage per hit below curve.** A standard CR X creature might make two attacks at Y damage each. The Executioner makes three or four attacks at 60–70% of Y damage each. The total DPR should match or slightly exceed standard for its CR; the distribution across more, weaker hits is the mechanical expression of the archetype.

2. **Set the attack bonus at or above curve.** This is the inverse of the Juggernaut. The Executioner hits often and reliably; it is the per-hit payoff that is modest. Do not reduce the attack bonus to compensate for the higher number of attacks — that turns the Executioner into a miss machine rather than a pressure machine.

3. **Assign ability scores from the primary strike's to-hit bonus.** Prioritize Dexterity or the ability score governing its primary attack. The Executioner is precise and fast, not strong. Constitution is a useful secondary — the Executioner is durable enough to keep working, not tough enough to absorb punishment indefinitely.

4. **Distribute damage across categories with Executioner priorities:**
   - **Actions:** The bulk of the DPR lives here, spread across three or four hits. Per-hit numbers will look low on paper — this is correct. Calculate total expected damage from the full Multiattack and verify against CR benchmarks, not individual hit values.
   - **Bonus Actions:** Moderate additional damage or a condition deepening. The Executioner's Bonus Actions are not its primary damage vector — they are routine extensions. Their damage contribution should be meaningful but not dominant.
   - **Reactions:** The volume reaction adds one primary strike's worth of damage when it triggers — account for this in expected DPR by estimating how often per round it fires. Against a typical party, assume it fires once per round. Against a party that has learned to fight it cleanly, assume less.

5. **Assign attack damage with disruption in mind.** When the party successfully disrupts the Executioner's routine — imposing disadvantage on its attacks, creating distance it cannot close, breaking the conditions feeding its secondary strike — its DPR should drop sharply. This is the Weakness (Attrition) expressed mechanically: the Executioner that cannot land hits has no threat remaining. The collapse should feel total, not gradual.

---

### The Executioner's Tell

Every Executioner stat block must have one. Write it in plain language before finalizing the stat block, then make sure it is visible in play within two rounds:

> *"This Executioner's routine has a seam at [specific observable moment or condition]. Players who [exploit this] will find the volume drops immediately and decisively."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the routine has no structure — it is just a damage dealer. Go back to the pattern tools and the Bonus Action sequence and build in a consistent, observable beat that players can disrupt. The Executioner is a machine. Machines have gears. Players need to be able to see where to put the wrench.

## The Fortress

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the party's damage is not landing, and something bad is happening while they figure out why. The puzzle is not how to hit harder — it is how to hit differently.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A Fortress's high concept must communicate impregnability — not threat, not aggression, but the sense that conventional force simply does not apply here. The Fortress is not a creature that fights well. It is a creature that cannot be fought the way the party wants to fight it.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what does this creature's defense look like from the outside?* It should be visible and physical — an aura of negation, a wall of layered resistances, an exterior that simply absorbs everything thrown at it. The puzzle is finding the gap in that exterior, and the gap should feel earned when the party finds it.

**Strong examples:** "The Ironclad Sentinel of the Undying Court," "The Siege That Walks," "That Which Has Not Yet Been Scratched."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "High AC Boss," "Immune to Magic," "Hard to Kill." These describe statistics, not identity. The high concept should communicate *why* conventional damage fails — the fiction behind the defense is what makes the solution feel satisfying rather than arbitrary.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Fortress template asks a specific question before anything else: **what makes conventional damage fail, and what is the specific condition that makes it work?**

Organize your tools into three categories before writing a single stat block entry:

**Defense Tools** — what makes the Fortress impenetrable to the wrong approach.
*(Examples: resistance to all damage that isn't a specific type, an AC so high that standard attack bonuses cannot reliably connect, a trait that converts a portion of all incoming damage to a negligible type, a threshold below which damage simply does not register. Critically: the defense must be specific enough to have a solution. "Immune to everything" is not a puzzle — it is a locked door. "Immune to everything except attacks made while the creature is Prone" is a puzzle.)*

**Pressure Tools** — the secondary threat that ticks upward while the party fails to crack the defense.
*(Examples: an escalating aura that deals damage at the start of each creature's turn, a stacking debuff applied whenever the party deals damage that the Fortress resists, a compounding condition that worsens by one step each round the Fortress is not below half HP. This is the Access Strength — and it must be clearly attributable to the Fortress itself, not the environment.)*

**Offense Tools** — the slow, telegraphed attacks that create immediate danger without being the encounter's primary threat.
*(Examples: a massive single-target strike with a long wind-up, an area denial that takes effect at the start of the Fortress's next turn rather than immediately, a grab that sets up a devastating follow-up but can be interrupted between setup and execution. These attacks look like the danger. They are not. They are what the party dodges while solving the real problem.)*

The tell that players must discover is embedded in the defense tools: the Fortress's resistance or immunity has a specific condition attached to it, and that condition is observable. It flinches when a specific damage type connects. Its aura flickers when approached from a certain direction. The trait that converts damage has a visible tell when it triggers. Players who track that tell for two rounds have the information they need.

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Fortress's action economy is built around **defensive presence and escalating secondary pressure**, not offensive output. Its attacks exist to demand attention, not to define the encounter.

#### Actions

**The Fortress should have:**

- A **primary strike** that is slow, heavily telegraphed, and hits for at or above-curve damage when it lands — but uses a saving throw rather than an attack roll, or requires the Fortress to forgo movement, or has some other visible setup cost. The party can see it coming. Failing to respond is a choice. The strike should impose a condition on a failed save in addition to damage — Stunned, Prone, Restrained — something that compounds the secondary pressure if the party is already struggling.

- A **secondary strike** that is faster, weaker, and used as a fallback when the primary is on cooldown or a target is out of range. This attack should impose forced movement: pushing the target out of an optimal position, into the aura's range, or away from an environmental feature the party might be using to their advantage.

- A **Multiattack** that lets the Fortress use either strike twice, or both strikes once. Unlike the Juggernaut, the Fortress should spread its attacks across targets — it is not focused on destroying one creature, it is holding the entire party at bay. The Multiattack exists to keep the party busy dodging while the secondary pressure builds.

> **Fortress-specific note:** The primary strike's wind-up is information delivery as much as it is a mechanical courtesy. A Fortress that always winds up its primary against the nearest creature tells the party that staying close is dangerous. One that always winds up against whoever last dealt damage that bypassed its defenses teaches the party that the correct approach has a cost, and they need to prepare for it. The targeting logic of the primary defines the tactical geometry the party must navigate while solving the real problem.

#### Bonus Actions

The Fortress's Bonus Actions are its **pressure escalation and defense reinforcement tools** — what ensures the secondary threat keeps ticking even when the party thinks they've found breathing room.

- A **pressure escalation bonus action** that advances the secondary threat by one step, extends its range, or applies it to a new target. This ability fires every round regardless of what the party does — it is the clock that cannot be paused, only solved. If the secondary threat is an aura, this ability might expand the aura's radius. If it is a stacking debuff, this ability might apply another stack to a creature not yet affected. The Fortress does not need to work hard to keep the pressure building — it simply persists.

- A **defense reinforcement bonus action** that briefly heightens one aspect of the Fortress's resistance or resets a defense the party has partially bypassed. This ability should be limited — once per phase, or requiring a specific condition — because its purpose is not to make the puzzle impossible but to ensure the party cannot solve it cheaply with a single well-timed ability. It should feel like the Fortress closing a gap it noticed being probed, not like an arbitrary reset.

> **On the reinforcement bonus action:** The Juggernaut's sustain bonus action teaches players the loop exists. The Fortress's reinforcement bonus action teaches players the solution window exists — but is finite. Players who see the defense reinforce will understand that the puzzle has a timing element: the correct approach must not just be found, it must be executed before the window closes.

#### Reactions

The Fortress has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the secondary pressure compounds and where the cost of the wrong approach is most directly communicated.

**Required:**

- **A pressure response reaction** *(trigger: a creature deals damage to the Fortress that the Fortress resists or is immune to).* The secondary threat immediately advances — the aura pulses outward, the debuff applies an additional stack to the attacker, the escalating condition worsens by one step on the nearest creature. The message is immediate and legible: *that approach is feeding the clock.* This reaction should not deal direct damage — it should worsen the party's situation in a way that is attributable and observable.

- **A defense statement reaction** *(trigger: a creature reduces the Fortress below a specific HP threshold for the first time).* The Fortress visibly reinforces — regaining a portion of the HP lost in that hit, briefly restoring a resistance that was bypassed, or applying its secondary pressure effect to every creature in range simultaneously. This reaction communicates that progress has been noticed, that the Fortress is responding, and that the party must sustain their correct approach rather than assume one good hit is enough. It should fire exactly once per phase.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A punishing return** *(trigger: a creature deals damage to the Fortress that bypasses its defenses).* The Fortress retaliates not with raw damage but with forced repositioning: the attacker is pushed, pulled, or teleported to a position that makes following up harder. The solution is real — but it has a cost. Players must account for that cost when planning their execution.
- **A threat reminder** *(trigger: a creature ends its turn more than 30 feet from the Fortress).* The Fortress extends its secondary pressure to reach that creature, or makes its primary strike available against targets at extended range for one round. The party cannot solve the puzzle from a safe distance — the Fortress brings the problem to them.
- **An adaptation** *(trigger: a creature deals damage to the Fortress using the same damage type as the previous hit against it).* The Fortress gains resistance to that damage type until the start of its next turn. Solving the puzzle is not enough — the party must vary their approach within the solution or the Fortress will close the gap they found. This option works best for multi-phase Fortresses where adaptation is a narrative theme.

> **On the pressure response reaction:** This is the Fortress's most important reaction for communicating the puzzle. Every time it fires, it must be narrated clearly — "the creature absorbs the lightning bolt and the air around it shimmers darker; two of you feel the cold worsen" or "the fire washes off its surface and the floor beneath you cracks further." Players who cannot see this reaction happening cannot learn from it. Write the ability so the DM has an obvious cue every time it triggers.

---

### 4. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Fortress's CR calibration follows the standard process with two Fortress-specific adjustments: **the Fortress's effective HP is higher than its stat block suggests, and its effective DPR is lower.**

The defense inflates durability beyond what HP and AC numbers imply, because a significant portion of the party's damage is being resisted or negated during the puzzle phase. The slow, telegraphed attacks reduce effective DPR below what the numbers imply, because the party can respond to them once they are no longer focused entirely on the penetration problem. Both adjustments must be accounted for simultaneously.

**Steps:**


1. **Set HP and AC both above baseline, but not simultaneously maxed.** The Fortress's durability comes primarily from its resistances and immunities, not from raw HP or AC numbers alone. A Fortress with very high AC *and* very high HP *and* strong resistances will feel unkillable even after the party solves the penetration puzzle. Choose one:
   - High AC, standard HP — the Fortress is hard to hit, but once the party is dealing full damage, it falls in a reasonable number of rounds.
   - Standard AC, high HP — the Fortress is easy to hit once the defenses are cracked, but there is a lot of it left. This option pairs better with the Weakness (Endurance — attacks become easy to avoid) because the party's ability to dodge the slow attacks feels more meaningful when many rounds remain.

2. **Define the defense's magnitude deliberately.** The penetration wall must be strong enough to feel unsolvable by the wrong approach but not so total that the right approach feels like a minor upgrade. A useful baseline: during the puzzle phase, the Fortress should be taking 30–50% of the damage it would take from an unresisted attack. Below 30%, the puzzle phase drags. Above 50%, players may not notice the wall exists.

3. **Set attack stats below curve in frequency, at curve in potency.** The Fortress's attacks are slow and telegraphed by design — they should fire less frequently than standard for its CR and rely on the secondary pressure to create real danger. The damage when they land should be at or above curve, because the party has warning and choosing not to respond is a meaningful failure. A Fortress that also deals relentless offensive pressure is not a Fortress — it is a boss with a frustrating gimmick.

4. **Distribute damage across categories with Fortress priorities:**
   - **Actions:** At or above curve on the primary when it lands, but weighted by its low fire rate. The secondary strike is below curve. The Fortress's action DPR will look low on paper — this is correct.
   - **Bonus Actions:** Both Bonus Actions deal no direct damage. The Fortress's Bonus Action DPR is zero — all of its Bonus Action budget is spent on pressure escalation and defense maintenance. Treat the secondary pressure as an equivalent DPR contribution: estimate how much HP the party loses per round to the escalating effect and add it to the Fortress's effective DPR total.
   - **Reactions:** Both required reactions deal no direct damage. The Fortress's reaction DPR is zero. Like the Juggernaut, its reactions are entirely informational — communicating the puzzle rather than adding to the damage total.

5. **Assign attack damage with the penetration solution in mind.** When the party finds the correct approach, the Fortress's effective DPR does not change — but the encounter's texture changes entirely. The attacks that were background noise while the party bounced off the defenses are now the only thing that matters, and they are slow enough to dodge. The Weakness opens not because the Fortress becomes weaker but because the party finally has the attention to apply to it.

---

### The Fortress's Tell

Every Fortress stat block must have one. Write it in plain language before finalizing the stat block, then make sure it is visible in play within two rounds:

> *"This Fortress's defenses fail when [specific observable condition]. Players who [execute this] will find their damage landing — and the attacks they've been dodging suddenly feel manageable."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the penetration puzzle has no solution — it is just a resilient boss. Go back to the defense tools and make the specific bypass condition more concrete, more observable, and more actionable. The wall must have a gate. Players need to be able to see it.

---

## The Phantom

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the party can see the boss, can target it, can even reach it — but their hits do not land, and every time they get close to solving why, the boss withdraws and forces them to start over.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A Phantom's high concept must communicate elusiveness through artistry rather than absence. The Phantom is not invisible, not intangible, not hidden behind walls. It is *present*, fully visible, often within arm's reach — and yet contact fails to land. The puzzle is finding why, and exploiting the window before it slips away again.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what kind of fighter cannot be hit by people who should obviously be able to hit them?* The answer lives in technique, presence, or showmanship — not in stealth or mobility alone. The Phantom is a duelist who refuses the duel on the party's terms.

**Strong examples:** "The Laughing Blade of the Crimson Court," "The Duelist Who Was Never There," "The Trickster of the Veiled Hand."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Evasive Boss," "High AC Fighter," "Always Misses." These describe statistics, not identity. The high concept should communicate *style* — the manner of evasion is what makes the encounter feel like fighting a specific kind of opponent, not a stat block with a high number.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Phantom template asks a specific question before anything else: **what makes attacks fail to connect, and what makes the Phantom withdraw before the party can sustain pressure?**

Organize your tools into three categories before writing a single stat block entry:

**Deflection Tools** — what makes attacks fail to connect when the Phantom is engaged.
*(Examples: a parry reaction that imposes disadvantage or outright negates a hit, a reactive teleport that displaces the Phantom a short distance when an attack would land, an AC inflated to a value standard attacks struggle to clear. Critically: the deflection must have a specific tell — a flourish, a gesture, a visible technique — so players can identify what is causing their attacks to fail, and what would bypass it.)*

**Withdrawal Tools** — what allows the Phantom to break contact when the party is close to solving the deflection.
*(Examples: a movement ability that creates immediate distance, a smokescreen or illusion that breaks line of sight, a vanishing effect that places the Phantom at a known but inaccessible location. The withdrawal must be telegraphed and patterned — the Phantom always withdraws under specific conditions, and players who track those conditions can predict and prepare for them.)*

**Reappearance Tools** — what brings the Phantom back into engagement on its terms.
*(Examples: an entrance attack that strikes from the new position, a returning condition that forces the party to track the Phantom's location across the room, an opening flourish that grants advantage on its first attack after returning. The reappearance is what creates the rhythm — withdraw, reappear, strike, withdraw again — and that rhythm is exactly what the party must learn to exploit.)*

The tell that players must discover is embedded in the deflection and withdrawal tools: the Phantom's parry has a setup, the withdrawal has a trigger, the reappearance has a pattern. Players who track these for two rounds will see the rhythm — and once they see it, they can use the recovery windows the Phantom is giving them to prepare the strike it cannot answer.

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Phantom's action economy is built around **selective, high-quality strikes interspersed with disengagements**, not sustained pressure. The Phantom does not need to hit often — it needs the party to feel that contact only happens on its terms.

#### Actions

**The Phantom should have:**

- A **primary strike** that is accurate, deals at-curve damage, and is used selectively — typically on the Phantom's first turn after reappearing, against an isolated or unprepared target. This attack should feel like a punctuation mark, not a routine — the Phantom does not flail at the party every round, it picks its moment and lands the moment it picks. The strike should impose a condition that interferes with follow-up: forced movement that breaks formation, disadvantage on the target's next attack, or a brief Slowed effect that makes pursuit harder.

- A **secondary strike** that is faster and lower-damage, used to maintain presence between primary strikes. This attack should be the Phantom's "showing off" move — a flourish, a passing cut, a contemptuous gesture. It deals less damage than the primary but reinforces the impression that the Phantom is in complete control of the engagement.

- A **Multiattack** that lets the Phantom use either strike twice, or both strikes once. The Phantom should rarely use Multiattack to focus a single target — its identity is about controlling space and selecting moments, not about destroying individual party members. When it uses Multiattack against two targets, both should be telegraphed: the Phantom is choosing who to engage, and that choice should be legible.

> **Phantom-specific note:** The Phantom should have noticeably fewer attacks per round than other archetypes of its CR. This is the rhythm made mechanical — the Phantom does not need to attack every round to be threatening. A round where it only withdraws and reappears can be more menacing than a round where it lands two hits, because the party is forced to react to repositioning rather than to incoming damage. Resist the urge to add more attacks to "balance" the encounter. The rhythm is the encounter.

#### Bonus Actions

The Phantom's Bonus Actions are its **rhythm maintenance tools** — what creates and reinforces the cycle of engagement and withdrawal that defines the encounter.

- A **withdrawal bonus action** that lets the Phantom move away from contact without provoking opportunity attacks, breaking line of sight, or teleporting to a known location. This ability should have a visible trigger — used after landing a primary strike, used when adjacent to two or more creatures, used when below a specific HP threshold. The trigger is the tell. Players who identify it can anticipate the withdrawal and prepare for the reappearance.

- A **reappearance bonus action** that activates when the Phantom returns to engagement after withdrawing — typically a short-range repositioning combined with the secondary strike, or a brief buff to its next attack roll. The reappearance is the Phantom's most dangerous moment for the party — it is when the rhythm restarts and the next primary strike is set up. But the reappearance is also the moment of greatest vulnerability for the Phantom, because its position is briefly fixed and its withdrawal is on cooldown.

> **On the withdrawal-reappearance cycle:** These two bonus actions should ideally be linked — one cannot be used without the other being available, and the use of one signals that the other will follow within a turn or two. This is the encounter's central rhythm. If the bonus actions feel disconnected, the rhythm dissolves and the Phantom becomes a generic evasive boss. The link is what makes it an archetype.

#### Reactions

The Phantom has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the Access wall lives mechanically — the moments where attacks should have connected but did not, and the Phantom's signature parries and deflections fire.

**Required:**

- **A deflection reaction** *(trigger: a creature hits the Phantom with an attack).* The Phantom imposes disadvantage on the attack roll, forces a re-roll, or treats the attack as having missed entirely if the original roll was below a specific threshold. This is the Access wall — the reason hits fail to connect. The reaction should have a clear narrative cue: the Phantom parries with a flourish, sidesteps with a laugh, deflects the strike with a contemptuous flick of the blade. Players must be able to see the reaction firing so they can identify the pattern of when it fires and when it does not.

- **A withdrawal reaction** *(trigger: the Phantom takes damage that breaches a specific threshold in a single hit, or a creature deals damage to the Phantom on consecutive turns).* The Phantom triggers its withdrawal bonus action immediately, ending its current engagement and resetting the rhythm. This reaction is the urgency mechanism — every time the party is close to landing sustained damage, the Phantom withdraws. The recovery window opens here.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A counterstroke** *(trigger: a creature misses the Phantom with an attack).* The Phantom makes one secondary strike against the attacker. This option emphasizes the Phantom's contempt — every missed attack is an invitation to be cut. It also gives the deflection reaction more bite: an attack that the Phantom deflects is one that just barely misses, and that miss is itself dangerous.
- **A misdirection** *(trigger: a creature targets the Phantom with an ability that requires a saving throw or attack roll).* The Phantom and one other creature within 5 feet of it swap places. The original target of the ability is now whoever ended up in the Phantom's space — often an ally. This option emphasizes the Phantom as trickster: the party's own attacks become tools against them. Use sparingly and only when the high concept supports it — this can feel unfair if overused.
- **A patron's bow** *(trigger: a creature reduces the Phantom below half HP for the first time).* The Phantom immediately uses its withdrawal bonus action and gains advantage on its next primary strike. The withdrawal here is not retreat — it is theatre. The Phantom acknowledges the hit, withdraws with a salute, and returns more dangerous than before. This option works best when the Phantom has a strong personality and the encounter is paced around dramatic beats.

> **On the deflection reaction:** This is the Phantom's most important reaction for communicating the puzzle. It must fire visibly and often — at least once per round, possibly more — so the party understands that their attacks are being actively answered, not merely missing. Narrate each trigger differently if possible: the Phantom parries, sidesteps, taunts, deflects with the off-hand. The variety reinforces that the Phantom is a fighter with technique, not a creature with a high AC stat.

---

### 4. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Phantom's CR calibration follows the standard process with one Phantom-specific adjustment: **the Phantom's effective HP is significantly higher than its stat block suggests, but only during the puzzle phase.**

Because the deflection reaction prevents a meaningful portion of the party's hits from landing, and because the withdrawal cycle imposes downtime in which the Phantom cannot be targeted at all, the Phantom functions as though it has substantially more HP than written during the period before the party solves the Access puzzle. Once they solve it, the effective HP collapses to roughly its written value.

**Steps:**


1. **Set AC high but HP at or below baseline.** The Phantom's durability lives in its reactions and its withdrawal rhythm, not in raw HP. A Phantom with high HP feels grindy even after the Access puzzle is solved — the party finally lands hits, and there are still many rounds of fight left. Setting HP at or slightly below baseline ensures the encounter ends decisively once the Weakness opens. The Phantom is a fencer, not a tank.

2. **Define the deflection's magnitude deliberately.** The deflection reaction must negate enough hits to feel like a real wall but not so many that the party feels they cannot connect at all. A useful baseline: the deflection reaction should reduce the party's effective hit rate by roughly 30–40% during the puzzle phase. This typically means it fires once or twice per round on three to four attacks made against the Phantom. Higher than that and the party feels powerless; lower than that and the puzzle dissolves into normal combat.

3. **Set attack stats at curve for the primary, below curve in frequency.** The Phantom hits hard when it hits, but it hits selectively. The primary strike's attack bonus and damage should be at curve for its CR; the rate at which the Phantom uses the primary should be below baseline — typically once per round, with the withdrawal cycle imposing rounds where it does not attack at all. The total expected DPR will look low for the CR. This is correct — the Phantom's threat is positional, not damage-based.

4. **Assign ability scores from the primary strike's to-hit and the deflection's Save DC (if applicable).** Dexterity should be the primary ability score. Charisma is a useful secondary — the Phantom is a performer, and that performance is part of the encounter's threat. Constitution should be average to slightly below — the Phantom is not built to absorb punishment.

5. **Distribute damage across categories with Phantom priorities:**
   - **Actions:** Below-curve overall DPR, weighted by the Phantom's reduced fire rate. The primary strike's per-hit damage is at curve; the frequency at which it is used is below curve. Average across expected use.
   - **Bonus Actions:** The withdrawal bonus action deals no damage. The reappearance bonus action may deal damage (paired with the secondary strike) or may simply reposition. The Phantom's Bonus Action DPR is low — most of its bonus action budget goes to the rhythm itself.
   - **Reactions:** The deflection reaction deals no damage but contributes substantially to effective HP. The withdrawal reaction deals no damage. The optional third reaction may deal damage (counterstroke) or may be positional (misdirection, patron's bow). Account for the deflection's effective HP contribution when calibrating against CR benchmarks — the Phantom's actual HP value will look low without it.

6. **Assign attack damage with the recovery window in mind.** The Phantom's Weakness is Attrition — the party's recovery during withdrawal phases outpaces the Phantom's damage output over the course of the encounter. This means the Phantom's per-round damage budget must be modest enough that a healer can keep pace once the Access puzzle is solved. If the Phantom hits too hard during its primary strikes, the recovery windows are not enough to compensate and the Weakness fails to land. Err on the side of slightly under-tuning the primary's damage rather than over-tuning it.

---

### The Phantom's Tell

Every Phantom stat block must have one. Write it in plain language before finalizing the stat block, then make sure it is visible in play within two rounds:

> *"This Phantom's deflection fails when [specific observable condition], and it withdraws when [specific observable trigger]. Players who [time their commitment to the recovery window] will find their damage landing — and the rhythm tipping in their favor with each cycle."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the Access puzzle has no readable solution and the rhythm has no exploitable pattern — the Phantom is just a high-AC boss that runs away. Go back to the deflection reaction and the withdrawal bonus action and define their triggers with enough specificity that the DM can narrate them consistently. The Phantom is theatre. The choreography must be visible from the front row.

---

## The Shield Master

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the party's attacks are not landing, and the ones that do are coming back at them. The puzzle is finding the angle the guard cannot cover — and exploiting it before the counter resolves.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A Shield Master's high concept must communicate absolute positional commitment — the immovability of something that has decided this ground is where the fight ends. The Shield Master is not fast, not elusive, not adaptable. It is *planted*. Everything it does begins and ends from the same spot, and that refusal to move is both its identity and its eventual undoing.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what does this creature defend, and how does it defend it?* The defense should be inseparable from its purpose. A Shield Master is not just a fighter with a high AC — it is a sentinel, a champion, a sworn protector of a specific place or principle. The guard is an expression of vow.

**Strong examples:** "The Sworn Bulwark of the Ember Throne," "The Last Wall of the Forgotten Order," "The Champion Who Will Not Step Back."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Defensive Boss," "Reactive Counterattacker," "High AC Tank." These describe statistics, not identity. The high concept should communicate *why* the Shield Master stands its ground — the conviction behind the guard is what makes the encounter feel like a confrontation rather than a stat check.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Shield Master template asks a specific question before anything else: **what is the guard, what does it cover, and what is the specific angle it cannot answer?**

Organize your tools into three categories before writing a single stat block entry:

**Guard Tools** — what intercepts attacks before they connect.
*(Examples: a reaction that imposes disadvantage on attacks made within the Shield Master's front arc, a parry that converts incoming damage into temporary HP, a stance that grants resistance to all damage from creatures within reach, a defensive trait that makes the first attack against the Shield Master each turn miss automatically. Critically: the guard must have a specific angular or positional condition. The Shield Master is not invulnerable in all directions — it is invulnerable from the directions it expects.)*

**Counter Tools** — what punishes attacks that fail to breach the guard.
*(Examples: a reaction strike against any creature whose attack the guard intercepts, reflected damage equal to a portion of what the guard absorbed, a counterstrike that imposes a condition on the attacker. The counter is the Strength made mechanical — the wrong approach does not merely fail, it actively harms the party.)*

**Anchor Tools** — what binds the Shield Master to its position and creates the threat the party must approach.
*(Examples: a zone of control that imposes opportunity attacks on any creature attempting to move past it, an aura that buffs allies or debuffs enemies within range, a sworn target the Shield Master will not allow to be approached. The anchor is what makes the Shield Master matter as an encounter element — it cannot simply be ignored, because something behind or beside it must be reached or defended.)*

The tell that players must discover is embedded in the guard tools: the Shield Master's defense has a specific blind spot, a specific damage type it cannot intercept, or a specific condition under which the guard drops. The tell must be observable from the first round — players who watch carefully should see the Shield Master turning to face new threats, repositioning its shield, adjusting its stance. The blind spot is whichever direction it cannot adjust to in time.

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Shield Master's action economy is built around **defensive presence augmented by punishing counter-pressure**, not offensive initiative. The Shield Master does not press the attack — it answers attacks made against it.

#### Actions

**The Shield Master should have:**

- A **primary strike** that is reliable, at-curve damage, and used against any creature that has remained within its reach for a full round — the punishing answer to anyone who tries to grind it down in melee. This attack should impose a condition that reinforces the Shield Master's positional control: Prone, forced movement back to a specific distance, or a brief Grappled effect that pins the target in place while the counter window opens. The strike says: *you stood here, and that was a mistake.*

- A **secondary strike** that is shorter-range and lower-damage, used to maintain pressure on creatures who have just attacked and missed, or who are attempting to leave the Shield Master's threatened zone. This attack should feel like a punctuation mark — a sharp jab from the shield's edge, a quick chop from the sword that follows up the parry, a forceful step that catches a retreating foe. It reinforces that disengaging from the Shield Master is dangerous, even briefly.

- A **Multiattack** that lets the Shield Master use either strike twice, or both strikes once. The Shield Master should rarely use Multiattack to focus a single target unless that target has been clearly identified as the threat to its anchor. Otherwise, attacks should be spread across creatures who have most recently struck the Shield Master — this reinforces the reactive identity. The Shield Master answers attackers, not selects targets.

> **Shield Master-specific note:** The primary strike's frequency should be lower than its CR baseline. The Shield Master's threat is the counter, not the strike. A Shield Master that hits hard every round on its own initiative feels like a regular bruiser with a high AC — the encounter loses its archetypal shape. Trim the offensive output and let the reactions carry the damage.

#### Bonus Actions

The Shield Master's Bonus Actions are its **stance maintenance and anchor expression tools** — what reinforces its position and communicates that the ground beneath it is the encounter's center of gravity.

- A **stance bonus action** that briefly enhances one aspect of the guard — granting resistance to a chosen damage type until the start of its next turn, increasing its AC by a small amount against the next attack, or extending its intercept reach by 5 feet. This ability should fire reliably every round and should be visible: the Shield Master shifts its grip, plants its feet, raises the shield to a new angle. The bonus action is the tell. Players who watch the stance change can sometimes predict which direction or damage type the guard will not cover this round.

- An **anchor expression bonus action** that reinforces the Shield Master's positional purpose — an attack of opportunity-style ability against any creature attempting to pass it, a defensive shout that grants temporary HP to a nearby ally, a sworn declaration that imposes disadvantage on attacks targeting the anchor (whether that anchor is an object, ally, or location). This ability should communicate clearly that the Shield Master is not simply defending itself — it is defending *something*, and the party will not pass.

> **On the stance bonus action:** This is the Shield Master's communication channel for the puzzle. If the guard's blind spot rotates between rounds, the stance bonus action is what signals the rotation. If the guard has a fixed weakness that the party must discover, the stance bonus action is what visually confirms the guard is reset and intact. The narration of the stance change carries the information players need to solve the encounter.

#### Reactions

The Shield Master has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the archetype's mechanical identity lives most fully — the guard, the counter, and the consequence of attacking wrongly.

**Required:**

- **A guard reaction** *(trigger: a creature hits the Shield Master with an attack roll while the Shield Master is conscious and not Restrained, Stunned, or Prone).* The Shield Master imposes disadvantage on the attack, treats the hit as a miss if the original roll was below a specific threshold, or reduces the damage by a substantial amount. This is the Access wall — the reason attacks fail to land. The reaction should have a clear narrative cue every time it fires: the shield comes up, the blade deflects, the stance absorbs the impact. Players must be able to see the guard answering their attacks so they can identify what is being intercepted and what would slip through.

- **A counter reaction** *(trigger: a creature deals damage to the Shield Master with a melee attack, or a creature ends its turn within the Shield Master's reach having attacked it that turn).* The Shield Master makes one immediate primary or secondary strike against that creature, or deals reflected damage equal to a portion of the damage taken. This is the Penetration Strength made mechanical — the wrong approach actively harms the attacker. The damage from this reaction can be substantial, but it must be consistent in trigger: players should know exactly what behavior provoked the counter and what behavior would not have.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A sworn intervention** *(trigger: a creature attacks the Shield Master's anchor — an ally, an object, or a defended location).* The Shield Master immediately moves up to its speed toward the attacker, ignoring opportunity attacks from the original attacker, and makes one primary strike. The Shield Master only moves to protect what it is sworn to defend. This option works best for Shield Masters with strong narrative anchors: champions of an order, sworn guardians of an item, protectors of a specific NPC.
- **A planted defiance** *(trigger: a creature attempts to move the Shield Master against its will — forced movement, a teleport effect, a grapple).* The Shield Master automatically succeeds on the effect's resistance check, or treats the forced movement as zero. The Shield Master cannot be moved. This option emphasizes positional commitment as identity — the Shield Master will not leave this spot for any reason.
- **A bulwark answer** *(trigger: an ally within 5 feet of the Shield Master is hit by an attack).* The Shield Master interposes itself, becoming the target of the attack instead and gaining the benefit of its guard reaction against it. This option emphasizes the Shield Master as protector rather than punisher — every attack against the party becomes an attack against the wall first.

> **On the counter reaction:** This is the Shield Master's most important reaction for communicating the Penetration Strength. It must fire consistently and visibly, and its damage must hurt — not catastrophically, but enough that the party feels the cost of trading blows. The narration should make the cause-and-effect immediate: "you swing, and the counter is already moving before your strike completes." Players who see this clearly will look for ways to attack that do not provoke it.

---

### 4. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Shield Master's CR calibration follows the standard process with two Shield Master-specific adjustments: **the Shield Master's effective HP is higher than its stat block suggests, and a portion of the party's expected DPR is converted into self-damage through the counter reaction.**

Both effects compound during the puzzle phase. The party deals less damage than expected to the Shield Master and simultaneously takes more damage than expected from their own attacks. When calibrating, account for both halves of this exchange.

**Steps:**


1. **Set AC high and HP at or slightly above baseline.** The Shield Master's durability comes from its guard reaction and its positional control, not exclusively from HP. AC should be at the upper end of its CR range — the Shield Master is hard to hit even before the guard reaction fires. HP should be at baseline or slightly above; the Weakness (Exposure) means the party will deal full damage to it once flanking is achieved, and overlong endgame phases feel anticlimactic for the archetype.

2. **Define the guard's magnitude deliberately.** The guard reaction must negate enough damage to feel meaningful but not so much that the party feels nothing they do matters. A useful baseline: the guard reaction should reduce the party's effective damage output by roughly 30–40% during the puzzle phase. This typically means it fires once per round on the highest-damage attack made against the Shield Master. Higher than that and the encounter feels uninteractive; lower than that and the guard fails to communicate that it exists.

3. **Define the counter's magnitude carefully.** The counter reaction's damage should be substantial enough to feel like real consequence, but not so high that single attacks risk dropping party members. A useful baseline: the counter should deal damage roughly equivalent to half the Shield Master's primary strike. The counter is the Strength, not a kill move — its purpose is to make the wrong approach uneconomical, not to threaten character death.

4. **Set attack stats below curve in frequency, at curve in potency.** The Shield Master attacks less often than its CR baseline would suggest, but each attack hits at-curve when it lands. The total expected DPR from actions alone will look low. The counter reaction adds substantially to the effective DPR — calculate the counter's expected damage per round based on how often the party is likely to attack the Shield Master, and add it to the action DPR for the true total.

5. **Assign ability scores from the primary strike's to-hit and the guard's Save DC (if applicable).** Strength should be the primary ability score — the Shield Master is grounded, powerful, and stationary. Constitution should be high — the Shield Master is built to absorb punishment when the guard fails. Dexterity should be low or average — the Shield Master is not fast, and this is part of the Weakness expression.

6. **Distribute damage across categories with Shield Master priorities:**
   - **Actions:** Below-baseline overall DPR, weighted by the Shield Master's reduced fire rate. At-curve per-hit damage. Spread attacks across recent attackers rather than focusing one target.
   - **Bonus Actions:** The stance bonus action deals no damage. The anchor expression bonus action may deal damage (in opportunity-attack style) or may not. The Shield Master's Bonus Action DPR is low — most of its bonus action budget is positional and communicative.
   - **Reactions:** The guard reaction deals no damage but contributes substantially to effective HP. The counter reaction deals significant damage, fires once per round on average, and constitutes a substantial portion of the Shield Master's effective DPR. The third reaction's damage varies by selection — calculate based on which option is chosen.

7. **Assign attack damage with the Exposure Weakness in mind.** Once the guard falls — through flanking, forced movement breaking the stance, or a multi-attacker overwhelm — the Shield Master's damage output should not collapse, but its survivability should drop sharply. The Shield Master's offensive presence remains until the end; what falls away is its untouchability. Do not under-tune the primary strike. The Shield Master should still be dangerous in its final phase, even as the Weakness reveals the path to closing the encounter.

---

### The Shield Master's Tell

Every Shield Master stat block must have one. Write it in plain language before finalizing the stat block, then make sure it is visible in play within two rounds:

> *"This Shield Master's guard fails when [specific observable angle or condition], and its counter triggers when [specific observable behavior]. Players who [position around the blind spot and avoid the counter trigger] will find their damage landing — and the Shield Master pinned in place, unable to follow."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the Access wall has no readable bypass and the Penetration Strength is just damage punishment — the Shield Master is just a high-AC boss that hurts you back. Go back to the guard tools and define the specific angle, damage type, or behavioral condition that the guard cannot intercept. The wall must have a flank. Players need to be able to find it.

## The Trickster

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the party cannot trust what they are seeing, and every attack they commit to risks being spent on something that was never really there. The puzzle is reading the deceptions for what they are — and the deceptions are finite.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A Trickster's high concept must communicate active manipulation of the combat picture — not stealth, not evasion, but the deliberate construction of a false situation that the party must learn to see through. The Trickster is not hidden. It is *misrepresenting*. The party can see something, point at it, attack it — and still be acting on a picture the Trickster authored.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what is the lie the Trickster is selling, and through what craft?* The lie should be specific and characterful, and the craft should be legible. Tricksters can express their archetype through any combination of three deception modes:

- **Misidentification** — the party cannot tell who or what the real threat is. Body doubles, illusory copies, magical disguises that swap the Trickster's appearance with an ally or environmental feature, possessed bystanders, swapped masks.
- **Obscurement** — the party cannot see the real threat at all. Smoke bombs, flash powder, fog clouds, sudden darkness, blinding flares, dust kicked up by movement, ink dispersed in water, conjured mist.
- **Action misdirection** — the party cannot trust what the real threat is *doing*. Feints that draw attacks to nothing, false tells that bait responses, manipulated targeting that turns the party's actions against them, the appearance of preparing one attack while executing another.

Most Tricksters lean on one mode as their signature and incorporate the others as supporting tools. A Trickster without a coherent craft is just a stat block with concealment. The craft is what makes the encounter feel like a confrontation with a specific kind of opponent.

**Strong examples:** "The Mirror-Maker of the Veiled Court" (misidentification), "The Smoke Walker of the Charred Quarter" (obscurement), "The Hundred-Faced Liar" (action misdirection), "She Who Throws Dust in the Sun's Eye" (mixed).

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Illusionist Boss," "Hard-to-Find Enemy," "Decoy Generator." These describe statistics, not identity. The high concept should communicate *style* — the manner of deception is what defines the encounter, because the deception is the encounter.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Trickster template asks a specific question before anything else: **what is the deception, how does it consume resources, and what is the tell that reveals the real target?**

Organize your tools into three categories before writing a single stat block entry:

**Deception Tools** — what creates the false combat picture. Pick from any of the three modes, or mix:
- *Misidentification examples:* a number of decoys or doubles that share the Trickster's space; an illusion that disguises the Trickster as one of the party members or an environmental feature; a glamour that swaps the Trickster's apparent position with one of its decoys when targeted; possessed bystanders the Trickster speaks through.
- *Obscurement examples:* smoke bombs thrown to create heavily obscured zones; flash powder that blinds anyone looking at the Trickster; magical fog that fills a 20-foot radius; conjured darkness centered on the Trickster's position; flares that impose disadvantage on attacks targeting anything within them.
- *Action misdirection examples:* a feint that intercepts the first attack of each round and reveals it as having struck nothing; a false tell that bait the party into reacting to an attack the Trickster never intended to make; a sleight that lets the Trickster perform two apparent actions in the moment its turn resolves, only one of which is real.

Critically: each deception must be a resource — destructible, expirable, single-use, or limited in charges. Smoke clouds dissipate over rounds. Flash powder has a limited number of uses. Decoys can be destroyed. Feints can be read once the pattern is established. The bag of tricks is finite, and the Trickster cannot replenish it freely.

**Tell Tools** — what allows attentive players to distinguish real from false, or to act despite the obscurement. Tells should match the deception mode:
- *For misidentification:* only the real Trickster casts a shadow; only the real Trickster can be detected by a specific sense; decoys take a recognizable mechanical action (always attack the same target, always end their turn at the same distance) while the real Trickster acts differently.
- *For obscurement:* the Trickster always moves in a specific direction within its smoke (toward the nearest party member, toward the exit, etc.); a specific sound or environmental cue accompanies the Trickster's position; the obscurement has a directional weakness (wind clears it from one side, a specific damage type dissipates it, etc.).
- *For action misdirection:* the Trickster's real attacks have a specific tell distinct from its feints (a half-second pause, a specific footing, an audible cue); the Trickster's misdirections target a different creature than its real attacks would; the false tell is always exaggerated while the real one is subtle.

The tells must be observable from the first round and consistent throughout the encounter. A Trickster whose tells change between rounds is not a puzzle, it is a moving target.

**Offense Tools** — what the Trickster actually does to the party when not maintaining its deceptions.
*(Examples: a single accurate attack used from concealment; a debuff that imposes disadvantage on attempts to detect or target the Trickster; a positional ability that lets the Trickster reposition immediately after striking, often into a new deception. The Trickster's offense is modest by archetype convention — its real threat is the misdirection, not the damage. The offense exists to make ignoring the Trickster impossible, not to define the encounter's danger.)*

The tell that players must discover is the central design challenge of the archetype. It must be present from the first round, observable through play, and consistent across phases — but it must also be subtle enough that players have to *notice* it rather than be handed it. A Trickster whose tell is obvious is not a puzzle; a Trickster whose tell is undiscoverable is not fair. The tell should reward attention without requiring specific ability access — no Trickster should be a gear check that only a party with True Seeing can solve.

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Trickster's action economy is built around **deception maintenance and misdirection rather than offensive output**. The Trickster's mechanical threat is what the party cannot do, not what the Trickster does.

#### Actions

**The Trickster should have:**

- A **primary strike** that is accurate but deals below-curve damage, used from concealment or from a position the party has not yet identified as the real Trickster's location. This attack should impose a condition that reinforces the deception — Frightened, a brief Charm, a sense-impairing effect, or for an obscurement-style Trickster, a condition that extends or deepens the obscurement on the target. The strike says: *you do not know where I am, and now you can see less.*

- A **secondary strike** that is short-range, lower-damage, and tied to the Trickster's chosen deception mode. For a misidentification Trickster, this attack might be made by one of the decoys (dealing reduced damage but maintaining the illusion of multiple threats). For an obscurement Trickster, it might be a quick strike from within smoke that adds another obscured square to the field. For an action-misdirection Trickster, it might be the "feint that lands" — the attack the party did not expect because they were watching for a different one. This attack reinforces the bag-of-tricks identity — the Trickster's offensive turns are also moments of deception expansion.

- A **Multiattack** that lets the Trickster use either strike twice, or use the primary and produce a deception with the secondary's action budget. The Trickster should rarely use Multiattack to deal pure damage — its action economy is divided between attacking and maintaining the deception, and Multiattack is where that division is most legible.

> **Trickster-specific note:** Some Tricksters can perform "deception actions" on their turn — abilities that extend the false picture rather than dealing damage. A misidentification Trickster might have its decoys take actions that mimic combat. An obscurement Trickster might throw a smoke bomb or detonate a flare as part of its action. An action-misdirection Trickster might perform a feint that requires the party to respond. These actions express the Trickster's craft on its own initiative; use them sparingly to preserve the sense of the deception's expense — every deception action should feel like a resource expenditure, not a free bonus attack.

#### Bonus Actions

The Trickster's Bonus Actions are its **deception maintenance and misdirection deployment tools** — what creates new deceptions, refreshes existing ones, or shifts the combat picture in the Trickster's favor.

- A **deception deployment bonus action** that produces a new deception of the Trickster's chosen mode: creating a decoy, throwing a smoke bomb that establishes a heavily obscured zone, setting up a feint that intercepts the next attack against the Trickster, or refreshing a deception that has been spent. This ability should have a clear resource cost — a limited number of uses per encounter, a cooldown, or a triggering condition that the party can deny. The cost is what makes the Weakness (Attrition) real: every deception deployed is one fewer the Trickster can deploy later.

- A **picture-control bonus action** that shifts the combat picture once deception is established. For a misidentification Trickster, this is a position swap with one of its decoys. For an obscurement Trickster, this is movement that keeps the Trickster within its obscured zone or extends the zone's coverage. For an action-misdirection Trickster, this is a fresh feint that resets the party's reads. This ability is the rhythm of the encounter — it fires reliably most rounds and is what forces the party to re-evaluate their assumptions after every action they take. Without it, the party would solve the deception once and be done; with it, the puzzle resets at the start of each round.

> **On the deception deployment bonus action:** This is the central resource gate of the archetype. The number of deceptions the Trickster can produce, and the cost at which it produces them, determines how long the puzzle phase lasts. A Trickster with infinite deceptions becomes a frustrating wall; a Trickster with too few feels like a one-trick boss the party solves immediately. A useful baseline: the Trickster should be able to maintain 2–4 active deceptions at any given time (decoys present, smoke clouds active, feints prepared), and deploying a new one should cost a bonus action and a limited resource (3–5 uses per encounter, or a specific triggering condition).

#### Reactions

The Trickster has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the Access wall lives most directly — the moments where an attack should have landed but is revealed to have struck nothing, or where the party's targeting is undone.

**Required:**

- **A misread reaction** *(trigger: a creature hits the Trickster — or what the creature believes to be the Trickster — with an attack).* The attack is revealed to have struck a deception rather than the real target. For misidentification Tricksters, the target dissolves as a decoy and the Trickster may swap places with another decoy in line of sight. For obscurement Tricksters, the obscurement is revealed to have hidden the Trickster's true position — it was never where the attack landed, and the Trickster's actual location is somewhere within the obscured zone. For action-misdirection Tricksters, the attack is revealed to have been baited by a feint that drew it away from the real Trickster. This is the Access wall — the reason hits fail to connect. The reaction should fire visibly with cues appropriate to the mode: the decoy dissolves, the smoke billows where no body falls, the Trickster's laughter comes from the opposite direction. Players must see the reaction firing so they can track what the deception was.

- **A picture-reset reaction** *(trigger: a creature successfully identifies the Trickster's real location, or removes a deception the Trickster has placed — through a specific ability, a successful skill check, or the destruction of a deception element).* The Trickster immediately deploys a fresh deception of its chosen mode: a new decoy, a new smoke cloud, a new feint. The reaction reinforces the recursive quality of the puzzle — solving one deception does not solve the encounter, only the next deception, and the party must apply what they have learned to a fresh instance of the same problem.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A vanishing trick** *(trigger: the Trickster is reduced to half HP for the first time).* All current deceptions dissipate. The Trickster gains the Invisible condition (or, for an obscurement Trickster, an equivalent obscured effect) until the end of its next turn, and produces two new deceptions in random unoccupied spaces within 60 feet at the start of its next turn. This option emphasizes the Trickster as escape artist — the moment of greatest danger is also the moment of greatest deception, and the puzzle resets to its hardest state.
- **A puppet pull** *(trigger: a creature within 30 feet of the Trickster takes the Attack action and misses).* The Trickster makes that creature reroll the attack against an ally instead, treating the original roll as if it had been made against the new target. This option emphasizes the Trickster as manipulator — the party's own attacks become weapons against them, and the deception extends beyond identification into action itself. Use sparingly; this can feel unfair if overused.
- **A laugh from elsewhere** *(trigger: a creature successfully resolves a deception — destroying a decoy, dispersing a smoke cloud, reading a feint).* The resolved deception's location becomes a temporary cue that imposes disadvantage on the attacker's next attack against the Trickster, and the Trickster gains advantage on its next attack against that creature. This option emphasizes the Trickster's contempt — every deception the party sees through is itself a setup for the next one.

> **On the misread reaction:** This is the Trickster's most important reaction for communicating the puzzle. It must fire with a clear and consistent narrative cue specific to the deception mode — the decoy dissolves into mist, the smoke billows around emptiness, the Trickster's voice mocks from the wrong direction. The cue should be specific and characterful, because that cue is the moment players learn what to look for. Players who see the same dissolution effect repeatedly will eventually notice what was different about the moment the real Trickster was hit.

---

### 4. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Trickster's CR calibration follows the standard process with two Trickster-specific adjustments: **the Trickster's effective HP is inflated significantly during the deception phase, and its effective DPR is below baseline throughout.**

The deception inflates effective HP because a meaningful portion of the party's damage is being absorbed by deceptions rather than the real target — whether that means decoys taking hits, attacks being made into smoke at empty squares, or feints baiting attacks away from the Trickster entirely. The Trickster's actual offensive output is modest by design — its threat is the misdirection, not the damage. Once the bag of tricks is exhausted, both adjustments resolve simultaneously: the effective HP collapses to the written value, and the DPR was always what the stat block said it was.

**Steps:**


1. **Set AC at baseline and HP below baseline.** The Trickster's durability comes entirely from its deceptions — the real Trickster, once located, is fragile by design. Setting AC at the lower end of its CR range and HP noticeably below baseline ensures the Weakness (Attrition) lands cleanly: when the deceptions run out, the encounter ends quickly. The Trickster is a Loki, not a god.

2. **Define the deception budget deliberately.** The number of deceptions available, the rate of replenishment, and the cost of deployment together determine the encounter's pacing. A useful baseline: the Trickster begins with 2–3 active deceptions and can deploy a new one each round at the cost of a limited resource (3–5 uses per encounter). This typically means the deception phase lasts 3–5 rounds, after which the Trickster is fighting with its actual stat block exposed. Adjust based on the party's expected damage output and the Trickster's chosen mode: a misidentification Trickster facing a high-damage party needs a larger reserve of decoys; an obscurement Trickster facing a party with strong area-denial spells needs more smoke charges to outlast them.

3. **Set attack stats at curve in accuracy, below curve in damage.** The Trickster hits reliably when it strikes, but its strikes are not what the encounter is about. The primary strike's attack bonus should be at curve; the damage should be modest. The total expected DPR will look low for the CR — this is correct. The Trickster's threat is what the party cannot do, not what it can.

4. **Assign ability scores from the primary strike's to-hit.** Dexterity should be the primary ability score — Tricksters of every mode rely on quick, precise movement. Charisma is a frequent secondary, especially for magical Tricksters whose deceptions involve illusions, charms, or false impressions. Intelligence may be the secondary instead for tactically-minded Tricksters whose tricks are physical (smoke bombs, prepared feints, mechanical decoys). Constitution should be low — the Trickster is not built to take punishment, and this is part of the Weakness expression.

5. **Distribute damage across categories with Trickster priorities:**
   - **Actions:** Below-curve overall DPR, weighted by the Trickster's selective fire rate. The primary strike's per-hit damage is below curve; the secondary strike (or deception actions) deals minimal damage. The Trickster's action DPR is the lowest of any defensive archetype.
   - **Bonus Actions:** Deception deployment and picture-control bonus actions deal no damage. The Trickster's Bonus Action DPR is zero — all of its bonus action budget is deception maintenance.
   - **Reactions:** The misread reaction deals no damage. The picture-reset reaction deals no damage. The Trickster's reaction DPR is zero. Like the Fortress, the Trickster's reactions are entirely informational and defensive.

6. **Assign attack damage with the Attrition Weakness in mind.** When the bag of tricks is exhausted, the Trickster's effective DPR does not change — but its effective HP collapses to its written value, and the party can finally apply that DPR against a real target. The Weakness lands not because the Trickster becomes weaker but because the party can finally see what they are fighting. The damage budget must be modest enough that the Trickster's exposed phase is short — a Trickster that hits like a Juggernaut after its tricks run out feels like two encounters bolted together, not one cohesive fight.

---

### The Trickster's Tell

Every Trickster stat block must have one. Write it in plain language before finalizing the stat block, then make sure it is visible in play within two rounds:

> *"This Trickster's deceptions can be distinguished from the real thing by [specific observable difference appropriate to the deception mode]. Players who [identify and track this tell] will find their attacks landing on the real target — and the Trickster's bag of tricks emptying faster than it can refill."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the deception has no readable solution and the puzzle is unfair — the Trickster is just a high-evasion boss with a frustrating gimmick. Go back to the tell tools and define the specific, consistent, observable difference between the real and the deception, in terms appropriate to whatever mode the Trickster is using. The tell must be present from round one. Players need to be able to see it, even if they do not immediately understand what they are seeing.

## The Warlock

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the party is being ground down by ranged pressure they cannot match, and they cannot stop the pressure without crossing a hostile space the boss controls. The puzzle is closing the distance — and the closing is what the boss has prepared the entire battlefield against.**

> *A note on the name:* "Warlock" describes the boss's combat identity, not its class, species, or how it deals damage. A Warlock may be a dragon breathing from a cliff, a sniper on a tower, a siege engine, a cursed shrine pumping out emanations, a hovering construct, a hag in a swamp with a longbow, or an artillery-class undead. The ranged attrition vector can be magical, chemical, mechanical, environmental, biological, or psychological. What matters is that the boss punishes distance, defends distance, and falls apart when the distance closes.

---

### 1. High Concept

A Warlock's high concept must communicate dominion over a contested space — the boss does not simply attack from far away, it has *claimed* the range between it and the party and made that range a hazard. The Warlock is not running away. It is *holding court*, and the journey to it is the encounter.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what does the space between the party and this boss look like?* The space should be characterful and specific — a killing field of crossfire, a curse zone radiating outward, a hail of arrows, a flooded courtyard, a sniper's gallery. The Warlock's threat is inseparable from the geometry of the encounter. A Warlock fought in a featureless room is a Warlock that has lost its identity before the first die is rolled.

**Strong examples:** "The Tower-Throned Archer of the Black Pass," "The Hateful Wyrm of the Bone Cairn," "She Who Holds the Killing Field," "The Hidden Engine of the Drowned Garrison."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Ranged Spellcaster Boss," "Sniper Enemy," "Long-Range Damage Dealer." These describe role, not identity. The high concept should communicate *the space the boss owns* — the dominion is what makes the encounter feel like a journey toward a confrontation rather than a damage exchange at extreme range.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Warlock template asks a specific question before anything else: **what is the attrition vector, what defends the space, and what is the path the party must take to close the distance?**

Organize your tools into three categories before writing a single stat block entry:

**Attrition Tools** — what punishes the party for being in the contested space.
*(Examples: a high-damage ranged attack that lands every round on a chosen target; an emanation that deals damage to every creature within a large radius of the Warlock; a curse or condition that ticks upward each round a creature has not reached the Warlock; a hail of secondary projectiles that fills the field with low-damage hazards; an environmental effect the Warlock maintains — fire, cold, suffocation, magical decay — that taxes the party for occupying the space. The attrition vector should be the encounter's defining mechanical pressure. Without it, the Warlock is just a ranged damage dealer with an evasion problem.)*

**Distance Tools** — what keeps the Warlock at range when the party tries to close.
*(Examples: a position that requires climbing, swimming, or crossing a hazard to reach; forced-movement effects that push or pull approaching creatures back; teleportation or repositioning abilities that reset the Warlock's distance from the party; obstacles or summoned barriers that block direct approach; allies or constructs that the Warlock can hide behind. Critically: the distance tools must have a discoverable path. The Warlock is not unreachable — it is reachable through a specific sequence the party must figure out under pressure.)*

**Threshold Tools** — what makes the final approach the most dangerous part of the encounter.
*(Examples: a desperate ability the Warlock activates when a creature reaches a specific proximity; an emergency repositioning that triggers once per encounter; a last-line hazard the Warlock has prepared as a final barrier. The threshold tools exist to make the moment of arrival feel earned — the party should feel the Warlock's panic when they finally close in, and that panic should manifest as one last hard obstacle before the Weakness opens.)*

The tell that players must discover is embedded in the distance tools: the path to the Warlock is observable, even if it is not immediately obvious. Cover the party can use. A direction the attrition vector does not reach. A specific approach angle that the displacement effects do not cover. An ally that, if defeated, lets the party reach the Warlock directly. Players who study the field for two rounds should see what the path is. Players who charge blindly should find their resources depleting faster than their progress.

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Warlock's action economy is built around **maintaining the attrition vector and reshaping the contested space**, not delivering knockout damage. The Warlock's threat is the sum of pressure over time, not any individual hit.

#### Actions

**The Warlock should have:**

- A **primary ranged attack** that is the encounter's defining attrition source — accurate, at or above curve in damage, used every round against the party's most exposed target. This attack should impose a condition that compounds the attrition: ongoing damage, a stacking debuff, forced movement that pushes the target back into more hazard, or a curse that worsens if the target does not reach the Warlock within a set number of rounds. The strike says: *standing still here costs you something you cannot afford.*

- A **secondary ranged attack** that is lower-damage but covers a different threat profile — area-affecting where the primary was single-target, or single-target where the primary was area-affecting. The secondary's purpose is to ensure the party cannot solve the primary's pressure with simple positioning. If clustering avoids the primary, the secondary punishes clustering. If spreading out avoids the primary, the secondary punishes spreading.

- A **Multiattack** that lets the Warlock use the primary twice, or the primary and secondary once each. The Warlock should spread its attacks across the field — its threat is the entire party feeling pressured, not any single target dying. A Warlock that focuses all its attacks on one creature has narrowed the encounter from "a journey across a hostile space" to "the cleric heals the fighter every round," and the archetype's identity is lost.

> **Warlock-specific note:** The Warlock should never have a meaningful melee attack. If the party closes the distance and the Warlock can simply turn and fight them up close, the Weakness (Access) fails to land. Give the Warlock a minor melee option for narrative completeness — a desperate strike with a knife, a panicked unarmed swing, a final defensive spell — but it should be noticeably weaker than its ranged output, and the difference should be felt the moment contact is made.

#### Bonus Actions

The Warlock's Bonus Actions are its **space-shaping tools** — what reinforces the dominion over the contested area and reshapes the field when the party makes progress.

- A **field maintenance bonus action** that sustains, extends, or refreshes the attrition vector. If the attrition is an emanation, this might expand its radius or intensify its damage. If it is a curse, this might apply it to a new target. If it is a projectile barrage, this might add another threat zone to the battlefield. The field maintenance bonus action fires reliably each round and is what ensures the attrition does not slacken. Without it, the party could simply tank the first round's damage and reach the Warlock unscathed; with it, every round of approach is a round of fresh pressure.

- A **distance maintenance bonus action** that reinforces the Warlock's position when the party closes in. This might be a short teleport, a forced-movement effect that pushes approaching creatures back, a wall or hazard summoned between the Warlock and the closest creature, or a positional ability that puts the Warlock back behind cover. This ability should have a clear cost — limited uses per encounter, a cooldown, or a triggering condition the party can deny — because it is what makes the Exposure Strength real. If the Warlock can keep distance forever, the encounter is unwinnable; if it can keep distance for three or four critical rounds, the encounter feels like a real approach.

> **On the distance maintenance bonus action:** This is the central pacing dial of the archetype. A Warlock with too many uses of distance maintenance becomes a frustrating wall — the party never quite closes. A Warlock with too few falls in one round of focused movement. A useful baseline: the Warlock can use this ability 3–5 times per encounter, with each use representing a meaningful obstacle the party must overcome before progressing further. Pair the cost with a triggering condition — used only when a creature ends its turn within 30 feet, used only once per round, used only if the Warlock has line of sight to the destination — to keep the use deliberate rather than reactive panic-spam.

#### Reactions

The Warlock has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the Warlock's positional control lives most actively — the moments where the party's approach is interrupted, reversed, or punished.

**Required:**

- **A reposition reaction** *(trigger: a creature ends its turn within a specific proximity to the Warlock — typically 30 feet — or a creature makes a successful attack against the Warlock from beyond melee range).* The Warlock immediately moves up to its speed, teleports a fixed distance, or activates an effect that places it at a new defended position. This reaction is the encounter's distance-reset mechanism — every time the party feels they have made progress, the Warlock can respond by extending the gap again. The reaction's range should be limited to maintain solvability: it does not reset the encounter to round one, it sets the party back one round of approach.

- **A field response reaction** *(trigger: a creature crosses a threshold in the contested space — entering a specific zone, breaking through a barrier, reaching half the distance to the Warlock).* The Warlock immediately reshapes the field in response: applying a new hazard, accelerating the attrition vector, or summoning an obstacle in the path of approach. This reaction punishes progress without making progress impossible. The party advances, the field worsens, the party advances again, the field worsens more — until the Warlock runs out of field to reshape.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A last-line panic** *(trigger: a creature ends its turn within melee reach of the Warlock for the first time).* The Warlock activates a single-use desperate ability: a burst of damage centered on itself, a massive teleport to a previously defended position, a final emanation that fills the area. This reaction fires exactly once. After it resolves, the Warlock is exposed and the Weakness lands. This option is best when the Warlock has a strong "siege ending" beat in its narrative — the moment the walls come down.
- **A focused strike** *(trigger: a creature reduces the Warlock below half HP for the first time from beyond a specific range).* The Warlock makes one immediate primary ranged attack against that creature with advantage. The message is clear: *the longer you stay at range, the more I attend to you specifically.* This option pushes the party harder toward closing the distance rather than trying to out-range the boss.
- **A curse of pursuit** *(trigger: a creature successfully crosses one of the Warlock's distance-maintenance effects — surviving a forced-movement effect, breaking through a barrier, completing the climb).* The Warlock places a stacking debuff on that creature, increasing the damage they take from the attrition vector for the rest of the encounter. The party can close the distance, but the closer it gets, the more it bleeds. This option works best for Warlocks whose attrition is curse-flavored — every approach costs more than the last.

> **On the reposition reaction:** This is the Warlock's most important reaction for sustaining the encounter's identity. It must fire reliably and visibly — the party should see the Warlock moving away, climbing higher, teleporting back to its perch. If the reposition is invisible or feels arbitrary, the encounter becomes a game of catch-the-rabbit. The reaction should always end with the Warlock in a position the party can still see and target, just from a worse angle. Distance is the puzzle, not concealment.

> **On deliberate cycling:** A party member with high movement or a mounted character can deliberately trigger the reposition reaction by ending their turn within the threshold, closing again next turn, and forcing the reaction a second time. This is intentional — the party is burning the Warlock's distance maintenance budget, which is exactly the correct approach. A DM who sees this happening should not treat it as exploitation; they should narrate the Warlock visibly straining to maintain its range and communicate that the repositioning uses are running low. The party has found the right lever. Let them pull it.

---

### 4. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Warlock's CR calibration follows the standard process with one Warlock-specific adjustment: **the Warlock's effective HP is significantly higher than its stat block suggests during the Exposure phase, but collapses to baseline once the party closes the distance.**

The HP inflation comes from the action-economy distortion of the attrition vector. During the approach, the party spends actions on movement, healing, defensive abilities, and crossing hazards rather than attacking the Warlock. Every action spent surviving the attrition is an action not spent reducing the Warlock's HP. The effective HP inflation continues until the party reaches the Warlock; at that point, the inflation collapses, and the Warlock's actual HP value is exposed.

**Steps:**


1. **Set AC at baseline and HP below baseline.** The Warlock's durability comes from inaccessibility, not from raw stats. AC should be at the lower end of its CR range — the Warlock is hard to hit because it is hard to reach, not because it is armored. HP should be noticeably below baseline; once the party closes the distance, the encounter should end quickly. A Warlock with high HP after the approach is solved feels like a punishment for the party that just spent four rounds crossing hazards to reach it.

2. **Define the attrition vector's magnitude deliberately.** The vector must deal enough damage to feel like a real ticking clock but not so much that the party cannot survive the approach. A useful baseline: the attrition vector should deal damage equivalent to one moderate hit per party member per round during the approach phase, distributed across the field. This typically means a single ranged attack against one target plus a smaller area effect or per-round damage tick across the party. The total should be sustainable for 3–5 rounds with a healer present, and unsustainable indefinitely.

3. **Set attack stats at or slightly above curve in accuracy, at curve in damage.** The Warlock hits reliably from range — its threat depends on the attrition landing every round. The primary ranged attack's attack bonus should be at the upper end of its CR baseline. Damage should be at curve; the attrition vector handles the cumulative pressure, so the primary does not need to over-deliver on individual hits.

4. **Assign ability scores from the primary ranged attack's to-hit and the attrition's Save DC (if applicable).** The relevant ability score depends entirely on the Warlock's nature — Dexterity for an archer or sniper, Intelligence or Charisma for a magical caster, Constitution for a creature whose attrition is its own emanation, Wisdom for a divine or natural source. Constitution should usually be low or moderate — the Warlock is not built to absorb punishment, and this is part of the Weakness expression.

5. **Distribute damage across categories with Warlock priorities:**
   - **Actions:** The primary ranged attack is at-curve. The secondary covers the other threat profile. Action DPR is approximately baseline for the CR — the Warlock is a damage dealer in this category, even if its other categories are more positional.
   - **Bonus Actions:** The field maintenance bonus action may deal damage (if it expands or intensifies the attrition vector) or may not (if it is purely positional). The distance maintenance bonus action deals no damage. The Warlock's Bonus Action DPR varies by build — calculate it based on whether the attrition vector is renewed or expanded by the field maintenance.
   - **Reactions:** The reposition reaction deals no damage. The field response reaction may deal damage (if it creates a new hazard) or may not (if it is purely repositional). The optional third reaction varies — focused strike adds significant damage when it fires, last-line panic adds a burst once, curse of pursuit adds compounding pressure over time.

6. **Assign attack damage with the Access Weakness in mind.** When the party reaches the Warlock, the attrition vector should become irrelevant or significantly degraded — a sniper cannot shoot point-blank without disadvantage, an emanation cannot push someone already inside it, a curse cannot tick on a creature that has already arrived. The Warlock's effective DPR should drop sharply when the party closes the distance. This is the Weakness made mechanical: the Warlock is not weaker, it is *neutralized*. Combined with the below-baseline HP, this means the endgame phase resolves in 1–2 rounds, which is correct. The journey was the encounter.

---

### The Warlock's Tell

Every Warlock stat block must have one. Write it in plain language before finalizing the stat block, then make sure it is visible in play within two rounds:

> *"This Warlock's dominion can be closed by [specific observable path or approach], and its attrition vector fails when [specific observable condition — typically melee proximity, but may be a damage type, a positional state, or the destruction of a specific element]. Players who [identify the path and commit to closing the distance] will find the Warlock surprisingly fragile once they arrive."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the Exposure Strength has no readable solution and the Attrition Defining Trait is just a damage tax — the Warlock is just a ranged boss that cannot be reached. Go back to the distance tools and define a path the party can find through observation. The dominion must have a route through. Players need to be able to see it, even if they have to fight through pressure to take it.

## The Duelist

Every structural choice in this template exists to express one thing: **the party's attacks are being read and answered before they finish landing, and the answers are sharper than the questions. The puzzle is finding the input the Duelist cannot process — the angle outside its model, the technique outside its experience, the simultaneous attack it cannot fully counter.**

---

### 1. High Concept

A Duelist's high concept must communicate reactive mastery — the sense of a fighter who has fought every kind of opponent and won, whose advantage is not strength but recognition. The Duelist is not faster than the party. It is not stronger. It is *more practiced at fighting people like them*, and that practice has compressed into reflex.

When writing your high concept, ask: *what has this fighter trained against?* The answer should feel specific and earned — decades of duels, a thousand executions, a lineage of masters, a curse that grants memory of every fight that ever ended in a sword. The Duelist's identity is the weight of that history made physical. A Duelist that has not earned its precision feels like a stat block with a high reaction count; a Duelist whose precision is the inheritance of a specific tradition or experience feels like a confrontation with a master.

**Strong examples:** "The Old Blade of the Hundred Duels," "The Sword Saint of the Wandering Pass," "She Who Reads the Cut Before the Cut," "The Reaper Who Has Already Seen You Die."

**Weak examples** (fix these): "Counter-Attacking Boss," "Reactive Fighter," "Punishes Mistakes." These describe mechanics, not identity. The high concept should communicate *the source of the read* — why this fighter knows what you are about to do. The encounter feels different when the Duelist's precision is rooted in something specific, even if the players never learn what it is.


---

### 2. Tools & Features

The Duelist template asks a specific question before anything else: **what does this fighter read, what does it counter, and what is the kind of input its model cannot process?**

Organize your tools into three categories before writing a single stat block entry:

**Read Tools** — what allows the Duelist to anticipate and answer attacks.
*(Examples: a passive trait that grants the Duelist advantage on saves against attacks made by creatures who have already attacked it this round; a reaction that activates when a specific kind of attack is declared; a heightened sense — sight, sound, intuition — that the party can attempt to deny; a trait that converts the first attack of each round against the Duelist into a Save throw instead of an attack roll. The read is what makes the Duelist a Duelist. It should be a visible, named feature — players need to know they are being read, not just hit.)*

**Counter Tools** — what the Duelist does when its read connects.
*(Examples: a counterstrike that deals more damage than the original attack would have dealt; a riposte that imposes a debilitating condition on the attacker; a reaction that turns the original attack against an ally; a lifesteal effect on every successful counter. The counters should hurt — not catastrophically, but enough that the party feels the cost of attacking predictably. The counters are the Endurance pressure made mechanical: every wrong input feeds the Duelist.)*

**Model Tools** — what defines the scope of the read, and what lies outside it.
*(Examples: the Duelist reads attacks made within its sight, but not from creatures it cannot see; it reads martial techniques but not magical ones, or vice versa; it reads attacks made one at a time but not simultaneous strikes; it reads attacks by a single attacker that has already engaged it, but not the first attack from a new combatant; it reads conventional weapons but not improvised ones. The model must have a definable scope — the things it covers, and the things it does not. Players who identify the scope find the Weakness.)*

The tell that players must discover is embedded in the model tools: the Duelist's read has a definable scope, and that scope has an exterior. The exterior is the Weakness. The model must be observable through play — the party watches what the Duelist counters and what it does not, and from that pattern infers the limit. A Duelist whose model is undefined is not a puzzle, it is a counter machine. The scope must be specific, consistent, and discoverable through attention.

---

### 3. Generate Attacks & Responses

The Duelist's action economy is built around **moderate offensive output amplified by overwhelming reactive precision**. The Duelist does not need to attack often or hard on its own initiative — the party's attacks against it are themselves the source of most of its damage.

#### Actions

**The Duelist should have:**

- A **primary strike** that is accurate, deals at-curve damage, and is used on the Duelist's own turn as a measured offensive — not a flurry, not a power attack, but a single deliberate strike against whichever creature has most recently presented an opening. This attack should impose a condition that constrains the target's next action: disadvantage on attack rolls against the Duelist, a brief Slowed effect, or a positional debuff that pushes the target into an angle the Duelist's model covers. The strike says: *I have decided how you are going to attack me next, and I have already prepared.*

- A **secondary strike** that is lower-damage and used as a fallback when the primary's preferred target is unreachable, or when the Duelist needs to disengage from one combatant to set up a read on another. This attack should be efficient rather than impressive — a fast jab, a quick cut, a measured kick. It exists to maintain the Duelist's presence without committing it to a position.

- A **Multiattack** that lets the Duelist use either strike twice, or both strikes once. The Duelist should spread its attacks across attackers rather than focusing one target — its identity is about answering the entire party, not destroying any single member. A Duelist that focuses its actions on one creature is functionally a Hunter; what makes it a Duelist is that every party member is being read simultaneously.

> **Duelist-specific note:** The Duelist's action damage should be noticeably below its CR baseline. The reactions carry the majority of the encounter's threat. A Duelist that hits hard on its own turn *and* counters hard on the party's turns has too much offensive presence — the encounter becomes a damage race with no time to think about the read. Trim the action damage. Let the reactions speak for the archetype.

#### Bonus Actions

The Duelist's Bonus Actions are its **read maintenance and positioning tools** — what keeps the model active and what lets the Duelist adjust its position to maintain optimal reading angles.

- A **stance bonus action** that signals which kind of read is currently active — a defensive stance that grants advantage on counters against melee attackers, an aggressive stance that grants advantage on counters against attackers who attempt to disengage, a measured stance that grants advantage on counters against the most recent attacker. This ability fires reliably each round and should be visible: the Duelist shifts its blade, settles its footing, adjusts its grip. The stance is information delivery. Players who watch the stance change can sometimes predict which kind of attack is about to be most strongly countered, and choose to attack outside that profile instead.

- A **read positioning bonus action** that moves the Duelist into a position from which its model covers more attackers — typically a centered position with line of sight to multiple party members, or a flanking position that puts the Duelist on the model's preferred side of an opponent. This ability is what ensures the Duelist's read remains broad as the encounter develops. Without it, the party could simply position themselves outside the read's scope and attack freely; with it, the Duelist constantly adjusts to maintain coverage.

> **On the stance bonus action:** This is the Duelist's communication channel for the puzzle. The model has a scope, and the stance signals which part of that scope is most active right now. A defensive stance tells the party that melee approaches will be most heavily countered this round; the party might respond by using ranged attacks, by attacking simultaneously, or by feinting. The stance does not have to fully telegraph the model, but it should give attentive players a working hypothesis about what to avoid.

#### Reactions

The Duelist has **3 reactions per round, only 1 per turn**. These are where the entire archetype lives mechanically. The reactions are the read made manifest — and the read is the encounter.

**Required:**

- **A counter reaction** *(trigger: a creature attacks the Duelist with an attack that falls within the model's scope — typically a melee or ranged attack from a visible attacker within a defined range, but the exact scope depends on the Duelist's model).* The Duelist makes one immediate primary strike against the attacker, or deals reflected damage equal to a portion of the attack's damage roll, or imposes a debilitating condition on the attacker until the start of the Duelist's next turn. This reaction is the Endurance Defining Trait — the reason every wrong attack hurts. The damage from this reaction can be substantial, because the Duelist's action damage is below baseline to compensate. The reaction must have a clear narrative cue: the Duelist's blade is moving before the attack lands, the riposte arrives before the strike is finished. Players must see the read happening so they understand what is being read.

- **A read reaction** *(trigger: a creature within the model's scope misses the Duelist with an attack, or a creature ends its turn within the model's scope without having attacked the Duelist).* The Duelist gains advantage on its next counter reaction against that creature, or imposes disadvantage on that creature's next attack against the Duelist. This reaction is the Access Strength — the reason it is hard to attack the Duelist effectively even when the party is trying to. Missing teaches the Duelist; hesitating teaches the Duelist. Only striking outside the model breaks the cycle.

**Optional third reaction (choose one based on your high concept):**

- **A killing read** *(trigger: a creature within the model's scope attacks the Duelist for the third consecutive time without varying the attack profile — same weapon, same direction, same distance).* The Duelist makes one primary strike against that creature with advantage and on a hit deals additional damage equal to the creature's level or proficiency bonus. The Duelist's model compresses with exposure: the more consistently the party attacks, the more dangerous the eventual answer.
- **A lifesteal counter** *(trigger: the Duelist successfully resolves a counter reaction).* The Duelist regains HP equal to half the damage dealt by the counter. This option works best for Duelists whose identity includes vampirism, blood magic, or some other thematic source of life-from-violence. The mechanical effect is to make the Endurance pressure also a sustain loop — the party's wrong attacks heal the Duelist, compressing the time available to find the right approach.
- **A reading parry** *(trigger: a creature within the model's scope targets the Duelist with an ability that requires a saving throw).* The Duelist makes the saving throw with advantage. If it succeeds, the ability has no effect on the Duelist. This option extends the model's scope to magical abilities and area effects — useful for Duelists who should also be difficult targets for spellcasters, not just melee fighters.

> **On the counter reaction:** This is the Duelist's most important reaction for communicating both the Defining Trait and the Strength. It must fire visibly, frequently, and decisively. Narrate each trigger: the Duelist's blade rises before the attack lands, the riposte arrives as the strike completes, the counter is already moving when the attack is declared. The visual language should make clear that the Duelist is *anticipating*, not just reacting. Players who see this clearly will start looking for ways to attack that the Duelist *cannot* anticipate.

---

### 4. Calibrate Challenge Rating

The Duelist's CR calibration follows the standard process with one Duelist-specific adjustment: **the Duelist's effective DPR is significantly higher than its action damage suggests, because the counter reaction adds a substantial damage contribution each round.**

The Duelist's actions deal below-baseline damage. The counters add the rest. When calibrating, calculate the counter reaction's expected contribution based on how often the party is likely to attack the Duelist with attacks that fall within the model's scope. For a party of four attacking aggressively, the counter reaction will typically fire once per round during the puzzle phase. That one counter, plus the action damage, should hit the CR's DPR baseline.

**Steps:**


1. **Set AC at baseline and HP below baseline.** The Duelist's durability comes from its read and counter reactions, not from raw HP. AC should be at the middle of its CR range — the Duelist is hard to hit because it is hard to attack effectively, not because it is armored. HP should be noticeably below baseline; once the party finds the input outside the model, the Duelist's defenses collapse and the encounter should end quickly. A Duelist with high HP after the Weakness opens feels like a punishment for the party that just figured out how to fight it.

2. **Define the model's scope deliberately.** The scope must be wide enough to feel like a real wall but narrow enough to have a discoverable exterior. A useful baseline: the model should cover roughly 60–75% of the attacks the party would make against the Duelist by default. The remaining 25–40% — attacks from outside the scope — are the path to the Weakness. Players who experiment with different attack profiles will find the gap. Players who attack the same way every round will feed the counter loop indefinitely.

3. **Define the counter's magnitude carefully.** The counter reaction's damage should be substantial — meaningfully higher than a standard reaction damage budget — because it is replacing the action damage the Duelist is giving up. A useful baseline: the counter should deal damage roughly equivalent to the Duelist's primary strike, plus a small additional amount as the model's mechanical signature. The counter must hurt enough that the party feels the cost of attacking inside the scope, but not so much that single attacks risk character death — character death from a counter means the party never gets to feel the moment of finding the gap.

4. **Set attack stats at curve in accuracy, below curve in damage.** The Duelist hits reliably when it attacks, but its attacks are not where the damage comes from. The primary strike's attack bonus should be at curve; the per-hit damage should be below curve. The total expected DPR from actions alone will look low. Combined with the counter reaction's contribution, the total reaches the CR baseline.

5. **Assign ability scores from the primary strike's to-hit and the read's Save DC (if applicable).** Dexterity should be the primary ability score for most Duelists — the precision and speed of the read are dexterous qualities. Wisdom is a useful secondary, representing the perception and intuition that drive the reading. Constitution should be low or moderate — the Duelist is not built to absorb punishment, and this is part of the Weakness expression.

6. **Distribute damage across categories with Duelist priorities:**
   - **Actions:** Below-baseline DPR. The primary strike's per-hit damage is below curve, and the Duelist attacks at standard frequency. Action DPR is roughly 50–60% of CR baseline.
   - **Bonus Actions:** The stance bonus action deals no damage. The read positioning bonus action deals no damage. The Duelist's Bonus Action DPR is zero — both bonus actions are informational and positional.
   - **Reactions:** The counter reaction deals significant damage and fires once per round on average against an aggressive party. The read reaction deals no damage but feeds the counter. The optional third reaction varies by selection — killing read adds substantial damage when it fires, lifesteal converts damage into sustain, reading parry extends the defensive scope. The Duelist's reaction DPR is the highest of any Tyrant archetype, and is the difference between the action DPR and the CR baseline.

7. **Assign attack damage with the Penetration Weakness in mind.** When the party finds the input outside the model, the counter reaction stops firing — the read does not connect, so the answer does not come. The Duelist's effective DPR drops sharply, because the bulk of its damage was reactive. Combined with the below-baseline HP, this means the endgame phase resolves in 1–2 rounds. The Duelist is not exhausted, not desperate, not damaged into incompetence. It is simply *reading the wrong fight*, and the party is finally attacking the one that exists.

---

### The Duelist's Tell

Every Duelist stat block must have one. Write it in plain language before finalizing the stat block, then make sure it is visible in play within two rounds:

> *"This Duelist's read covers [specific observable scope — angle, attacker count, attack type, range, technique], and its counters fail when [specific observable input outside that scope]. Players who [find the input outside the model and commit to it] will find their attacks landing without answer — and the Duelist's defenses collapsing decisively."*

If you cannot complete that sentence, the Endurance Defining Trait has no readable solution and the Access Strength is just a generic high-AC effect — the Duelist is just a reaction-spam boss with no escape. Go back to the model tools and define the scope of the read in terms players can observe and reason about. The model must have an exterior. Players need to be able to find it, even if they have to test the edges through attempts that get countered.
