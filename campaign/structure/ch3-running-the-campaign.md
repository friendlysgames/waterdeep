# Chapter 3: Running the Campaign

---

## Chapter Purpose

The DM's structural manual. Read before the campaign begins — then kept close. Where Chapter 2 is reference material about the world, Chapter 3 is reference material about how to run this specific version of it. It answers the questions that won't be in any individual arc: how do the factions move between sessions? What does the Three Clue Rule look like in practice for a city-wide investigation? What is the festival calendar actually for?

The Adventure Summary gives the full campaign shape in one view. The Structural Rules section is consulted whenever a recurring mechanic triggers. The Grand Game in Play section is the DM's living faction tracker — read before each session, updated after. The Design Notes explain why the major structural choices were made.

---

## 1. Core Concept

Dragon Heist is a city, and cities don't pause while the players think. The four villain factions move independently, react to what the PCs do, compete with each other, and pursue their own goals on their own schedules. The DM's job is to manage a system of competing interests and let the PCs find their place inside it.

The mechanical infrastructure here lets the DM manage that system without burning out between sessions.

### Structural Rules

**Purpose:** Establishes the recurring mechanical systems that operate throughout the campaign — invoked constantly, not explained in individual arcs.

**Content:**

*The Two Zhentarims*

The campaign contains two distinct Zhentarim factions that must never be conflated:

- **The Doom Raiders** (Davil Starsong's cell): a player-joinable faction; presents a public face of legitimate business and moderate criminal enterprise; will not cooperate with Manshoon and does not acknowledge his cell as legitimate Zhentarim; found in Trollskull Alley and across the city as apparent professionals
- **Manshoon's Splinter Cell**: the villain faction; operates from Kolat Towers; the "Zhentarim" that conducts assassinations and uses mind-controlled servants; Manshoon views the Doom Raiders as deserters who stole his name

When PCs encounter "the Zhentarim" in any given scene, the DM must know which cell is present. These factions will not cooperate. They will actively undermine each other if their operations overlap. The distinction should become apparent to attentive PCs during Arc B.

*Faction Response Teams*

Each villain faction maintains mobile squads — small groups (2–4 members) deployed reactively when the PCs hit faction assets. Cover:
- Composition: what a typical response team looks like for each faction (personality, tactics, escalation threshold)
- Deployment triggers: what activates a response team (PC incursion into a faction asset, faction surveillance flagging PC activity, orders from a faction lieutenant)
- Clue-seeding props: every response team carries intelligence items (documents, coded messages, maps) that reward PCs who search or interrogate rather than simply defeat; each item connects to a different investigation thread
- Escalation: hitting one faction's assets does not automatically alert others; factions do not share intelligence freely; but PC actions that become visible citywide (Watch involvement, public confrontation) may reach multiple factions

Faction-specific team personalities:
- **Xanathar Guild**: brute force and gazer scouts; paranoid overreaction; will call for reinforcements faster than necessary
- **Manshoon's Zhentarim**: professional mercenary tactics; more willing to negotiate mid-fight; always more members waiting nearby than visible
- **Doom Raiders** (if turned hostile): streetwise ambushes; leverage over PC contacts; prefer cutting deals to prolonged conflict
- **Bregan D'aerthe**: disguises and misdirection; non-lethal containment preferred; will not commit to direct conflict in the open city
- **Cassalanters**: hired muscle with devil backup on the lower temple level; social and legal leverage as a first response (threats of Watch involvement, reputation damage)

*Festival Calendar*

The Waterdeep festival calendar is a structural device, not flavor. Festivals provide social cover for heist preparation, create timing pressure, and anchor the Grand Game to specific dates. The DM should establish the campaign's starting date and track the calendar actively.

Key dates:

| Festival / Date | Date | Structural function |
|----------|------|---------------------|
| Fleetswake | Ches 21–30 | Maritime festival; Sea Maidens Faire arrives in harbor; cover for Arc H reconnaissance |
| Twin Parades | Ches 21 | Opening day of Fleetswake; nimblewright sighting in the procession (Arc B ev-07); fireball strikes the following morning |
| The Fireball | Ches 22 | Campaign inciting incident; opens Arc C; the morning after the Twin Parades |
| Waukeentide | Tarsakh 1–10 | Trade festival; Cassalanter social events peak; cover for Arc G surveillance phase |
| Founders' Day | Flamerule 1 | HARD DEADLINE: Cassalanter sacrifice window closes; if PCs have not resolved the children's situation, the sacrifice occurs |

The DM should decide the campaign start date (Alturiak or early Ches is typical, leaving two months before Fleetswake) and maintain a session-by-session calendar. Players who are paying attention should be able to track the approach of Founders' Day without being told what it means until they understand the Cassalanters.

*Three Clue Rule in Practice*

Every investigation beat in this campaign follows the Three Clue Rule: at least three independent clue paths lead to every conclusion the PCs need to reach. No single failed roll, missed NPC, or skipped location should derail the investigation.

How to apply it:
- **Active clues**: require a roll, a decision, or a successful interaction to discover
- **Passive clues**: arrive without PC effort (NPCs volunteer information, something is visible on entry, an ally passes word)
- **Deduction clues**: available to PCs who combine two pieces of existing information without needing a new discovery

Every critical investigation beat should have at least one of each type. If all three paths require successful rolls, the rule is technically met but practically fragile.

Critical beats to audit (one table per beat, verification in Appendix E revelation lists):
- Finding Floon (Arc A)
- Identifying the nimblewright and its owner (Arc C)
- Connecting the fireball to the Gralhunds (Arc C)
- Locating each Eye of the Stone (Arc E)
- Finding each villain faction's lair (Arc E)
- Opening the Vault of Dragons (Arc J)

*The Fireball Victim*

The fireball that opens Arc C must kill or destroy a named NPC the players bonded with during Arc B. This is the DM's active observation task throughout Arc B — not a decision made at session zero, but a read made at the table.

How to run it:
- During Arc B, watch for which NPC the group gravitates toward. Not necessarily the one with the most page time — the one the players ask about, reference between sessions, return to.
- Identify the victim before Arc C begins. Write them into the fireball scene. Do not leave it vague.
- The scene should not be survivable-with-a-heal. The victim is dead or, in Lif's case, gone — the campaign's first irreversible consequence.

Suggested candidates (not prescriptive — the actual victim is whoever the players chose):

| Candidate | Why it works |
|-----------|-------------|
| Fala Lefaliir (herbalist) | The most likely bond for groups who frequent their shop; gentle and memorable; death feels like losing a neighborhood |
| Jalester Silvermane or Davil Starsong (faction contacts) | Makes the investigation political AND personal; ties the fireball directly to the Grand Game's cost |
| Lif the poltergeist (Trollskull Manor ghost) | Non-lethal in the conventional sense but the manor's personality disappears; works best if the group anthropomorphized Lif heavily |
| A PC's backstory NPC | Maximum personal stakes; requires session zero coordination to establish the NPC exists in Waterdeep; must be a named character with at least one scene |

One rule: the victim cannot be a PC. One firm suggestion: the victim should not be someone the DM found narratively convenient to remove. The players need to feel the loss. A DM who picked the victim for their own reasons made a different scene with a different result.

*Faction State Tracking*

Between sessions, the DM should update a simple faction state record for each villain faction. A brief checklist is enough. For each faction, track:
- Current knowledge of PC identities and capabilities
- Whether PC actions have triggered a response (response team deployed? direct leadership notified?)
- What the faction is actively pursuing this week independent of PCs
- Whether the faction's position in the Grand Game has shifted (Eye acquired or lost, ally gained or turned, key operative compromised)

Inter-faction activity continues off-screen. If the PCs spend three sessions on Cassalanter outposts, Xanathar and Manshoon have not been idle. The DM should have a rough answer for what each faction did during that time if the PCs investigate.

**Tone:** Technical and precise. Consult these during session prep; they're not table-talk. Write for quick reference, not extended reading.

### The Grand Game in Play

**Purpose:** Gives the DM a practical faction reference — what each faction knows and is doing at campaign start, how they escalate, and how they interact with each other independent of the PCs.

**Content:**

*Starting Faction Knowledge (campaign day one)*

What each villain faction knows before the PCs do anything:

- **Xanathar's Guild:** Knows Dalakhar was moving the Stone; knows the Stone is now in circulation; does not know about the Eyes or the vault's location; is reacting to the loss of expected income rather than pursuing the gold directly; Nar'l Xibrindas's intelligence has been selectively filtered by Jarlaxle
- **Manshoon's Splinter Cell:** Knows the Stone exists and what it unlocks; Urstul Floxin's team has been tracking Dalakhar; does not yet know the Stone reached the Gralhunds; Kolat Towers is established and operational
- **The Cassalanters:** Know exactly what they need and why (the vault gold pays the debt); have been planning the Founders' Day timeline for eight years; do not yet know the Stone is in play; their outpost network is focused on soul coin acquisition, not the Stone
- **Bregan D'aerthe:** Nar'l Xibrindas has fed Jarlaxle accurate intelligence about the Stone and the Eyes since before the campaign begins; Jarlaxle has the most complete picture of any faction; the Sea Maidens Faire is in port and operational; he is waiting to see which party proves most useful

*Escalation Tiers*

For each villain faction: how their posture toward the PCs shifts across five tiers.

| Tier | Faction posture | Trigger | What the DM does |
|------|-----------------|---------|------------------|
| 1 — Unaware | Faction doesn't know the PCs exist | Campaign start | Background activity only |
| 2 — Observed | Faction has registered the PCs as a variable | PCs appear at a faction-adjacent scene | Nar'l/intelligence assets begin tracking; no action taken |
| 3 — Investigated | Faction is actively learning about the PCs | PCs hit a faction asset or acquire something faction wants | Response team deployed; clue-seeding props in play |
| 4 — Engaged | Faction has made the PCs a priority problem | PCs have cost the faction significantly or hold an Eye | Lieutenant takes personal interest; job offer OR assassination attempt depending on faction |
| 5 — Confrontation | Faction is in direct conflict with PCs | PCs have broken an agreement, stolen from a principal, or blocked a core goal | Full mobilization; faction leader attention; Arc-level consequences |

Each faction reaches these tiers at different paces. Xanathar escalates faster and more irrationally. Jarlaxle almost never reaches tier 4 without choosing to. The Cassalanters prefer tier 3 indefinitely — the less visible their pursuit, the better.

*Inter-Faction Conflict (independent of PCs)*

The Grand Game does not pause for the PCs. Between sessions, the factions act on their own interests:

- Manshoon and Xanathar's Guild: active street-level conflict in the Dock Ward; Manshoon's operatives are probing Guild territory; Xanathar has issued a kill order on Manshoon (failed twice so far)
- Jarlaxle and Xanathar: managed tension; Nar'l keeps Xanathar occupied with curated intelligence; Jarlaxle's goal is a predictable beholder, not a dead one
- Jarlaxle and Manshoon: fragile non-interference; each considers the other the only genuine peer in the game; neither wants direct conflict because both would rather the other faction absorb the other factions' attention
- The Cassalanters and everyone else: largely parallel track; they are not fighting the other factions for the Stone; they intend to wait until the Stone is activated and the vault location revealed, then move on the vault directly; their outpost activity is soul-coin focused, not Stone-focused

When a PC action weakens one faction significantly, the other factions respond — not because they know what the PCs did, but because they notice the suddenly quieter territory or the absence of a known operative. The DM should play this organically: faction activity fills vacuums.

*When Factions Back Down*

Not every escalation resolves through combat or confrontation. Guidance on:
- When a faction withdraws from a conflict it is losing (Doom Raiders will almost always negotiate; Cassalanters retreat to legal/social pressure; Xanathar rarely backs down once engaged)
- What a faction wants in exchange for standing down (Doom Raiders: information; Manshoon: an asset or a demonstration of usefulness; Cassalanters: discretion; Bregan D'aerthe: the political favor they've always wanted)
- What a faction remembers (all of them; Xanathar especially; grudges held by Xanathar are permanent unless expunged through an act that satisfies the beholder's ego)

**Tone:** Operational. This is how a DM runs a living city between sessions without needing to improvise faction behavior from scratch every week.

### Player Factions

**Purpose:** Brief operational overview of the factions PCs can join, so the DM understands each faction's role and character before play begins. Full profiles, mission tables, and renown progression live in the appendices.

**Content:**

Seven factions are available to PCs. Six are standard player factions; one is unique.

*The six standard factions*

| Faction | Character | What they want from PCs |
|---------|-----------|------------------------|
| Harpers | City's shadow conscience; intelligence-first, non-lethal where possible | Information on faction activity; protection of vulnerable NPCs; exposure of corruption |
| Lords' Alliance | Political stability and Waterdeep's institutional authority | The gold returned to the city; factions contained; no public embarrassment of the Lords |
| Emerald Enclave | Nature and balance; thin presence in a city, but present | Anything connected to unnatural or fiendish corruption (Cassalanters; undead in Undermountain) |
| Order of the Gauntlet | Active suppression of evil; direct action over subtlety | Combat support; assault on known villain lairs; the Cassalanter sacrifice prevented |
| Force Grey (Gray Hands) | Vajra Safahr's personal agent corps; high-powered, high-discretion | The same things the Open Lord wants, but handled quietly and without the Lords' Alliance's politics |
| Zhentarim (Doom Raiders) | Davil Starsong's cell; criminal enterprise with a palatable face | Intelligence on Manshoon's operation; containment of Xanathar Guild activity; anything that weakens their rivals |

*The unique faction: Bregan D'aerthe*

Bregan D'aerthe is the only villain faction PCs can join. This changes Arc H's structure entirely — the Sea Maidens Faire becomes a home base, and Jarlaxle sends the party after another faction's Eye rather than being the target of a heist. The arc's inversion is the point: PCs working for Jarlaxle experience the same heist framework from the other side.

Bregan D'aerthe is available only if the nimblewright investigation (Arc C) leads to the Sea Maidens Faire and the PCs respond to Jarlaxle's approach by negotiating rather than stealing. PCs who join take on Jarlaxle's goal (Lords' Alliance membership for Luskan) as their own faction obligation, which comes due in Arc J.

*Conflicting loyalties*

PCs in different factions will have conflicting faction interests by design. The Lords' Alliance wants the gold returned; the Doom Raiders want Manshoon destroyed; Force Grey wants everything kept quiet; Bregan D'aerthe wants a political favor. This is the campaign's social texture. The DM should let faction loyalties create real pressure on individual PCs without forcing party fragmentation.

**Full faction detail:** Appendix C (faction overviews — goals, key members, territory, resources); Appendix D (running factions in play — missions by renown tier, faction hooks per arc, conflicting loyalty guidance).

**Tone:** Overview-level. Read for orientation; run the factions from the appendices.

### Design Notes: Running the Campaign

**Purpose:** The macro-level design note section — the "why" behind every major structural decision in the campaign architecture. Each note explains what was wrong with the original, what replaced it, and what the replacement accomplishes. Written for a DM who has read Dragon Heist, heard community opinions about it, and wants to understand the intent behind the remix's departures.

**Content:**

*Why Simultaneous Villains*

The original Dragon Heist uses a "season villain" structure — one villain faction per run-through. This is addressed directly and the decision explained: what the season structure accomplishes (cleaner focus, dedicated arc), what it costs (players lose the political texture of competing factions, the city feels less alive, the specific villain chosen skews the campaign's tone uncontrollably). The remix's choice to feature all four simultaneously is argued on three grounds: it makes Waterdeep feel like a genuine power struggle rather than a scripted encounter chain; it creates organic player agency when PCs can pick which faction to hit first; and it means no single villain defines the campaign's identity.

*Why the Heist Framework*

Dragon Heist is marketed as a heist but plays as an investigation followed by a dungeon. The structural decision to reorganize Act III around the five-step heist framework is explained here: Identify Score, Gather Info, Surveillance, Prep, Operation — applied consistently across all four lairs with distinct genres (dungeon infiltration, social infiltration, caper, raid). This ensures mechanical variety and rewards player investment in planning rather than punishing it.

*Why the Eyes Are Distributed*

The original has one MacGuffin (the Stone of Golorr) passed between factions. The remix distributes the three Eyes separately, requiring the PCs to penetrate multiple lairs. The design note explains this decision: a single MacGuffin creates a single bottleneck; three Eyes mean PCs must engage all four factions rather than focusing on one; the distribution also creates negotiation currency (trade an Eye for an alliance, use an Eye as bait, steal an Eye twice if necessary).

*Why Trollskull Manor*

The original gives the PCs Trollskull Manor as a reward after Arc A but provides limited mechanical reason to invest in it. This note explains the motivation engineering decision: the manor creates something the PCs care about protecting before the fireball destroys it; the neighborhood relationships developed in Arc B become the emotional cost of Arc C; the Trollskull community is the campaign's human stakes made concrete. Without this investment, the fireball is just a plot event.

*Why the Fireball Must Kill Someone*

The fireball that opens Arc C is a story beat in the original — notable but not devastating. This design note argues that the fireball must kill or destroy a named NPC the players bonded with during Arc B. The text does not name the victim — the DM identifies them. This is non-negotiable motivation engineering: personal loss converts a civic investigation into a personal crusade. The three clue paths to the nimblewright remain, but the players pursue them with urgency rather than obligation.

*Why Founders' Day Is a Hard Deadline*

Ammalia Cassalanter's deadline does not appear in the infernal contract — it is her own calculation. The design note explains why this matters: it is a morally significant detail. The Cassalanters are not working to a devil's timetable; they are working to a mother's calculation of how fast her children's souls are eroding. This makes the deadline simultaneously more flexible (it could be wrong) and more urgent (there is no appeal). The note also explains why Founders' Day was chosen over other dates: it is late enough in the campaign year to give PCs time, early enough that it creates genuine pressure before Act IV, and its civic significance makes the sacrifice narratively resonant.

*Why Jarlaxle's Entry Is Conditional*

The design note defends the conditional entry mechanic — Jarlaxle enters the Grand Game as a named player only if the nimblewright investigation reaches the Sea Maidens Faire. This is argued on player agency grounds: if Jarlaxle is always present and always relevant, he dominates the campaign's tone; his entry being conditional means groups that follow the thread get the full Bregan D'aerthe arc, while groups that don't still have three complete villain arcs. The note also covers the backup obligation: the Three Clue Rule requires at least three independent paths to the Sea Maidens Faire, and at least one must be passive (requiring no active player investigation to trigger). A DM who finds the group is unlikely to follow the nimblewright thread is expected to introduce the backup path rather than let the arc disappear.

*Why the Gold Is Not Given to the City*

Arc J produces 500,000gp. The original adventure suggests returning it to Waterdeep as the most heroic outcome. This design note argues against structuring Arc J around this expectation: the players must have genuine agency over the gold for the campaign's moral questions to have weight. The note explains all five resolution paths (keep, return, distribute, use for a specific purpose, trade to a faction) and what each one means for the political aftermath. The DM is told explicitly: do not railroad the gold away from the players. The consequences of keeping it should be dramatic, not punitive.

*Why Aurinax Is Given a Moral Complexity*

The gold dragon guardian in the vault is rewritten from a straightforward obstacle into a character with a genuine crisis. The design note explains what was wrong with the original (a guardian who exists only as an encounter) and what the rewrite accomplishes: a good dragon who has spent decades keeping a corrupt politician's secret deserves to be more interesting than his stat block. Aurinax can be reasoned with. His attitude toward the vault's purpose has shifted. How PCs approach him should determine whether he is an obstacle, a complication, or an unexpected ally.

**Tone:** Direct, engaged, occasionally personal. Design notes address the DM as a peer with opinions about Dragon Heist and give them a real argument. When a popular community fix was considered and rejected, that rejection is named and explained.

## 3. Key Decisions Before Writing

1. **The Founders' Day resolution:** The DM must decide before play begins which Cassalanter resolution paths they are prepared to support. Path 4 (the sacrifice happens) is a valid dramatic outcome and should not be foreclosed by soft DM reluctance — but it changes the campaign's emotional register for everything that follows. Decide in advance, not mid-session.

2. **Session length and pacing:** The milestone table assumes 3–4 hour sessions. Groups playing shorter sessions will need more milestones or consolidated arcs. Groups playing longer may find individual arcs resolve faster than expected. The DM should read the milestone table as a pacing guide, not a contract.

3. **The design notes audience:** The Design Notes section in the final document is written for the DM, not the players. Decide before drafting whether to write them as internal DM notes (slightly informal, allows "I") or as more formal published text (third person, no first-person voice). This choice should be consistent across all design notes sections in the campaign.

4. **Faction state tracking method:** The text recommends a between-session checklist but does not prescribe a format. The DM should decide whether to use the Appendix E faction tracker, a simple notebook system, or a digital tool. The chapter should reference whichever method the DM will actually use, not describe an elaborate system that will be abandoned after session three.
