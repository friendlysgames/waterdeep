# Design Notes: Finding Floon

## Investigation Architecture

***Investigation Restructuring.*** The source Chapter 1 presents the Dock Ward investigation as a sequential chain: Skewered Dragon → warehouse name → Renaer → sewer. A single failed Charisma check at the Skewered Dragon can stall the party indefinitely, because the adventure makes that tavern the sole source for the warehouse's location. The Three Clue Rule treatment distributes the warehouse address across four independent paths for Revelation #2: the Skewered Dragon itself (simultaneously serving Revelation #1 and #2), neighborhood canvass, prisoner questioning, and the dropped bead trail. No single failure blocks progress. Revelation #1 has three independent paths; Revelation #3 has three. The four-path Revelation #2 reflects the warehouse address being the bottleneck in the source, placing extra paths where the adventure was most fragile. Players who follow all paths simultaneously discover more about the world; players who follow one path successfully still reach the warehouse.

## Faction Architecture

***The Two Zhentarims in Finding Floon.*** Dragon Heist presents the Zhentarim as a single faction. This remix uses Finding Floon to establish the faction fracture that defines the Manshoon subplot. Yagra Stonefist (a Doom Raiders operative, Davil Starsong's people) is at the Yawning Portal bar, openly wearing the Zhentarim pendant, fighting Xanathar Guild thugs. The Candle Lane warehouse belongs to Manshoon's cell, Urstul Floxin's people, operating separately from Davil. Players who later join the Doom Raiders in Trollskull Alley and then raid Kolat Towers in Kolat Towers will have seen both Zhentarim factions from Scene 1: one as a potential ally, one as an adversary, both operating under the same symbol. Players feel the distinction before anyone explains it.

***Bregan D'Aerthe Observer.*** The drow woman at the Yawning Portal balcony table is the campaign's earliest Bregan D'Aerthe presence. She has no name in ev-01, and they're not meant to notice her unless they ask who is watching the well. She reappears in Trollskull Alley as a background presence, same table on different evenings. Players who look into her background will find she's part of Jarlaxle's Waterdeep intelligence network monitoring the Undermountain entrance. By the time they reach the Sea Maidens Faire in Sea Maidens Faire, they can look back at ev-01 and understand that Jarlaxle has had eyes on them since their first night in the city.

## Long-Game Seeds

***Cassalanter Name-Drop Through Renaer.*** Renaer's aside about the Cassalanters' property acquisitions near the Brandath tombs sits unexamined in player notes until Cassalanter Villa. He raises it as an oddity he can't explain. Players who track it will revisit the detail when the Cassalanter Villa becomes a heist target; Renaer recaps it in Cassalanter Villa for those who've moved on. The name-drop seeds without pressing. A first-level party has no use for it yet, and none is offered.

***Nihiloor as Foreshadowing.*** Q07 in the sewer hideout is the campaign's first contact with Nihiloor. Xanathar's Lair eventually identifies it as the true intelligence driving the Xanathar Guild, running three parallel projects, exercising psionic control over Xanathar, and infiltrating Waterdeep's civil institutions (see the **Xanathar's Guild** organization page). In Finding Floon, Nihiloor doesn't fight them. It's simply there — seated, watching Grum'shar work — and then it's gone, faster than a creature that large has any right to move, through a portal they can't follow. This restraint is intentional; the encounter registers the creature's existence and its agency over the Guild cell before any context is available to understand what it is. Nihiloor's full scope surfaces across Trollskull Alley through Xanathar.s Lair. By the time they reach Undermountain Level 17 in the Dungeon of the Mad Mage, its presence in Finding Floon should read as a thread that began in the sewers under Trollskull Alley.

## NPC Design

***Renaer's Introduction.*** In the source, finding Renaer in the warehouse is a pleasant surprise in an otherwise thin scene (a friendly NPC who points the party downward). This treatment makes Renaer's introduction structural: the warehouse is the quest's pivot. The party arrives expecting Floon and finds someone who matters more to the campaign's back story. Renaer's knowledge state is asymmetric by design. He knows what Manshoon's cell wanted and why the locket mattered to his father, without knowing what the locket contained. His ignorance is genuine. This positions him as a trustworthy ally (he doesn't conceal what he knows) while preserving the campaign's central mystery (the nature of the Eyes, the Vault's location). The gap between what they'll eventually learn and what Renaer knows gives the warehouse scene a retrospective weight absent from the source text.

***Durnan's Secret.*** Durnan's Masked Lord status and Red Sashes leadership (see the **Durnan** page in Notable Figures) don't surface in Finding Floon and don't appear in any player-facing text. His ev-01 portrayal (minimal words, the troll handled in seconds, "You fought well" as the full debrief) reads as "formidable retired adventurer" to players and as "Masked Lord making deliberate choices about how much power to display" to the DM. The gap between those two readings is Durnan's dramatic potential. If they gain enough standing to learn his secrets in Trollskull Alley or beyond, ev-01 retroactively becomes a performance — a man who could have stopped that brawl before the first punch deciding it was more useful to let it play out.

## Structural Mechanics

***Stealth-or-Force Preview.*** The warehouse presents the quest's first real structural choice: enter quietly and catch the kenku off-guard, or force entry and fight a prepared defense. Neither approach is obviously correct. This binary (infiltration versus direct assault) is the same decision they'll face at every heist target across the heist quests. Finding Floon is the tutorial, with first-level stakes and kenku rather than guild lieutenants. Each subsequent heist quest's design notes should acknowledge this parallel explicitly, so the DM can frame escalating heist complexity as the campaign's through-line skill development rather than a separate genre convention dropped into the story.
